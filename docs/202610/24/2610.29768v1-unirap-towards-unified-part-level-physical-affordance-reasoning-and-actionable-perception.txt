## Abstract 

Vision-language perception has achieved impressive progress in aligning natural language with visual observations, yet grounding high-level semantics into part-level physical interaction remains challenging. To address this gap, we propose UniRAP, a unified model for inferring part-level physical affordances and mapping language instructions to actionable geometric representations. UniRAP formu-lates this problem as a conditional multimodal generation task, integrating visual inputs, textual instructions, and optional prompts into a shared spatiotemporal representation space through a unified interface token mechanism. To predict executable contact geometry, we introduce a Unified Affordance Decoder (UAD), which jointly performs object detection, part-level affordance segmentation, and 4-DoF interaction pose estimation by leveraging intermediate segmentation fea-tures. In addition, we propose a curriculum-based transfer training strategy that progressively adapts the model from general visual parsing to interaction-aware per-ception, improving data efficiency under limited textual annotations. Experiments show that UniRAP achieves state-of-the-art performance on referring expression segmentation, affordance grounding, and interaction pose estimation, while main-taining strong spatiotemporal consistency in dynamic video scenarios. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of UniRAP as a unified perception framework for language-guided physical manipulation. 

## 1 Introduction 

Vision-Language Perception (VLP) aims to establish semantic correspondences between natural language and visual observations, enabling models to interpret and localize relevant elements in a scene conditioned on linguistic instructions [1, 2]. With the advancement of Vision-Language Models (VLMs), these systems have demonstrated strong capabilities in cross-modal alignment and semantic understanding in open-world settings, achieving notable success in tasks such as visual question answering [3–7]. Building upon these foundations, subsequent works have extended such models to incorporate spatial perception, enabling them to generate coarse visual grounding from text, such as bounding boxes [8–13] or segmentation masks [14–19]. However, these capabilities remain largely confined to semantic understanding and alignment, lacking the fine-grained, actionable modeling required for physical interaction.

In embodied intelligence scenarios, this limitation becomes particularly critical: systems must not only understand what and where , but also further reason about how to contact [20, 21]. This requires models to possess part-level actionable perception , i.e., the ability to model interaction-relevant functional properties (affordances) [22, 23] and their corresponding operability, grounded in pixel-level spatial alignment and part-level structural understanding [24–27]. Without such capability, vision-language models struggle to translate language instructions into executable action representations, thereby limiting their applicability in real-world physical environments [28–30]. Despite notable progress, existing approaches still exhibit systematic limitations in advancing toward actionable perception. On one hand, cross-modal methods [31–33, 27, 34] based on CLIP/DINO-style visual representation alignment rely on static embedding spaces, which are inherently limited in modeling complex linguistic structures and compositional relationships. On the other hand, although VLM-based approaches demonstrate strong multi-step reasoning capabilities, their modeling granu-larity remains predominantly object-centric. Even when augmented with intermediate representations such as keypoints [35–41], detection [42, 13, 12, 43], or segmentation [44–46, 14, 15], these methods still fail to explicitly capture part-level functional affordances and contact regions, all of which are essential for reasoning about real-world interaction feasibility. Furthermore, RealVLG-R1 [47] attempts to predict 4-DoF grasp poses via structured language generation. However, this approach relies on an autoregressive generation mechanism, which is prone to limited precision and instability, making it inadequate for pixel-level actionable tasks. To address the aforementioned challenges, we propose UniRAP , a Uni fied vision-language model for Reasoning-Aware Actionable Perception. As shown in Fig. 1, UniRAP supports both textual and visual prompts (e.g., points and bounding boxes) and is capable of producing multi-granularity 2affordance perception outputs over continuous data modalities, including images and videos. These outputs include object detection bounding boxes, part-level affordance segmentation masks, and interaction-oriented structured representations. Notably, UniRAP adopts a 4-DoF grasp pose as an explicit representation for actionable perception, which establishes a principled correspondence between high-level linguistic semantics and physical interaction. To mitigate data imbalance and cross-task interference arising from heterogeneous perceptual granu-larity across tasks, a unified prompting and decoding paradigm is designed. Specifically, a structured 

<REF> token is introduced to integrate explicit visual prompts and implicit textual references into a shared <SEG> interface, thereby enabling consistent modeling of diverse localization signals. Further-more, a Unified Affordance Decoder (UAD) is proposed to facilitate cross-task representation sharing through the reuse of intermediate features, while preserving general segmentation capability to the greatest extent possible. Finally, a curriculum-based transfer training strategy is adopted, allowing the model to progressively acquire stable and consistent actionable perception capabilities without compromising its original reasoning performance. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows: 1. We propose UniRAP, a unified model that maps multimodal prompts to fine-grained physical contact geometry. It produces consistent multi-granularity affordance representations across both image and video domains. 2. A unified prompting and decoding pipeline is designed, together with a transfer-based training strategy, to alleviate task imbalance in cross-task learning and enable synergistic optimization across heterogeneous perception tasks. 3. Extensive experimental results demonstrate that UniRAP achieves a favorable trade-off be-tween accuracy and stability across a range of tasks, including general segmentation, affordance perception, and part-level interaction grounding. 

## 2 Related Work 

Non-LLM Vision-Language Perception. Inspired by vision–language pretraining models such as CLIP [48, 49] and DINO [50, 51], early VLP research primarily addresses coarse-grained tasks, including open-vocabulary object detection [52–64] and semantic segmentation [65, 26, 66–71]. These methods rely on region–text alignment to associate visual and linguistic representations, enabling generalization beyond fixed category spaces. Subsequent studies extend VLP to affordance-aware perception, shifting from object recognition to modeling functional properties, typically formulated as part-level segmentation [22, 72–79] or region-level localization [80–83]. More recently, this paradigm further expands to actionable perception, such as contact point prediction [84–87] and 4-DoF grasp pose estimation [31, 32, 88–90], aiming to bridge the gap between visual perception and physical manipulation. However, due to limited textual understanding in pretrained models, these methods remain restricted to simple category-level language and struggle with compositional and reasoning-intensive instructions, which limits their applicability in open-world embodied scenarios. 

LLM-based Vision-Language Perception. In recent years, large-scale VLMs have demonstrated strong semantic understanding capabilities in tasks such as visual question answering [3, 5, 7, 91, 8, 4, 92]. However, due to their reliance on global or region-level representations, they remain limited in pixel-level visual perception. To address this limitation, some approaches [93, 94, 12, 95, 13, 8] enforce the generation of structured language through SFT [96, 97] or RLVR [98, 99], enabling coarse-grained object localization. For dense segmentation tasks, VisionLLM [100] adopts a text-based polygon output paradigm, which is highly dependent on large-scale training data and still suffers from limited accuracy. To overcome these issues, LISA [14] introduces the “Embedding as Mask” paradigm, which extracts implicit features from the language model via a special token and injects them into SAM [24], enabling stable pixel-level predictions. Building on this line, subsequent studies further advance decoder design [101–104], scale up training data [105], and extend capabilities to video settings [106–108, 16]. UniPixel [15] further proposes the PixelQA task, unifying segmentation and region-referred visual question answering. In the context of affordance perception, existing methods largely remain limited to embodied question answering [109, 43, 110, 40] or keypoint prediction [36, 111, 112, 35, 37–39, 41]. To date, RAGNet [46] is the only work that extends LISA [14] to affordance-level segmentation. However, it still lacks interaction-centric modeling and the capability to process video data. ## 3 UniRAP 

Overview. To enable a unified model for vision–language spatiotemporal actionable perception, UniRAP is built upon Qwen2.5-VL [8] and SAM2 [113]. As illustrated in Fig. 2, given a visual input (image or video) xv , a textual prompt xt, and optional visual reference prompts xr (e.g., points or bounding boxes), UniRAP first leverages a multimodal large model F to generate textual responses containing <SEG> tokens. When the textual prompt xt includes a <REF> token, the visual prompts are encoded into discrete embeddings via a visual referring encoder and injected into the textual sequence, which is then jointly processed by the multimodal model F. These two forms of prompting are unified within a shared representation space centered on <SEG> tokens, facilitating transferability and generalization to tasks without explicit language referring cues. In the visual branch, the input 

xv is encoded by a visual encoder Ev , and the resulting features are fused with historical frame representations stored in a memory bank via a memory attention mechanism, enhancing temporal consistency and object persistence modeling. The fused features are then aligned with implicit prompt embeddings projected from <SEG> tokens through a MLP projector γ, and jointly fed into a unified affordance decoder D. The model yields multi-task predictions, including affordance segmentation masks, region detection results, and interaction poses (4-DoF grasp poses), thereby enabling fine-grained actionable perception in complex spatiotemporal scenarios. 

3.1 Unified Vision-Language Spatiotemporal Modeling 

To address vision–language spatiotemporal affordance perception under low-data conditions, UniRAP extends the “Embedding as Mask” paradigm [14, 101, 106, 15] by reformulating the problem as a conditional multimodal generation framework based on interface tokens. Specifically, the model takes as input visual observations (images or videos) xv , textual prompts xt, and optionally visual reference prompts xr (e.g., points or bounding boxes). Through a unified interface token mechanism, it generates reasoning-aware textual outputs, thereby enabling spatial alignment and joint modeling of textual and visual referents. 3.2 Unified Affordance Decoder 

Given that vision–language segmentation data are relatively abundant, whereas annotations for fine-grained interaction perception (e.g., 4-DoF grasp poses) are scarce, we propose a Unified Affordance Decoder (UAD). The goal is to effectively transfer intermediate representations (visual features and implicit prompt embeddings) learned from large-scale coarse-grained perception data to fine-grained tasks, thereby improving affordance perception performance. Specifically, as illustrated in Fig. 3, the UAD is built upon the Mask Decoder of SAM [113, 24]. During the decoding stage, a Two-way Transformer [63] takes as input the memory-enhanced visual features fmem , positional encodings p, and task tokens augmented with object-localization prompt embeddings hseg , including the object pointer token, IoU token, mask tokens tm, bbox token tb,and grasp token tg . Through joint modeling via bidirectional cross-attention and self-attention, the model enables coordinated updates between visual features and token representations, resulting in context-enhanced representations. To enable unified decoding across different granularities, UAD adopts a token-based feature modula-tion mechanism for prediction. In the Mask Head, the updated visual features are first upsampled via UpscaleNet to obtain a high-resolution representation fup . The mask tokens tm, projected through a HyperNet, are then used to perform element-wise modulation of fup , producing four candidate mask logits. A stability-based selection strategy is further applied to iteratively refine these candidates, resulting in the final optimal segmentation mask ˆM.For the object detection task, the Bbox Head adopts a DETR-style residual regression modeling strategy [63]. An initial bounding box b0 is first derived from the optimal segmentation mask ˆM

via geometric construction. The bbox token tb then modulates the upsampled features fup via FiLM [115], followed by mask-guided weighted pooling under ˆM. The resulting feature is fed into a ResidualMLP to predict a bounding box offset for refinement. This process can be formulated as:

Training Objective. The training objective of UniRAP is formulated as a weighted linear combination of a language modeling loss and multiple task-specific losses. The mask decoding component includes a Focal Loss and a Dice Loss for mask prediction, a MAE loss for IoU estimation, and a cross-entropy loss for objectness classification. The object detection branch employs an L1 regression loss and a GIoU loss to supervise bounding box prediction, while the grasp pose estimation branch is optimized using a Huber Loss and a BCE Loss. Furthermore, to enforce spatial consistency across multi-granularity perception, additional constraints are introduced, including a mask-in-bbox loss to ensure containment between masks and bounding boxes, and a consistency loss that aligns grasp center points with the corresponding mask regions. Details are provided in Appendix B.3.

4.2 Referring and Reasoning Affordance Segmentation Performance 

To evaluate UniRAP’s capability in affordance segmentation, we followed the evaluation protocol of RAGNet [46] and conducted systematic experiments on the annotated HANDAL [23], GraspNet [133], and 3DOI [134] datasets. Gray indicates zero-shot benchmarks. As shown in Tab. 3 and Tab. 4, UniRAP achieves strong performance on both explicit text-guided and reasoning-based affordance segmentation tasks. In referring affordance segmentation, UniRAP significantly outperforms RAGNet, which is also designed for affordance modeling, and demonstrates notably better generalization in unseen scenarios. Benefiting from its transfer-learning-oriented design, this advantage becomes even more pronounced in reasoning-based affordance segmentation. On the zero-shot 3DOI dataset, UniRAP improves gIoU and cIoU by 17.6 and 15.1, respectively, indicating stronger robustness and adaptability in complex semantic reasoning and spatial localization. 

4.3 Actionable Perception Performance 

We evaluated the actionable perception performance of UniRAP on the RealVLG-Benchmark [47], focusing on its capability in both coarse-grained segmentation and fine-grained interaction grounding. Given a natural language description of a target object, the benchmark requires the model to reason about its bounding box, segmentation mask, and 4-DoF grasp pose. Details of the evaluation metrics are provided in Appendix B.4. As illustrated in Tab. 5, for coarse-grained object detection, RealVLG-R1 [47] retains a certain advantage, benefiting from the large-scale detection pretraining of the foundation model Qwen2.5-VL [8]. However, for segmentation tasks, existing baselines are unable to perform end-to-end prediction and instead rely on a two-stage “bbox + SAM2” pipeline, which introduces error accumulation and leads to slightly inferior performance compared to UniRAP, particularly in zero-shot Novel scenarios. For fine-grained 4-DoF grasp pose estimation, text-based generation baselines struggle to capture pixel-level localization, whereas UniRAP’s end-to-end formulation demonstrates clear advantages, enabling both stable grasp prediction and stronger generalization. In terms of grasp accuracy (gAcc), especially in novel settings, UniRAP achieves up to a threefold improvement over RealVLG-R1. 

4.4 Ablation Study 

We conducted ablation studies on selected key components of UniRAP, as summarized in Tab. 6. Affordance pre-training substantially improved affordance understanding and reasoning segmenta-tion, increasing the Referring and Reasoning gIoU on 3DOI by 9.9 and 15.4 points, respectively. Visual referring pre-training further strengthened the association between language instructions and actionable interaction poses, improving grasp accuracy from 26.4 to 63.3 when combined with our 9proposed Grasp Head. Actionable affordance fine-tuning jointly optimized segmentation, localization, and grasp prediction, improving grasp accuracy from 19.6 to 63.3 while maintaining competitive segmentation performance. Regarding prediction heads, the Bbox Head provided additional spatial constraints for the SAM2 decoder, suppressing background interference and improving ReasonSeg, ReVOS, and 3DOI performance by 1.1, 1.5, and up to 1.9 points, respectively. Finally, replacing our regression-based Grasp Head with a heatmap-based alternative reduced grasp accuracy from 63.3 to 13.4, demonstrating the effectiveness of direct grasp prediction from Transformer representations.

## 5 Conclusion 

We proposed UniRAP, a unified vision-language model that bridges high-level vision-language reasoning with fine-grained physical contact perception. By introducing a token-based interface between the VLM backbone and a dense decoder, UniRAP enables end-to-end mapping from language instructions to interaction-centric geometric representations, supporting affordance segmentation, target region detection, and 4-DoF interaction pose estimation. A curriculum-based transfer learning strategy further improves generalization across heterogeneous tasks. Extensive experiments show consistent gains in both perception and interaction alignment, which establishes a more robust foundation for language-guided robotic systems in dynamic environments. ## A UniRAP Model 

A.1 Architecture 

Within the unified vision–language segmentation paradigm, recent approaches [15, 108, 46, 14] commonly adopt an embedding-as-mask design. In this framework, multimodal large language models generate specific tokens (e.g., <SEG> [14], <AFF> [46] , and TRK [106]) during text decoding, and the corresponding high-level semantic representations are treated as prompt embeddings to guide segmentation models, such as SAM [24], in producing target regions. This paradigm has been systematically formalized in LISA [14] and its subsequent extensions, and has been widely reused across diverse tasks. Although these methods differ in implementation details and input modalities, for instance, UniPixel [15] supports both image and video inputs and introduces finer-grained supervision via the PixelQA task, they largely share an implicit assumption: visual understanding can be modeled as a direct mapping from semantic representations to pixel space, i.e., a semantic-to-dense prediction process. Under this assumption, variations across methods primarily manifest in task formulation and supervision granularity rather than in the underlying capability paradigm. However, this assumption becomes inadequate in scenarios involving physical interaction. Taking contact point prediction as an example, the objective extends beyond identifying “what the object is” (semantic level) and “where the object is” (spatial level); it further requires reasoning about “which local region of the object should be contacted” and “whether such contact is geometrically and functionally feasible and stable”. This problem inherently introduces additional dimensions of reasoning, including local geometric constraints, potential interaction affordances, and task-relevant functional semantics, thereby exceeding the expressive capacity of conventional segmentation formulations. Motivated by this observation, UniRAP extends the applicability of the unified paradigm. Rather than treating the <SEG> token solely as a semantic anchor for region localization, UniRAP leverages its high-level representation to encode potential physical interaction intent, and, through coupling with a segmentation model, enables a cross-level mapping from semantic understanding to contact reasoning. In contrast to existing methods that primarily focus on pixel-wise correspondences, UniRAP addresses a more structured prediction problem: localizing contact regions in continuous space that satisfy both physical and functional constraints. From a capability perspective, UniRAP therefore does not merely apply existing architectures to a new task, but rather generalizes the paradigm from perception-driven dense prediction to interaction-oriented reasoning under physical constraints. This extension suggests that language-guided segmentation frameworks are not only applicable to conventional visual understanding tasks, but also hold potential for advancing toward higher-level embodied intelligence. 20 A.2 Visual Referring Encoder 

For UniRAP, both point and box prompts are uniformly represented as spatiotemporal coordinates 

(x, y, t ), where bounding boxes are discretized into representative points such as corners or uniformly sampled boundary locations. We adopt a Fourier mapping [114] with random frequency bases to embed low-dimensional coordinates into a high-dimensional representation space. Specifically, we define the positional encoding as: 

γ(c) = [sin( Bc ), cos( Bc )] , c ∈ R3 (8) where c = ( x, y, t )⊤, and B ∈ RL×3 is a random Gaussian matrix whose entries are sampled as: 

Bij ∼ N (0 , σ 2) (9) This random Fourier feature embedding allows the model to capture high-frequency spatial and tem-poral variations in a linearly separable representation space, which is essential for precise localization and interaction-sensitive reasoning. Next, the encoded prompt embeddings are transformed into discrete tokens via a learnable linear projection and further interact within LLM, enabling the joint modeling of language-driven semantics and geometry-aware visual prompts. While this design is methodologically inspired by the SAM [24], it differs fundamentally in the pathway of information interaction: the output of the Visual Referring Encoder is no longer fused directly with visual encoder features. Instead, it engages in interaction with the LLM, allowing prompt features to be explicitly aggregated into the semantic representation of the <SEG> token, thereby achieving cross-modal alignment within a unified semantic space. Furthermore, this mechanism is distinct from approaches such as SA2VA [108] and UniPixel [15]. These methods typically convert structured prompts, such as points and bounding boxes, into mask representations for region-based visual question answering tasks. In contrast, our proposed approach adopts a token-level interaction paradigm, mapping tasks without explicit textual annotations into a text-guided <SEG> token representation space. This unification reduces distributional discrepancies across tasks at the representation level and mitigates the complexity of cross-task transfer learning. 

## B Experiments 

B.1 Implementation Details 

UniRAP is built upon Qwen2.5-VL (3B) [8] and SAM 2.1 [113], with the hidden dimensionality of the visual prompt encoder set to 256. Within the affordance decoder, all ground-truth annotations are uniformly resampled to a fixed resolution of 768 × 768 via center padding. This design differs from the direct resizing strategy adopted in SAM2. The motivation is that tasks requiring coordinate inference based on object geometry, such as bounding box estimation and grasp pose prediction, are sensitive to geometric distortions, which can be significantly exacerbated by naive resizing and consequently degrade prediction accuracy. For bounding box representation, we adopt the parameterization (cx, c y , w, h ). After center padding, all elements are normalized with respect to the scale of 768. For 4-DoF grasp pose prediction, the representation (cx, c y , cos 2 θ, sin 2 θ, w ) is employed, where (cx, c y ) denotes the grasp center and θ

represents the counterclockwise angle relative to the x-axis. To address the periodicity of angular variables, the angle is encoded as (cos 2 θ, sin 2 θ). The parameter w corresponds to the width of the grasp rectangle (see Fig. 3 for illustration). The number of candidate grasp poses Ng is 32. During training, LoRA is applied to fine-tune Qwen2.5-VL, with the rank and scaling factor set to 128 and 256, respectively. For video inputs, 8 frames are randomly sampled per instance. All experiments are conducted on 8 A800 (80GB) GPUs, and the model is trained for a single epoch.

## C Transferring Actionable Perception to Robotic Manipulation 

C.1 Motivation 

Recent Vision–Language–Action (VLA) systems have achieved substantial progress in robotic manipulation tasks. However, these approaches still exhibit notable limitations in fine-grained visual localization required for precise interaction, as well as in their ability to generalize across complex scenes, object variations, and diverse language instructions [29, 30]. In particular, establishing pixel-level correspondences between natural language commands and actionable regions (e.g., grasp points or interaction surfaces) remains challenging. Existing methods often demonstrate instability and insufficient reliability, especially in cluttered or contact-dense environments. Although perception–action decoupling strategies may not achieve the theoretical upper bound performance of end-to-end approaches, they offer a key advantage in leveraging the generalization capability of perception models. This enables more robust transfer across variations in environments, embodiments, camera configurations, and linguistic instructions. Drawing inspiration from the development trajectory of autonomous driving systems such as Tesla FSD, we argue that a more feasible short-term pathway is to first deploy decoupled solutions in relatively simple scenarios, progressively accumulate embodiment-specific data, and subsequently transition toward end-to-end training in a staged manner. Accordingly, this work focuses on modeling executable vision–language localization as an intermedi-ate representation for robotic manipulation. Specifically, rather than directly learning an end-to-end control policy, we generate reliable pixel-level or pose-level interaction cues and integrate them with classical geometric planning modules. This hybrid formulation enhances both the robustness and interpretability of robotic execution in complex environments. 

C.2 UniRAP for Reasoning-Aware Robotic Grasping 

UniRAP can be integrated into robotic grasping pipelines from two complementary perspectives. First, its coarse-grained perceptual outputs, such as bounding boxes or segmentation masks, can be directly combined with geometry-based grasp synthesis methods (e.g., GraspNet [133]). In this setting, UniRAP serves as a semantic front-end that filters task-relevant regions, while downstream geometric planners operate within these regions to generate feasible grasp candidates. This hybrid design preserves the robustness and efficiency of classical pipelines while introducing task-level semantic awareness. Second, UniRAP produces interaction-aware predictions in the form of 4-DoF grasp poses, which encode both spatial location and orientation constraints aligned with the inferred contact intent. These poses can be directly mapped into the robot coordinate system via standard hand–eye calibration procedures, enabling execution without requiring additional policy learning. ## D Discussions 

D.1 Limitations & Future Work 

Due to computational constraints, we were unable to further scale the training data or increase the model size, which in turn limited potential performance gains. Although incorporating pixel-level visual question answering tasks, such as PixelQA [15], may offer additional benefits, these were not included within the scope of this study. Given that this work focuses on perception models for embodied scenarios, we argue that it is important to investigate the capability boundaries of smaller models, with an emphasis on achieving real-time actionable perception. Such an approach facilitates deployment on edge devices and reduces the overall computational demands of real-world systems. 

D.2 Potential Societal Impacts 

This work presents a pixel-level vision–language perception framework tailored for embodied sce-narios, enabling a unified formulation that spans coarse-grained object detection and segmentation, functional segmentation, and physical interaction pose estimation. The proposed approach is particu-larly relevant to application domains requiring frequent and adaptive interaction between agents and their environments, such as robotic home assistance, security, and healthcare. These settings typically demand fine-grained understanding of images and videos, as well as the capability to reason about physical interactions. Moreover, in other embodied applications involving multimodal intelligent agents, the proposed framework can serve as a more expressive and practical alternative. To the best of our knowledge, this work does not raise any specific potential negative societal impacts that require explicit disclosure.
