Video Question Grounding (VideoQG) requires both accurate question an-
swering and precise temporal localization. Existing supervised methods rely on
costly temporal annotations, while existing zero-shot approaches are primarily
designed for Video Question Answering (VQA) and are not optimized for tem-
poral grounding. We propose DiffGround, an action-driven zero-shot framework
built upon frozen Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs). Our framework
first predicts an answer-relevant action cue that transforms the input question
into an explicit semantic target for grounding. Action Cue converts a reasoning-
oriented question into an action-centric retrieval query. Guided by the action
cue, a Vision-Language Model (VLM) efficiently selects informative keyframes
for reasoning. Finally, we introduce Semantic Diff Refinement, a training-free
temporal refinement method that exploits semantic differences between neigh-
boring SigLIP2 embeddings to recover complete action segments from sparse
predictions. Extensive experiments on NExT-GQA [16] and STAR [15] show that
DiffGround consistently outperforms existing zero-shot and supervised methods,
establishing a new state of the art for Video Question Grounding. We plan to
publicly release the source code at https://github.com/steven413d/DiffGround.

Introduction
Video Question Grounding (VideoQG) is an emerging multimodal task that
requires models to not only answer questions about video content but also accu-
rately localize the temporal segments supporting their answers. Compared with
conventional Video Question Answering (VQA), VideoQG requires both seman-
tic reasoning and fine-grained temporal localization, making it a fundamental
challenge for video understanding with applications in video retrieval, human-
computer interaction, and embodied AI.
Existing approaches can be broadly categorized into supervised and zero-shot
methods, both of which suffer from important limitations. Supervised methods
rely on expensive temporal annotations and often exhibit a trade-off between
VQA accuracy and grounding precision. More recently, Multimodal Large Lan-
guage Models (MLLMs) have enabled zero-shot VideoQG without additional
training. However, existing methods are primarily designed for VQA rather than
temporal grounding, limiting their ability to accurately localize actions.
We identify two fundamental challenges underlying existing zero-shot ap-
proaches. First, they directly use the input question as the retrieval query for

between neighboring Vision-Language Model (VLM) embeddings, producing
reusable action segments for each video. Given a question, a frozen MLLM per-

description that serves as an explicit retrieval target. Guided by both the ques-

keyframes. Finally, Semantic Diff Refinement expands sparse grounding predic-

Our framework is built upon three key innovations. First, we introduce an
Action Cue Prediction module that converts reasoning-oriented questions into
concise action-centric representations, substantially improving frame retrieval
quality with only a single MLLM inference. Second, we propose a VLM-guided
keyframe selection strategy that jointly matches the question and predicted ac-
tion cue, enabling efficient action-aware frame retrieval. Third, we introduce
Semantic Diff Refinement, a training-free temporal refinement method that ex-
ploits semantic differences between adjacent SigLIP2 embeddings to recover com-
plete action segments. Compared with conventional motion-based segmentation
methods, Semantic Diff directly captures semantic transitions, operates in the
semantic embedding space rather than relying on low-level motion cues, and re-
quires minimal hyperparameter tuning. Since visual embeddings are extracted
once and reused across multiple questions associated with the same video, the
preprocessing is performed entirely offline, significantly reducing online inference
cost.
Extensive experiments on NExT-GQA [16] and STAR [15] demonstrate that
DiffGround consistently outperforms existing zero-shot and supervised approaches,
establishing a new state of the art in both Video Question Grounding and Video
Question Answering.

Related Works
2.1
Caption and Agent based Methods
Recent zero-shot frameworks leverage textual intermediate representations to
facilitate long-video understanding. LLoVi [21] and MoReVQA [7] first gener-
ate dense video captions and index them into textual memories for retrieval-
based reasoning. Meanwhile, agent-based frameworks, such as VideoAgent [13]
and BOLT [6], formulate video understanding as an iterative planning process,
where an MLLM repeatedly queries localized video segments through multiple
reasoning steps.
These approaches demonstrate strong zero-shot reasoning capability by ex-
ploiting the language understanding ability of MLLMs. However, they generally
require numerous MLLM calls during either offline indexing or online planning,
leading to considerable computational overhead. Moreover, because temporal
reasoning is performed primarily through textual representations, the resulting
temporal localization is constrained by the granularity of generated captions,
making precise boundary localization challenging. In contrast, our approach di-
rectly operates on visual representations while requiring only a single MLLM
inference for each query.

2.2
Training-based Methods
Training-based Video Question Grounding methods can be broadly categorized
into weakly supervised and temporally supervised approaches.
Weakly supervised methods, such as CRA [2] and TOGA [3], are trained
solely with video-level question-answer pairs. While avoiding dense temporal
annotations, they typically rely on coarse visual-textual correlations and often
struggle to precisely localize action boundaries.
Temporally supervised approaches explicitly exploit temporal annotations to
improve grounding performance. Representative methods include ActPrompt [14],
which introduces action-centric prompts to guide temporal attention, and fully
supervised frameworks such as Grounded-VideoLLM [12], VideoStreaming [8],
and SeViLA [19], which fine-tune large video-language models using dense tem-
poral annotations.
Unlike these methods, DiffGround is a training-free framework that requires
no parameter updates or temporal annotations. Instead, it performs action-aware
grounding through Action Cue Prediction and Semantic Diff Refinement, en-
abling strong generalization to reasoning-oriented VideoQG tasks.
2.3
Frame Sampling-based Methods
To reduce the computational cost of long-video understanding, recent zero-shot
methods perform sparse frame sampling before MLLM inference. Representa-
tive approaches include TStar [18], Adaptive Key Frame Sampling [10], and
Q-Frame [22], which select informative frames based on spatial saliency, cross-
attention, or vision-language similarity. By avoiding dense video rendering and
task-specific fine-tuning, these methods provide an efficient solution for zero-shot
VideoQA.
However, existing frame sampling strategies are primarily designed for ques-
tion answering rather than temporal grounding. First, they retrieve discrete
keyframes without explicitly recovering continuous temporal spans. Consequently,
temporal boundaries must be inferred from sparse observations, often leading to
inaccurate localization. Second, their retrieval strategies remain largely object-
centric, emphasizing visual entities instead of action dynamics. As a result, they
may successfully identify relevant objects while missing the temporal evolution
of the target action. Our proposed DiffGround addresses both challenges through
Action Cue Prediction for action-aware frame retrieval and Semantic Diff Re-
finement for recovering complete action segments from sparse predictions.

Method
3.1
Overview
Given a video V and a question q, DiffGround predicts both the answer and
its supporting temporal segment through an offline preprocessing stage and an
online grounding stage, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

Fig. 2: Overview of the proposed DiffGround framework. DiffGround consists
of three stages. First, a frozen MLLM predicts an Action Cue from the input question
and uniformly sampled video frames, converting a reasoning-oriented question into
an action-centric retrieval query. Second, a frozen VLM (SigLIP2) jointly matches the
question and Action Cue to retrieve the Top-K most informative keyframes. Meanwhile,
an offline Semantic Diff Segmentation module partitions the video into semantically
coherent action segments. Finally, the retrieved keyframes are used for Video Question
Answering, and the predicted timestamps are refined by the precomputed semantic
segments to recover complete temporal boundaries for Video Question Grounding.
During offline preprocessing, we partition the video into semantically co-
herent action segments by measuring semantic differences between neighboring
SigLIP2 [11] visual embeddings. These action segments are computed once for
each video and reused for all subsequent questions.
During online inference, a frozen MLLM first performs an initial reasoning
step to generate an Action Cue, a concise action-centric description of the target
event. The Action Cue is combined with the original question to retrieve informa-
tive keyframes using a Vision-Language Model (VLM). The selected keyframes
are then fed into the MLLM to predict the final answer. Finally, Semantic Diff
Refinement aligns the retrieved keyframes with the precomputed action segments
to recover accurate temporal boundaries.
3.2
Offline Semantic Diff Segmentation
The Top-K keyframes selected by the VLM provide sparse observations of the
target action but do not directly reveal its complete temporal extent. We there-
fore seek to partition each video into semantically coherent action segments,
allowing sparse frame predictions to be naturally expanded into complete tem-
poral intervals during inference.

3.3
Action Cue Prediction
Existing zero-shot methods directly use the input question as the retrieval query
for frame selection. However, natural language questions are designed for reason-
ing rather than visual retrieval, often containing interrogative phrases, temporal
references, and contextual descriptions that dilute the retrieval signal. Conse-
quently, the retrieved frames frequently correspond to relevant objects instead
of the target action.
To address this issue, we introduce an Action Cue Prediction module
that performs an initial reasoning step before frame retrieval. Given the input

3.5
Online Semantic Diff Refinement
The semantic segmentation described above is performed entirely offline and
shared by all questions associated with the same video.
During online inference, the VLM first retrieves the Top-K keyframes using
the unified query representation described in Section 3.4. As illustrated in Fig. 2,
the selected keyframes are then fed into a second MLLM call to jointly predict
the answer and the supporting keyframe indices. Each predicted keyframe is sub-
sequently mapped to its corresponding precomputed semantic action segment.

4.2
Evaluation Metrics
We consider five metrics: intersection over union (IoU), intersection over predic-
tion (IoP), IoU@0.5, IoP@0.5, and Acc@GQA proposed in NExT-GQA [16]. IoU
measures the overlap between ground truth and predicted groundings. IoP mea-
sures the portion of predicted grounding containing the ground truth, similar to
precision. While these metrics focus on grounding, Acc@GQA focuses on both
the correctness of the answers and correct grounding with IoP ⩽0.5. Apart from
these metrics, we consider the Acc@QA metric for QA performance.

4.3
Implementation Details
Experimental Setup. We evaluate DiffGround on the NExT-GQA test set
(5,553 video-question pairs) and the STAR validation set (7,098 video-question
pairs). Unless otherwise specified, GPT-4o [4] is used as the Multimodal Large
Language Model (MLLM), while SigLIP2 serves as the Vision-Language Model
(VLM). The temperature is set to 0.0 for Action Cue Prediction to ensure de-
terministic outputs and 0.2 for the final Video Question Answering inference.
Offline Semantic Segmentation. The only dataset-specific hyperparameter
is the threshold coefficient α used in Algorithm 1. We set α = −0.25 for NExT-
GQA and α = 0.25 for STAR according to the average ground-truth answer
duration of each dataset. Smaller values produce finer-grained action segments,
whereas larger values generate coarser temporal partitions.
Implementation Details. All experiments are conducted on servers equipped
with two NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs (24GB memory each). Additional implemen-
tation details, prompt templates, comparisons with alternative VLMs (CLIP and
SigLIP2) and MLLMs (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL and Qwen3-VL), additional ablation
studies, and the proposed Acc@GQA (IoU) evaluation metric are provided in
the supplementary material.

4.6
Effectiveness of Action Cue Enhanced Key Frame Sampling
Table 4 and Table 5 analyze different frame retrieval strategies. Using the ques-
tion alone substantially improves Acc@GQA over uniform sampling, confirming
that semantic retrieval is considerably more effective than fixed sampling. How-
ever, this improvement comes at the expense of temporal grounding, as IoU
consistently decreases on both datasets. This observation indicates that ques-
tions are optimized for reasoning rather than visual retrieval, often identifying a
few highly discriminative frames while overlooking the complete temporal extent
of the target action.
We further compare our Action Cue with a simplified Object Cue that con-
tains only the primary object noun. Although both cues improve retrieval over
the baseline, the Action Cue consistently achieves higher Acc@GQA and IoP.
This demonstrates that explicitly modeling actions and object interactions pro-
vides a more informative retrieval target than isolated object descriptions.
Finally, combining the original question with the predicted Action Cue achieves
the strongest retrieval performance across both datasets. Nevertheless, despite
producing the highest Acc@GQA, the retrieved keyframes still exhibit relatively

4.7
Effectiveness of Semantic Diff Refinement
Table 4 and Table 5 evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed Semantic Diff
Refinement. Comparing “Our (Question + Action Cue)” with the complete Dif-
fGround framework shows that Semantic Diff consistently improves temporal
grounding performance on both NExT-GQA and STAR. In particular, mIoU in-
creases from 19.9% to 25.5% on NExT-GQA and from 7.7% to 20.1% on STAR,
demonstrating that the proposed refinement effectively recovers more complete
action boundaries from sparse keyframe predictions.
Interestingly, Semantic Diff Refinement slightly decreases Acc@GQA (IoP)
and mIoP while substantially improving IoU. This behavior reflects the differ-
ent optimization objectives of Video Question Answering and Video Question
Grounding. Action Cue Prediction is designed to retrieve a small number of
highly discriminative frames that maximize question answering accuracy. Such
compact predictions often achieve high overlap precision but cover only part of
the target action, leading to relatively low temporal IoU. In contrast, Semantic
Diff Refinement expands these sparse predictions to semantically coherent ac-
tion segments, producing predictions that more closely align with the complete
temporal extent of the ground-truth action.
Consequently, the refined segments may include additional contextual frames
that contribute little to frame-level retrieval metrics, resulting in a slight reduc-
tion in Acc@GQA (IoP) and mIoP. However, they provide substantially more
accurate temporal localization, as reflected by the consistent improvements in
mIoU. These results suggest that optimizing frame retrieval alone is insufficient
for Video Question Grounding, and that explicit temporal refinement is neces-
sary to recover complete action segments.
A complementary analysis using an IoU-based variant of Acc@GQA is
provided in the supplementary material, further illustrating the trade-off be-
tween retrieval-oriented and localization-oriented evaluation.
5
Conclusions
We introduced DiffGround, a training-free, action-driven framework for zero-
shot Video Question Grounding. Our key insight is that transforming reasoning-
oriented questions into action-centric representations and refining sparse predic-
tions using semantic transitions enables accurate and efficient temporal ground-
ing. Extensive experiments on NExT-GQA and STAR demonstrate that Dif-
fGround establishes a new state of the art while remaining computationally
efficient.

Appendix: A New Evaluation Metric for Video
Question Grounding: Acc@GQA (IoU)
To provide a more rigorous evaluation of temporal precision, we introduce a
complementary metric, Acc@GQA (IoU). The standard Acc@GQA metric [16]
relies on Intersection over Prediction (IoP@5). While useful, this metric can be
misleading: because it measures only precision (containment), an extremely short
prediction—even a single timestamp—can achieve a perfect score as long as it
falls within the ground truth. This fails to penalize models that capture only a
fraction of the full event duration.
To measure how accurately the predicted segment aligns with the ground
truth, we define Acc@GQA (IoU) using an Intersection over Union (IoU) thresh-
old of 0.3. As shown in Table 7, this metric reveals the true impact of our pipeline.
While adding the Semantic Difference Refinement (SD) causes a slight drop in
the containment-focused Acc@GQA (IoP) (from 38.5% to 35.0%), it drives a
substantial improvement in the alignment-focused Acc@GQA (IoU), boosting it
from 24.5% to 32.0%. This confirms that our refinement module effectively ex-
pands the prediction to match the true action boundaries, trading "safe," partial
containment for precise, full-event grounding.

Appendix: Performance of Utilizing Open-source
MLLMs
In Table 8, we evaluate the generalization capability of our framework by replac-
ing GPT-4o with state-of-the-art open-source MLLMs: Qwen2.5-VL-Instruct-
7B [1] and Qwen3VL-Instruct-8B [17]. While these models achieve reasonable
VQA performance (e.g., 74.8% Acc@QA for Qwen2.5), they exhibit a signifi-
cant drop in temporal grounding accuracy, with mIoU scores falling below 16%.
Qualitative analysis reveals that unlike GPT-4o [4], these smaller open-source
models struggle to follow the strict formatting constraints of our prompt. They
frequently hallucinate repetitive or generic index ranges (e.g., consistently out-
putting "1, 2" or "4, 5") regardless of the video content, likely due to a lack
of exposure to this specific grounding-via-indexing task during their instruction
tuning. This highlights that while open-source MLLMs are closing the gap in
visual reasoning, they still lag behind proprietary models in complex instruction
following and structured output generation.
