Abstract— Visual navigation in cluttered environments re-
quires robots to recover previously observed structure and
infer obstacles and free space that remain occluded. We
present a framework that combines temporal aggregation and
occupancy completion in an evidential bird’s-eye-view (BEV)
representation predicted from short RGB sequences. A decoder
adapts frozen VGGT-Omega features and combines historical
residual proposals using cellwise weights and a learned no-
update option. The BEV predictor produces a local map in
the latest ego frame without depth or camera-pose inputs or
explicit geometric warping. The evidential model treats observed
free space separately and predicts occupancy with associated
confidence across the remaining supported region. We train the
predictor on simulated RGB sequences paired with complete
and visibility-masked occupancy annotations. Our experiments
evaluate navigation in simulation and on a wheeled robot, with
additional demonstrations on quadruped and humanoid robots.
In simulated sequential two-goal navigation, our system achieves
89.6% success, outperforming the strongest evaluated baseline
by 32.5 percentage points. Across 60 wheeled-robot trials in 20
scenes, it achieves 73.3% success, compared with 33.3% for the
strongest evaluated baseline.

I. INTRODUCTION
Mobile robots navigating cluttered environments must make
spatial decisions from incomplete visual observations (Fig. 1).
Each camera view reveals only part of the surrounding layout.
Relying only on the latest view can cause a robot to revisit
unproductive routes or become trapped in local minima and
fail to reach its goal. Observations retained within the RGB
window provide evidence about nearby regions that are no
longer visible in the current image. The more complete map
gives the planner additional local context to choose more
efficient paths. Yet some obstacles and free space remain
hidden behind furniture and walls throughout this process.
Predicting these hidden regions can inform planning, but the
robot must account for the uncertainty of those predictions.
The challenge is therefore to build a local BEV map that
retains useful information across time, distinguishes observed
space from inferred structure, and indicates how reliable its
predictions are.
Learned occupancy anticipation has demonstrated that
visual context can help predict unseen structure [1]. In
autonomous driving, occupancy completion has shown strong
results in scene understanding by predicting occupancy in
both visible and occluded regions [2], [3]. For mobile
robots, anticipating obstacles and free space beyond current
observations can similarly provide a more complete map for
planning, supporting exploration and navigation before those
regions are directly observed [1], [4].

BEV occupancy maps place obstacles, free space, and
candidate paths in a common top-down frame. This repre-
sentation makes the basis of motion decisions transparent.
The map describes the environment, while the planner and
controller handle robot footprints, clearance margins, and
motion constraints. This separation leaves the choice of
planning algorithm open to the task and robot platform.
To address these challenges, we propose an RGB-based
framework for temporal BEV mapping and occupancy
completion. It combines evidence from earlier observations
with predictions of persistently hidden obstacles and free
space, producing occupancy and reliability estimates for
local planning. The BEV predictor requires neither depth nor
camera-pose inputs and uses no explicit geometric warping.
The main contributions are:
1) Spatially selective temporal BEV fusion. We propose
a framework that integrates RGB histories into an ego-
centric occupancy BEV. Historical residual proposals
from a shared decoder are weighted independently at
each map location. The fusion includes a learned no-
update option that allows the anchor representation to
be retained.
2) Observation-aware evidential occupancy completion.
We develop an occupancy-learning formulation that dis-
tinguishes observed free space from inferred occupancy
and predicts spatial support and confidence for planning.
Visibility-aware supervision supports completion of
occluded obstacles and free space while regularizing
evidence assigned to incorrect predictions.
3) Navigation integration and physical validation. We
integrate predicted occupancy and reliability with estab-
lished local motion planners. Experiments show higher
navigation success rates than the evaluated baselines
in simulation and wheeled-robot trials. Additional
quadruped and humanoid demonstrations illustrate
deployment on other embodiments.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Visual Navigation and Map-Based Planning
Goal-conditioned policies learn visual navigation through
diverse robot experience and action diffusion [5], [6], while
geometric supervision [7], [8] and multimodal goals [9] extend
their capabilities. Despite demonstrated cross-embodiment
transfer, their behavior can remain sensitive to robot size,
motion capabilities, and camera configurations represented
in training [5], [7].
Explicit traversability methods connect visual predictions to
local planning. GeNIE [10] projects single-frame traversability
into a BEV cost map and identifies the absence of a
maintained local map as a source of local-minimum failures.
In autonomous driving, camera-based BEV methods pro-
vide spatial representations for downstream perception and
planning. Lift-Splat-Shoot [11] and BEVFormer [12] com-
bine RGB features from multiple cameras in a common
BEV representation. These maps provide a spatial basis for
navigation. More recent methods such as ViGT [13] learn
to combine surround-view RGB images without requiring
camera calibration. The wider view helps planners identify
obstacles and choose clear routes.
These designs motivate our learned spatial queries. Our
system supplies occupancy and reliability to motion planners.
The robot footprint and traversal costs can be adjusted at
planning time without changing the learned BEV predictor.
B. Occupancy Prediction and Anticipation
Occupancy anticipation addresses a further requirement for
indoor navigation: inferring obstacles and free space hidden
by furniture and walls. Map-based completion methods extend
partially observed top-down layouts after map construction [4],
[14], whereas geometry-guided semantic scene completion
methods predict volumetric geometry and semantic categories
from RGB using supplied camera geometry and, in some
designs, internally predicted depth [15]–[17]. Navigation-
oriented methods further couple hidden-space prediction with
confidence for exploration and navigation, including RGB or
RGB-D occupancy anticipation in OccAnt [1] and RGB-D
semantic completion in SSCNav [18].
We focus on local occupied/free completion in persistently
unobserved regions, distinguishing observed free space from
inferred occupancy and estimating confidence in the com-
pleted map. This distinction allows the map to represent both
the inferred spatial layout and the reliability of its predictions.
C. Temporal Visual Geometry and Spatial Memory
Temporal integration complements occupancy completion
by retaining spatial evidence across successive observations.
Dense mapping has long pursued this objective through
active ranging, as in LiDAR-inertial systems such as FAST-
LIO2 [19]. Cameras offer rich visual information without
a dedicated range sensor, but recovering structure and
motion from images depends on reliable correspondence and
alignment. Weak texture, motion blur, and rapid viewpoint
changes can challenge this estimation process [20], [21].
Learned visual SLAM improves robustness by combining
learned correspondence with geometric optimization, as in
DROID-SLAM [21]; feed-forward models such as VGGT [22]
and VGGT-Omega [23] further learn geometry across multiple
images.
However, accurate surface reconstruction alone does not
establish the free-space coverage required for navigation.
BEVFormer [12] aligns historical BEV features using ego-
motion; SSCNav [18] registers observations using camera
poses; GeoUP [24] conditions visual features on camera
intrinsics and camera-to-reference poses. Errors in this
information can misalign historical features or maps, creating
false obstacles or distorting passage clearance. Such errors
are especially consequential when motion degrades image
quality or alignment.
Our work adopts the idea of learning spatial relationships
across views to selectively integrate RGB history into a local
BEV anchored to the latest ego frame, retaining evidence of
previously observed structure. The BEV predictor decodes
this history without depth or camera-pose inputs or explicit
geometric warping.

cells. This separation distinguishes free space supported by
observations from free space inferred through completion,

D. Supervision and Optimization
Complete and visibility-masked occupancy targets are
expressed in the latest ego frame. Support U covers the
camera-footprint union wherever complete labels are valid,
including occluded regions. For the fused prediction, O
contains free cells visible in at least one input frame, with
all annotations expressed in the latest ego frame. These
cells supervise the routing gate, while the remaining domain
G = U \ O supervises occupancy evidence. We partition G
into hidden free cells F, visible occupied surfaces S, and
hidden occupied cells Ho to balance supervision across these
regions.

E. Inference and Planning
The planner uses the predicted BEV and a metric point goal,
both expressed in the latest ego frame. Supported occupied
cells are inflated according to the robot footprint and safety
margin to form hard collision constraints. Traversal costs
combine occupancy pj, obstacle clearance, and distribution
reliability κj = sj clip(1 −4Vj, 0, 1). Within supported
traversable regions, lower reliability incurs a larger traver-
sal penalty. Unsupported cells retain their unknown labels
and incur finite traversal penalties, subject to the collision
constraints.
When the goal lies outside the map or the predicted
reachable region, the planner selects a local subgoal within
that region. Only a short path segment is executed, with an


additional limit on travel through unknown space. The next
RGB observation updates the BEV before replanning.
IV. EXPERIMENTS
In this section, we evaluate the proposed representation
from map prediction to navigation. We present our model
training procedure in Sec. IV-A and the comparison with
baselines in Sec. IV-B. Sections IV-B and IV-C examine
occupancy completion and simulation navigation, respectively.
Section IV-D reports the physical-robot evaluation.
A. Data Collection and Training
1) Data collection: We collect RGB sequences along
sampled navigable paths in Habitat environments from
HM3D [26], HSSD [27], and Matterport3D [28], varying cam-
era height, field of view, and motion speed. We select frames
using visual similarity to reduce redundancy while preserving
temporal order. The indoor corpus contains approximately
535k training sessions from 1k scenes and 26k validation
sessions from 54 disjoint scenes. Outdoor data comprise
approximately 100k training sessions from nine CARLA
towns. For each session, we generate complete and visibility-
masked BEV targets in the latest ego frame, following the
target definitions in Sec. III-D.
2) Training configuration: We freeze the pretrained VGGT-
Omega encoder and optimize the BEV adapters, temporal
decoder, and prediction heads. The decoder accepts variable-
length RGB sequences, with training sequences containing
approximately ten frames. Predictions use a 256 × 256 BEV
grid spanning 10 × 10 m. Hidden-occupied supervision and
wrong-class evidence regularization are introduced gradually
during training.
Gibson [29] and ProcTHOR [30] are unseen datasets used
only for evaluation and are entirely excluded from our BEV
training data.
B. Occupancy Prediction under Partial Observability
1) Evaluation settings: We evaluate 1,200 matched routes
from 101 scenes in HM3D, HSSD, and Gibson, with 400
routes per dataset. Scenes used for our BEV training are
excluded. Single-image prediction uses the final image of
each ten-frame route on a forward-facing 3.25 × 3.25 m grid
with 5 cm cells. History mapping uses all ten images on
a 256 × 256 grid spanning 10 × 10 m. Both settings share
the same final ego frame, and evaluation covers valid labels
within the corresponding camera-footprint union.
We compare against DiScene [15], SplatSSC [16], VGGT-
Omega [23], OccAnt-RGB, and OccAnt-RGBD [1]. DiScene
and SplatSSC receive camera calibration; their volumetric
predictions are projected into BEV and accumulated across
views using ground-truth poses. For OccAnt, we bypass
learned pose estimation and use ground-truth poses for
inter-frame accumulation, removing odometry errors from
the comparison. OccAnt-RGBD additionally receives sensor
depth. VGGT-Omega maps are projected from predicted
geometry and cameras using camera-height calibration. Our
predictor receives only RGB images, without camera intrinsics
or inter-frame extrinsics.

D. Physical-Robot Evaluation
We evaluate navigation on a wheeled robot in 20 scenes,
with three trials per scene and outcomes recorded for all
three compared methods. On these 60 matched trials, Ours
achieves 44/60 successes (73.3%), versus 20/60 (33.3%) for
X-NavDP and 16/60 (26.7%) for OmniVLA. LiMo-AUG
records no successes in 12 trials and is omitted from the
matched comparison because of incomplete coverage.
The scene difficulty labels help locate the remaining
failures. Ours succeeds in 14 of 18 matched trials labeled
Hard, compared with two for OmniVLA and one for X-NavDP.
In the Extreme Hard group, Ours succeeds in eight of 18,
versus none and one, respectively. The advantage therefore
persists in difficult scenes, but success remains limited in the
most challenging recorded group. Additional quadruped and
humanoid demonstrations are qualitative and excluded from
these statistics.
V. CONCLUSION
We presented an RGB-based local navigation framework
combining selective temporal BEV fusion with observation-
aware evidential occupancy completion. The predictor inte-
grates RGB history into a unified local map without depth
or camera-pose inputs, providing occupancy and reliability
for Dijkstra planning. Experiments demonstrate strong com-
pletion accuracy and the highest reported overall mIoU with
confidence filtering, alongside an accuracy–coverage tradeoff.
The integrated system achieves the highest reported success
rate and SPL in both simulated navigation benchmarks and
higher success in matched wheeled-robot trials. These results
support completed BEV maps as a practical interface between
RGB perception and local planning.
