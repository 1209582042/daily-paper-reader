## ABSTRACT 

Human memories are organized in a way that connects events with their cor-responding spatial contexts. An AI assistant should also be able to memorize and recall the nexus of events and spaces. Thus we introduce SENRI , a task that studies this space–event connection in long, continuous real-life egocentric video. It contains six task types in two directions: Event →Space , which recov-ers spatial knowledge from event cues, and Space →Event , which retrieves events from regions and trajectories. To evaluate this, we build SENRI-Bench , con-taining 500 manually annotated multiple-choice questions over 10 uninterrupted 

self-recorded videos with around 25.3 hours of content. Also, we evaluate three typical long video understanding paradigms: direct perception, evidence seeking, and persistent memory. The task remains challenging across all three paradigms, highlighting substantial room for improvement. Then we show two representative failure modes of existing memory methods and address these evidence gaps with 

SENRI-Agent , a controller that uses Target-Guided Visual Check (TGVC) to pro-pose local visual check tasks for parallel verifiers and integrate the reports into a working memory to help with reasoning. Built upon existing memory systems, SENRI-Agent improves the performance of existing baselines without modifying their stored memories. We hope SENRI will provide valuable insights into how real-life egocentric understanding should be evaluated, reveal the gaps in existing approaches, and offer a possible direction for improvement. ## 1 INTRODUCTION 

The world we inhabit is a continuous weaving of the spaces and events. As we move through it from an egocentric perspective, what happens becomes inseparable from how we experience the space where it happens. Our memories preserve the combination of the two. We can effortlessly ask, “Where was I when I tasted the fruit flower tea?” or “What did I encounter after walking to the end of the canal and returning along the opposite side?” and expect answers rich enough to reconstruct the experience in the mind’s eye. This ability to bind what happened with where it happened is fundamental to how we remember and understand lived experience. We therefore ask: can an AI assistant reason over the same space–event nexus from real-life videos? None of existing benchmarks fully evaluate this. As Table 1 shows, spatial-understanding bench-marks test spatial tasks such as relations and counting, yet they seldom ground spatial questions in events or consist exclusively of uncut, one-take videos captured in real-life environments (Yang et al., 2025a; 2026a; Gu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a). Long-video benchmarks provide real-life footage and emphasize event understanding and long range recall. But they neither consistently evaluate spatial reasoning (Wang et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2026b; Yang et al., 2025b) nor establish the evaluation of the connection between space and events in newly recorded videos (Chandrasegaran et al., 2024). This gap leaves the nexus between what happened and where it hap-pened largely untested and motivates us to work on a new perspective of long video understanding. To this end, we introduce SENRI , a task for understanding the Space– Event Nexus in Real-L Ife videos. SENRI has two complementary directions. Event →Space (ES) starts from an experienced or observed event and asks about its spatial context through localization, relation reasoning, ob-ject counting, and navigation planning . Then we build SENRI-Bench from 10 self-recorded videos totaling 25.3 hours and 500 manually anno-tated multiple-choice questions. Each video is a single uninterrupted take, preserving movement between scenes and the temporal order of events. The person who records a video also writes its questions. Then we evaluate 28 configurations across three long video understanding paradigms for long video understanding: direct perception that directly consumes sampled frames (Li et al., 2026b; Yan et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026a; Qwen Team, 2026a;b;c;d; Team et al., 2026; Google DeepMind, 2026a;b;c; OpenAI, 2025; 2026), evidence seeking agents that reason over a long video by inte-grating tools (Zeng et al., 2026; Qiu et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026b; Yin et al., 2026), and persis-tent memory systems that first build memories then perform querying and reasoning over memo-ries (Long et al., 2026; Nie et al., 2026; Yeo et al., 2026; Rege et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a). It turns out that the strongest direct perception, evidence seeking, and persistent memory configurations only reach 52.2%, 59.0%, and 58.8% accuracy respectively. Moreover, our findings include that meth-ods’ performance differs across different tasks and the inference model also has a large effect on persistent memory systems. Those findings suggest that the space–event nexus is a challenging task for existing long video understanding methods and implies the possible gaps in current systems. Also, we show two representative failure modes in existing memory methods in a case study: weak space–event nexus in text memory and missing target details in visual memory. That motivates us to develop SENRI-Agent, a controller that builds upon previous search and answer workflow but further introduces Target-Guided Visual Check (TGVC) which first specifies the event and spatial context implied by retrieved memories, then assigns targeted visual checks to parallel visual veri-fiers. Verifiers return targeted finding reports which are further used to update the working memory, allowing the reasoner to perform more targeted verification. With the inference models and stored memories held fixed, SENRI-Agent improves from two baselines’ performance, without rebuilding their memories or modifying retrieving interfaces. ## 2 SENRI-B ENCH 

Task Formulation and Taxonomy. Each SENRI-Bench example pairs a long, continuous ego-centric video with a four-choice question that connects an event to its spatial context. The questions follow two reciprocal directions. Event →Space (ES) uses an event as the retrieval cue and queries its spatial context through four task types: localization identifies where an event occurred; relation reasoning recovers spatial relations among entities or places; object counting counts distinct entities within the context; and navigation planning recovers or plans a route. Space →Event (SE) uses spa-tial context as the cue and asks the model to retrieve what occurred there: region-conditioned event retrieval (RER) queries events associated with a specified place or region; trajectory event retrieval 

queries events encountered along a described route or trajectory. 

Data Collection, Annotation, and Validation. SENRI-Bench contains 10 self-recorded egocen-tric videos totaling 25.3 hours, with 50 questions per video. We collected new recordings outside public video repositories, which reduces the risk that they appeared in the pretraining corpora of the evaluated models. Every video is captured in a single uninterrupted take using a chest-mounted 3Preprint. action camera. The recordings span both indoor and outdoor environments, including shopping malls, supermarkets, restaurants, museums, parks, campuses, streets, public transit and so on. The videos preserve physical transitions between places, for example, a walk from a park to a subway station, movement among floors and stores in a mall, and travel between indoor and outdoor regions. Then we process the recordings with EgoBlur (Raina et al., 2023) to detect and blur visible faces and vehicle license plates. The person who records a video manually writes its questions. This design gives the annotator first-hand knowledge of the route, environment, and events, supporting questions about spatial connections and intervening events. The annotator constructs candidate four-choice questions, assigns each to one of the six task types, and provides one correct answer together with three challenging distractors. An independent verifier then checks each question against the full video, ensuring that it is clearly worded, grounded in visible evidence, and admits a single un-ambiguous answer. Questions with ambiguous wording, overlapping answer choices, or insufficient visual evidence are revised or removed to ensure the correctness and minimize the ambiguity. 

Statistics and Visualization. SENRI-Bench comprises 500 four-choice questions over 10 videos, with 50 questions per video and 25.3 hours of footage in total (2.53 hours per video on average). The benchmark contains 334 Event →Space questions and 166 Space →Event questions. The former comprise 89 localization, 90 relation reasoning, 86 object counting, and 69 navigation planning questions; the latter comprise 88 RER and 78 trajectory event retrieval (TER) questions. Correct-answer positions are balanced both within individual videos and across the full benchmark, with 130, 130, 120, and 120 answers in positions A–D, respectively. Figure 1 visualizes this hierarchical task distribution, the balanced correct-answer positions, and the lexical composition of the questions and answer options. Figure 2 shows one representative example from each task type and pairs every question with its visual evidence picked from the original long video in a temporal order. 

## 3 BENCHMARK RESULTS 

3.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Evaluated Methods. We evaluate 28 model configurations spanning three paradigms for long-video understanding. Direct perception covers both frontier proprietary models and represen-tative open-weight video models, including general-purpose multimodal models (Qwen Team, 2026a;b;c;d; Team et al., 2026; Google DeepMind, 2026b;c;a; OpenAI, 2025; 2026) and models spe-cialized for spatial or long-video understanding (Li et al., 2026b; Yan et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026a). Evidence seeking covers systems that inspect query-relevant video regions on demand through se-quential or parallel tool use (Zeng et al., 2026; Qiu et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026b; Yin et al., 2026). Persistent memory covers systems that preprocess a video into reusable text or visual memories be-fore receiving a question then perform multi-round retrieving and reasoning before answering (Long et al., 2026; Nie et al., 2026; Yeo et al., 2026; Rege et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a). 

Evaluation Protocol. Every configuration receives the same 500 questions and answer options. For direct perception models, we uniformly sample 512 frames from each video and provide them to the model together with the question. Cambrian-S-7B, Cambrian-S-7B-LFP, InternVideo3-8B, and VideoChat3-4B are instead evaluated using the default inference settings provided by their orig-inal repositories. Most evidence seeking agents defaultly uses its original configurations, including its search procedure, tool configuration, and inference model. Most persistent memory systems use their native memory interface and retrieval-answer pipeline. To maximize the fairness of compari-son, we substitute the inference backbone of VideoARM, WorldMM, EG-Agent, and MAGIC-Video with Gemini 3.7 Flash and Qwen3.8-27B respectively while keeping the memories constructed by default settings. Other methods use their own fine-tuned versions of models so that we keep their original inference backbone. Moreover, for methods that are or depend on models that can consume videos, we encodes the frames into videos to reduce token cost and avoids context-length limita-tions. 3.2 MAIN FINDINGS 

Performance on SENRI. SENRI remains difficult for all three approaches. The best overall result is 59.0%, and no baseline reaches 60%. Direct perception models range from 31.4% to 52.2%, evidence seeking systems range from 29.8% to 59.0%, and persistent memory systems range from 33.6% to 58.8%. Among direct perception models, Gemini 3.7 Flash obtains 52.2% and overall performance of proprietary models is better than open-source ones. VideoARM with Gemini reaches 59.0%, while WorldMM and MAGIC-Video with the same inference model reach 58.8% and 58.6%, suggesting that existing agentic approaches are not yet sufficient to fully address the challenges. These results also leave a difference between the two directions of SENRI. ES accuracy is lower than SE accuracy in 25 of the 28 configurations. The strongest direct perception model scores 48.5% on ES and 59.6% on SE. VideoARM scores 56.0% and 65.1%, while WorldMM with Gemini scores 56.6% and 63.3%. Only Qwen3.6-35B-A3B and EG-Agent with Gemini perform better on ES, and Qwen3.5-9B obtains the same score in both directions. This suggests that under the current task composition, models struggle more with recovering spatial details from event cues than the opposite. 

Methods’ performance differs across tasks. No method leads all six task types. Persistent mem-ory methods achieve the best results on the four ES tasks, but the leading method changes from one task to another. Former quantitative results show that existing memory systems still fall short of understanding space–event nexus in real-life videos. To better understand limitations, we qualitatively analyze representative failure modes in Figure 3. It illustrates two ways in which retrieved memories can be relevant yet insufficient for answering an event-conditioned spatial question. In this example, the event cue “picking up a Labubu figure” must be linked to the corresponding shelf with 4 rows. 

I. Weak space–event nexus in text memory. In existing memory methods, retrievers write queries to find relevant text memories but find it hard to model space–event relationships. For example, the system retrieves three superficially related merchandise interactions: picking up handheld fans, in-specting toy keychains, and picking up a card pack. However, the two picking-up events occur in other places, while the only result does not show the spatial details. The retrieved text therefore identifies plausible moments without preserving the event–space nexus or the spatial attribute re-quired by the question. The ambiguity might cause misleading reasoning, or increases the numbers of reasoning rounds needed to further inspect the original video visually.

## 4 SENRI-A GENT 

4.1 METHOD 

The failure modes mentioned above reveal the ambiguity of retrieved memories and motivate a new approach that goes beyond simple retrieval. Thus we propose SENRI-Agent , a controller workflow built upon existing constructed memories and augment the systems with a Target-Guided Visual Check (TGVC) module. The controller uses the retrieved candidates to propose local visual check tasks for parallel verifiers and integrate the reports into a working memory to help with reasoning. The workflow is illustrated in Figure 4, which also shows the difference from the previous one.

Working Memory. Visual verification produces structured reports together with their supporting evidence frames. We maintain them as a bounded multimodal working memory 

WM t = ( zt, {pi}i∈S t ) = ( zt, {(ri, E i)}i∈S t ) , (3) where zt is a concise summary, St indexes the verification outputs currently retained in working memory, ri is a selected verifier report, and Ei contains its supporting timestamped evidence frames. After each verify action, the working memory is updated by the new verification reports Pt:

WM t+1 = U(Q, WM t, M<t , Pt), (4) where M<t contains the memories exposed in all previous rounds, and Pt contains the newly gen-erated report–evidence pairs. The update refreshes the summary and selects a small subset of verifi-cation outputs that are most useful for subsequent decisions. Selected reports are preserved together with their original supporting evidence frames, while the summary records established facts, unre-solved ambiguities, and evidence still needed. This bounded representation prevents the multimodal context from growing across rounds while retaining the evidence required for later reasoning. 4.2 EXPERIMENTS 

Experimental Setup. SENRI-Agent is built upon two representative persistent memory methods, WorldMM and MAGIC-Video by reusing their constructed memories and retrieval interfaces. We evaluate SENRI-Agent on SENRI-Bench against the original workflows of the two baselines using two inference models, Qwen3.8-27B and Gemini 3.7 Flash. The maximum number of reasoning rounds is set to 5, followed by one answer round, as in the baselines. We keep 3 reports and 4 evidence frames per report in the working memory and set maximum 3 checks per verification.

## 5 RELATED WORK 

Spatial Understanding Benchmarks. Existing video benchmarks assess spatial intelligence from multiple perspectives. VSI-Bench (Yang et al., 2025a) evaluates spatial reasoning within individual indoor scenes. VSI-Super (Yang et al., 2026a) concatenates recordings to extend recall and count-ing. VSI-Super-Wild (Gu et al., 2026) uses continuous natural recordings, and UCS-Bench (Wang et al., 2026a) evaluates spatial reasoning under changes in user position and orientation. LongSpace-Bench (Lang et al., 2026) evaluates spatial memory in room-tour videos. These tasks primarily characterize spatial configurations, motion, and object counts, whereas SENRI focuses on how such spatial information is grounded in and linked to events in self-recorded real-life videos. 

Long Video Understanding Benchmarks. LVBench (Wang et al., 2025), LongVideoBench (Wu et al., 2024), the Video-MME series (Fu et al., 2025; 2026), and MLVU (Zhou et al., 2025) bench-mark long video understanding across diverse video sources and multiple tasks. HourVideo (Chan-drasegaran et al., 2024) extends such evaluation to hour-long videos and includes spatial reasoning. EgoSchema (Mangalam et al., 2023) focuses on temporally extended reasoning over egocentric ac-tivities, while HippoVlog (Lin et al., 2026b) and EgoLifeQA (Yang et al., 2025b) move toward un-derstanding long-form daily-life experiences. More recently, EgoMemReason (Wang et al., 2026b) and EgoMonth (Chen et al., 2026b) evaluate long-horizon memory over continuous experiences spanning days or weeks. SENRI centers its evaluation on the connection between space and events. Each video is a single uninterrupted take that preserves movement between spaces and events. 

Agentic Long Video Understanding Methods. A growing body of work formulates long video understanding as an agentic process. One line of work performs evidence seeking, where an agent iteratively selects temporal regions, invokes multimodal tools, and refines its reasoning based on the acquired evidence (Wang et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2026; Qiu et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026b; Yin et al., 2026; Lin et al., 2026a; Tian et al., 2026). Related agentic methods have also been explored for spatial intelligence, where spatial tools are used to accumulate geometric and semantic evidence across observations (Dai et al., 2026). Another line builds persistent memory over videos, organizing visual experience into reusable episodic, semantic, multimodal, or structured representations that can support subsequent queries (Long et al., 2026; Nie et al., 2026; Yeo et al., 2026; Rege et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a; Chen et al., 2026a; Choi et al., 2026). Our method builds on the established memories and uses the retrieved evidence for target-guided visual check in the original video. 

## 6 CONCLUSION 

In this work, we introduced SENRI, a new perspective on long video understanding that focuses on the nexus between events and their spatial contexts. We instantiated this problem in SENRI-Bench, a benchmark of long, uninterrupted, self-recorded egocentric videos with six task types spanning two complementary directions, Event →Space and Space →Event. Through an extensive evaluation of direct perception, evidence seeking, and persistent memory approaches, we found that existing systems still struggle on this task. Our analysis further revealed two representative limitations of current memory systems. Motivated by these observations, we proposed SENRI-Agent, which aug-ments existing persistent memory systems with Target-Guided Visual Check and multimodal work-ing memory, consistently improving their performance without modifying the underlying memories. We hope SENRI provides a useful testbed for studying how long video systems can move beyond existing abilities toward building memories that preserve the structure of lived experience. 9Preprint. A.2 MORE EVALUATION DETAILS 

We provide additional details on model inputs, inference settings, and scoring. Our evaluation covers three ways of accessing long-video evidence: direct video perception, on-demand evidence seeking, and persistent memory. Each configuration is evaluated on the same 500 underlying questions, with four answer options per question. 

Direct video perception. For general purpose direct perception models, the main evaluation uses a budget of 512 uniformly sampled frames per video. The sampled observations are supplied together with the question and answer options, allowing the model to reason directly over visual input. For evaluations using preconstructed video carriers, we verify that each carrier contains exactly 512 sampled frames. The open weight Qwen baselines use non-thinking greedy decoding, with the native video processing interface configured for at most 512 frames. We retain the released inference settings for the specialist direct-perception systems, including Cambrian-S-7B, Cambrian-S-7B-LFP, InternVideo3-8B, and VideoChat3-4B. Their visual sampling and processing procedures therefore follow their respective implementations. These results evaluate the available systems under their intended inference procedures. 

Evidence-seeking systems. Evidence-seeking methods access video observations through their released search or inspection interfaces. The inference backbone reported in Table 2 is the model that makes the final answer decision; a system may additionally use other models within its tools. For example, LongVideo-R1 uses a Qwen3- 8B controller together with Qwen2.5-VL-72B and Qwen2.5-VL- 32B video tools. For VideoARM, we keep other configurations fixed when comparing Qwen3.8-27B and Gemini 3.7 Flash. This controls the search setup across inference backbones, although their generated decisions and resulting execution trajectories may still differ. 

Persistent-memory systems. Persistent-memory methods construct reusable memories before re-ceiving the benchmark questions. At evaluation time, the question-answering workflow accesses these memories through the method’s retrieval interface. For the paired Qwen3.8-27B and Gemini 3.7 Flash evaluations of WorldMM, EG-Agent, and MAGIC-Video, we reuse the same method spe-cific memories and retrieval implementations. Memory is not rebuilt separately for each inference 21 Preprint. backbone. Consequently, these paired comparisons measure how different inference models use a fixed memory resource within each method. Memory construction models are distinguished from the inference backbones listed in the results table. For example, M3- Agent additionally uses Qwen2.5-Omni-7B during memory construction. Such components remain part of the evaluated system even when they are not the model responsible for selecting the final answer. 

Metrics and aggregation. Each category accuracy is computed using the number of questions in that category as its denominator. Overall accuracy is computed directly over the complete bench-mark. We retain integer correct counts during aggregation and round percentages to one decimal place only after division. This ensures that the reported overall and subset results are derived con-sistently from the final question-level predictions. Reported differences in percentage points are calculated from the displayed accuracies.

## C MORE EXPERIMENTS 

C.1 EFFECT OF NUMBER OF FRAMES SAMPLED 

We evaluate four Qwen models on all 500 SENRI-Bench questions with maximum frame budgets of 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, and 1,024, using greedy decoding with thinking disabled. Figure 5 shows that all four models obtain higher accuracy at 512 frames than at 32 frames, with gains of 3.6 to 9.4 percentage points. The improvement varies across models: Qwen3.6-27B rises from 34.4% to 43.8%, while Qwen3.5-27B rises from 40.2% to 43.8%. Several models also lose accuracy at intermediate budgets, so the benefit of additional frames is not uniform. Increasing the budget from 512 to 1,024 frames raises Qwen3.5-27B from 43.8% to 46.8%, but lowers accuracy for the other three models. The comparison therefore shows that additional frames can improve accuracy, but the largest budget does not consistently give the best result and justifies the 512-frame budget used in our main experiments. We examine how the gains in Table 3 are distributed across videos. For each memory system and inference model, we compare the accuracy of the original workflow and SENRI-Agent on each video. Each comparison covers the same 500 questions, with 50 questions per video. Figure 6 shows that SENRI-Agent improves WorldMM on seven of the ten videos with either in-ference model. With MAGIC-Video, it improves five videos, ties two, and declines on three with Qwen3.8-27B; with Gemini 3.7 Flash, it improves six and declines on four. The magnitude of the gains also varies. We chose 4-choice design by follow-ing the common practice in existing work on long video understanding benchmarks. Expanding the recordings and adding questions that require free-form answers are natural extensions of the current benchmark and are good directions for future research. 

Comparing ES and SE. The two directions contain different task types: ES covers localization, relation reasoning, object counting, and navigation planning, while SE covers two forms of event retrieval. The lower ES accuracy in 25 of the 28 configurations therefore describes performance under this task composition; it does not establish that Event →Space is intrinsically harder. For example, object counting is a challenging category included only in ES. Future evaluations could pair questions in both directions around the same events and locations, with comparable evidence requirements and question difficulty, to better separate the effects of direction and task composition. 

Method evaluation. We did not cover all possible configurations of evaluated methods. And we did not cover all possible methods available. But we chose representative ones. More models’ per-formance on SENRI-Bench remains interesting future work. We evaluate SENRI-Agent with two persistent memory systems and two inference models. Its improvements across these four configu-rations support the use of targeted verification, while its applicability to other memory architectures remains to be tested. And its performance on other datasets are also valuable future directions. However, the current benchmark and method are designed to study the space–event nexus in long egocentric videos, and we leave the exploration of other video types to future work. 26
