ABSTRACT 

Image-based spatial reasoning requires perception, decomposition, and inference, but the stage that limits accuracy can change during training. Answer-level re-wards neither localize failures to individual stages nor provide discriminative group-relative feedback when all rollouts receive the same reward. Process super-vision provides intermediate feedback, but fixed weights cannot adapt reward allo-cation to these changing bottlenecks. To address this gap, we propose CALIPER, an adaptive process-reward framework supported by SpatialTrace, a corpus of stage-decomposed spatial reasoning traces. CALIPER jointly estimates the as-sociations between stage-wise reference coverage and answer correctness through within-group ridge regression, using these associations as utility proxies to grad-ually update reward weights. The resulting process reward supervises all rollout groups, including uniformly failing ones. Because utility estimates can become unreliable when answer-discriminative groups are scarce, a closed-loop harness monitors estimator reliability and validation performance to guide checkpoint roll-back, rollout diversification, and early stopping. Extensive experiments across eight benchmarks and three backbones show that CALIPER achieves the highest mean under both one-shot and harness training, with consistent gains over fixed allocation across RL optimizers. Further analyses confirm the benefits of adaptive reward allocation and harness control, with favorable scaling in model/data size. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Image-based spatial reasoning remains a persistent weakness of vision-language models, spanning metric and relational reasoning in 3D scenes (Ma et al., 2025), open-domain scene understanding (Zhang et al., 2025) and region-level search in high-resolution images (Wu & Xie, 2024). Although typically evaluated as a direct mapping from an image and question to an answer, solving these tasks often involves distinct stages of perception, decomposition and inference, whose relative importance varies across instances. Reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR) (Guo et al., 2025), often using group-relative advantages over multiple rollouts of the same question (Shao et al., 2024), has been extended to multimodal reasoning by Vision-R1 (Huang et al., 2026) and DeepEyes (Zheng et al., 2026). However, its answer-level supervision has two limitations. It cannot distinguish failures in perception, decomposition or inference, and it provides no discriminative signal when all rollouts receive the same reward, especially on difficult spatial instances. Reference-assisted verification enriches answer grading but retains a scalar outcome reward, leaving stage-level credit assignment and uniform-reward degeneracy unresolved, as illustrated in Fig. 1a,b. Process supervision (Uesato et al., 2022) provides finer feedback through human-annotated step la-bels (Lightman et al., 2024) or rollout-derived labels in Math-Shepherd (Wang et al., 2024b). How-ever, a stage score quantifies alignment with a reference or grading criterion. It does not determine the supervision weight appropriate to that stage. Combining such scores with predefined weights leaves reward allocation insensitive to changes in the relationship between stage performance and answer correctness. Fig. 1c illustrates this gap in adaptive credit allocation across perception, de-composition and inference despite access to intermediate feedback.

Stage utility guides allocation through the association between stage performance and answer cor-rectness under the current rollout distribution. Low reference coverage does not establish the ex-pected benefit of increasing a stage’s reward weight. Allocation therefore requires an estimate of this association, conditioned on the policy’s evolving behavior across spatial competencies. Roll-out groups with variation in answer correctness supply the contrast needed for estimation, whereas uniformly correct or incorrect groups provide no such contrast. Adaptive supervision must estimate utility from informative groups and maintain reliable allocation as they become scarce. To address this gap, we introduce SpatialTrace and CALIPER, as illustrated in Fig. 1d. SpatialTrace represents heterogeneous spatial tasks through a common schema for perception, decomposition and inference while retaining available reasoning from incomplete traces. CALIPER uses this represen-tation to allocate process supervision according to estimated stage utility. It estimates the association between stage coverage and answer correctness from answer-discriminative rollout groups, then ap-plies the resulting allocation to all groups, including uniformly failing ones. A closed-loop harness monitors estimation reliability and validation performance to guide checkpoint rollback, rollout di-versification and stopping. Our contributions are as follows. • We formulate process-reward allocation as a policy-dependent estimation problem, distin-guishing stage quality from its association with answer correctness. Utility estimation is restricted to informative groups, while process supervision applies to all groups. • We construct SpatialTrace, a reasoning-augmented spatial dataset spanning heterogeneous spatial tasks, in which approximately 84% of items carry complete three-stage traces and the remainder retain available reasoning without imputed stage labels. • We propose CALIPER, a closed-loop framework that estimates stage utility from answer-discriminative rollout groups, applies the resulting allocation to all groups, including de-generate groups, and audits that estimate through a rule-based harness. • Across eight benchmarks and three backbones, CALIPER attains the highest mean under both protocols and improves on fixed allocation across RL optimizers. Ablations support adaptive allocation and harness control, with gains at larger model and data scales. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Spatial Reasoning in Vision-Language Models. Spatial reasoning in vision-language models advances through spatial supervision, visual search and outcome optimization. SpatialVLM (Chen 2et al., 2024a) and SpatialRGPT (Cheng et al., 2024) derive metric and relational supervision from 3D reconstruction or depth. Benchmarks include 3DSRBench (Ma et al., 2025) for 3D relations, SpatialEval (Wang et al., 2024a) for synthetic reasoning, MME-RealWorld (Zhang et al., 2025) for real-world scenes and RoboSpatial (Song et al., 2025) for embodied settings. V* (Wu & Xie, 2024), ZoomEye (Shen et al., 2025) and DeepEyes (Zheng et al., 2026) improve evidence acqui-sition through region selection, cropping and zooming. RLVR extends to multimodal reasoning in Vision-R1 (Huang et al., 2026), R1-Zero-style spatial training (Liao et al., 2025) and video spatial reasoning in SpaceR (Ouyang et al., 2025). These efforts improve supervision, evidence acquisition and outcome optimization while leaving intermediate-stage credit allocation unresolved. 

Process Supervision in RLVR. Chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al., 2022) makes intermedi-ate reasoning explicit, while DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025) optimizes reasoning trajectories with verifiable outcome rewards. GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) uses group-relative advantages, DAPO (Yu et al., 2025) modifies clipping and rollout filtering, and GSPO (Zheng et al., 2025a) uses sequence-level importance ratios. These methods govern how rewards are translated into policy updates, but do not change their granularity. Process supervision instead assigns feedback to intermediate rea-soning steps, using direct annotations (Lightman et al., 2024) or labels derived from rollouts (Wang et al., 2024b). PRIME (Cui et al., 2025) integrates implicit process rewards into online RL, and PURE (Cheng et al., 2025) replaces summation with min-form credit assignment. Such feedback alleviates outcome-reward sparsity, but its aggregation follows a fixed rule, leaving reward alloca-tion insensitive to the policy’s changing bottlenecks. CALIPER addresses this orthogonal problem by estimating policy-dependent stage utility and adapting process-reward weights accordingly. 

3 SPATIAL TRACE DATASET 

Task Formulation. SpatialTrace targets spatial question answering with an explicit reasoning trace. Given an image I and a question x, a vision-language policy receives q = ( I, x ) and gener-ates a trace τ followed by an answer a. The desired behavior comprises extracting relevant visual evidence, decomposing the question into spatial subproblems, and deriving an answer consistent with that evidence. We represent these components as τ = ( τp, τ d, τ i), corresponding to perception, decomposition and inference. A reference answer a⋆ supports outcome verification, while stage annotations support separate assessment of these reasoning components. Construction. Learning this behavior requires coverage of spatial problems with different percep-tual and inferential demands. SpatialTrace draws metric 3D questions from 3DSRBench (Ma et al., 2025), Omni3D-Bench (Brazil et al., 2023) and SAT-Spatial-VQA-5k (Ray et al., 2025). Open-domain questions are sampled from MMBench (Liu et al., 2024), MME-RealWorld-Lite (Zhang et al., 2025) and MMStar (Chen et al., 2024b). Region-level search instances are sampled from RoboSpatial-Home (Song et al., 2025), SpatialEval (Wang et al., 2024a) and SpatialRGPT-Bench (Cheng et al., 2024), with SeePhys (Xiang et al., 2026) supplying auxiliary physical reasoning. Fig. 2 summarizes SpatialTrace’s 1,180 single-image questions with verifiable answers. Family quotas preserve coverage, with source-stratified splits of 1,000 training and 180 validation items. 3Traces. The annotations encode the relationship between visual evidence, intermediate reasoning and the reference answer. Perception records relevant visual observations. Decomposition identifies the subproblems required by the question. Inference connects the observations to the answer. Each record also includes answer reconciliation and the final answer, allowing discrepancies between vi-sual evidence and the source label to be recorded. An image-grounded annotator drafts the trace, and a text-only editor refines its reasoning and records unsupported inferences or ambiguity. Ap-proximately 84% of the corpus contains all three stage fields. Other records retain their answers and available reasoning without imputed stage labels. Appendix A details the annotation protocol.

5 EXPERIMENTS 

Implementation Details. The primary experiments use the GRPO implementation of EasyR1 (Zheng et al., 2025b) on verl (Sheng et al., 2025). The backbone is frozen throughout and a LoRA adapter (Hu et al., 2022) of rank 64 is trained with n = 8 rollouts per question at temperature 1.2,AdamW at learning rate 1×10 −5, and 512 questions per optimizer step. Backbones are Qwen3-VL-4B (Bai et al., 2025a), Qwen2.5-VL-7B (Bai et al., 2025b) and InternVL3-8B (Zhu et al., 2025). One-shot training comprises ten optimizer steps, approximately five passes over 1,000 items. Har-ness training comprises at most six rounds of five steps. Each run uses eight NVIDIA H200 GPUs (141 GB each) on a single node. Unless varied explicitly, the backbones share the reward, estimator, allocation and harness settings specified in Appendix B. 

Baselines. We compare two untrained configurations and three reward-based training baselines. Direct prompting requests an answer without a reasoning trace. Chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al., 2022) uses the same three-stage format as trained methods to isolate explicit reasoning before training. Within each training protocol, all methods share the backbone, adapter and rollout settings. Outcome reward uses final-answer supervision, following GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and Vision-R1 (Huang et al., 2026). Reference-assisted reward is a controlled variant in which the verifier receives the SpatialTrace reference as grading context. The process reward model (PRM) baseline follows stage-level labeling in Lightman et al. (2024) and Wang et al. (2024b), and combines its three predicted scores under fixed weights. Appendix B.1 details reward scorers, baseline and prompts. 

Benchmarks & Metrics. We train each backbone on our SpatialTrace dataset (Section 3) and evaluate it on eight primary benchmarks. Four target 3D and relational spatial understanding, namely 3DSRBench (Ma et al., 2025), RoboSpatial-Home (Song et al., 2025), and spatial question-answering splits built on Omni3D (Brazil et al., 2023) and SAT (Ray et al., 2025). Two are the general multimodal benchmarks MMBench (Liu et al., 2024) and MMStar (Chen et al., 2024b), and the remaining two are the high-resolution real-world benchmark MME-RealWorld-Lite (Zhang et al., 2025) and the synthetic spatial puzzles of SpatialEval (Wang et al., 2024a). Training and evalu-ation images are disjoint (Appendix A). We report benchmark scores (%) and their unweighted mean (Avg) on fixed 200 -item subsets. 5.1 MAIN RESULTS 

Performance Comparison. Table 1 shows that CALIPER achieves the highest mean score across the eight benchmarks for all three backbones under both training protocols. Under harness training, its margin over the strongest trained baseline reaches 2.06 percentage points on InternVL3-8B. 6 CONCLUSION 

This paper proposes CALIPER, an adaptive process-reward framework that allocates spatial-reasoning supervision according to policy-dependent stage utility. CALIPER estimates utility from answer-discriminative rollout groups and applies the resulting stage weights to all groups, including those with uniformly incorrect answers. A closed-loop harness monitors estimator reliability and validation performance to guide training decisions. Extensive experiments across eight benchmarks, three backbones and three optimizers show gains over matched fixed weights. 9AI USE STATEMENT

Sources and Sampling. SpatialTrace combines ten single-image question sources grouped by rea-soning demand in Table 4. QUESTION VALIDITY item, binary 

“Given the image and the question, can an attentive reader determine a single answer from visible evidence?”  

> POSITIVE

The image contains the objects and relations the question asks about, and one answer follows from them.  

> FAILURE

The evidence is occluded or outside the frame, the reference frame is unspecified, or several answers remain defensible. 

PERCEPTION FIDELITY perception, three-point ordinal 

“Does each observation report objects, attributes or positions that are visible in the image and needed for the question?”  

> POSITIVE

Faithful reports only visible content and covers the evidence the answer depends on.  

> FAILURE

Partial is correct but omits one needed object or relation. Unfaithful describes absent objects, mis-reads a position or orientation, or states the answer in place of evidence.

“Would answering these subquestions in order suffice to answer the original question?”  

> POSITIVE

Adequate covers every object, quantity and reference frame the answer depends on.  

> FAILURE

Partial misses one of them. Inadequate restates the question, targets irrelevant objects, or answers the subquestions itself.

“How well does the inference derive the reference answer from the recorded observations?”  

> POSITIVE

> POSITIVE

The judgment, made before the recorded answer alignment is shown, matches that record.  

> FAILURE

The rater finds an unsupported label or unjustified precision that the record accepts, or rejects a recorded concern.

Question. Consider the real-world 3D locations and orientations of the objects. If I stand at the grey car’s position facing where it is facing, is the fire hydrant on the left or right of me? 

Ground-truth answer. B. on the right 

Perception 

• A grey car is parked on the left side of the image, facing towards the right. 

• A red fire hydrant is located on the sidewalk near the intersection, closer to the right side of the image. 

Decomposition 

• What is the position and orientation of the grey car? 

• Where is the fire hydrant located relative to the car? 

• If standing at the car’s position and facing its direction, which side is the fire hydrant on? 

Inference 

• Step 1: The grey car is observed to be parked on the left side of the image and is facing towards the right. This establishes its orientation as directed to the right. 

• Step 2: The fire hydrant is located on the sidewalk near the intersection and is closer to the right side of the image. This places it spatially to the right of the car’s orientation. 

• Step 3: If a person stands at the grey car’s position and faces the direction the car is facing (towards the right), the fire hydrant, being positioned to the right of the car, would also be on the right side of the person. 

Omni3D-Bench, 3D metric reasoning 

Question. If the combined height of the TV and TV stand is 2.7m, how tall is the fireplace? 

Ground-truth answer. 2.989 

Perception 

• The TV is mounted on a stand, and both are visible in the image. 

• The fireplace is visible on the right side of the image. 

• The TV and TV stand are positioned at the same height as the fireplace. 

• The height of the TV and stand is given as 2.7 meters. 

• The fireplace has a visible height that extends above the TV and stand. 

Decomposition 

• What is the height of the TV and stand? (Given as 2.7 meters) 

• How much taller is the fireplace compared to the TV and stand? 

• What is the total height of the fireplace? 

Inference 

• Step 1: The height of the TV and stand is explicitly given as 2.7 meters. 

• Step 2: The fireplace is visually observed to extend above the top of the TV and stand. Based on the image, the additional height of the fireplace above the TV and stand appears to be slightly less than 0.3 meters. 

• Step 3: Using a coarse visual estimate, the additional height of the fireplace is approxi-mated as 0.289 meters. This estimate is based on proportional comparison in the image, but no exact measurement tools are available. 

• Step 4: Adding the height of the TV and stand (2.7 meters) to the estimated additional height of the fireplace (0.289 meters) gives a total height of 2.989 meters for the fireplace. 

Answer alignment, recorded as not supported 

• The stated precision of 2.989 meters exceeds what can be justified from the available vi-sual evidence. The answer should be treated as a plausible estimate rather than an exact measurement.

B.1 REWARD SCORING AND BASELINE DETAILS 

Reward Computation. The policy generates three numbered reasoning stages followed by an answer. DeepSeek-V4-Flash (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2026), at temperature 0, scores all three stages in one call by comparing each stage with its corresponding reference. Independent grading prevents evidence from one stage from inflating another stage’s score. For incomplete references, the scorer excludes missing stages and renormalizes the remaining allocation. The same judge evaluates free-form answers if option matching is inapplicable. The physics term checks units, magnitudes and plausibility in the answer region and is active only if at least one check applies. The length penalty increases linearly from zero at 1500 tokens to one at the 3072 -token cap, scaling the weighted reward as in Eq. 2. 

Process Reward Model Scorer. The PRM baseline combines a LoRA adapter with a three-output regression head on the policy backbone. It is fitted to stage labels obtained by scoring the untrained policy’s rollouts against SpatialTrace references. During policy training, its predictions replace reference-based stage scores in Eq. 2 and receive equal weights. CALIPER additionally accesses reference traces at scoring time. The frozen-allocation control preserves that access and the judge, isolating the contribution of weight adaptation. 

Reward Prompts. The judge computes the stage-coverage terms in Eq. 2 from one call per rollout. Inputs contain up to 500 question characters, 6000 reasoning characters and ten reference steps per stage. Stage labels distinguish visual observations, question decomposition and reasoning. The split clause indicates a multi-stage reference, and the strict clause requires evidence to match its designated stage. The loose-judging ablation replaces only that requirement, allowing a rollout step to match any reference stage. 19 Stage-Matching Judge
