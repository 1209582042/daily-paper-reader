# EgoP 2C: Internalizing Plan-Perceive-Compose for Spatial Reasoning in Egocentric Video 

Chaoyu Li 1,2,∗, Arka Sadhu 2, Yuliang Li 2, Hyo Jin Kim 2, Yuning Chai 2, Pooyan Fazli 11Arizona State University, 2Meta

Work done during an internship at Meta. 

Answering complex spatial reasoning questions from egocentric video requires determining what evidence is needed, acquiring targeted observations, and combining them through multiple reasoning steps. Existing vision-language models (VLMs) struggle to perform this process reliably. Agentic systems address this challenge by querying external vision and geometry tools, but training their reasoning leaves these tools unchanged, so inaccurate observations can still lead to incorrect answers. We introduce EgoP 2C, a framework that internalizes a plan-perceive-compose process within a single VLM. To this end, we first train specialized Planner and Perception teachers using separate adapters (Dual-LoRA), then fine-tune the corresponding experts in a mixture-of-experts (MoE) student under their supervision. We subsequently train the student on trajectories generated by its current policy using a segment-level extension of multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD), assigning different trajectory segments to different supervisors. The Planner teacher supervises which evidence-seeking questions to ask, when to stop gathering evidence, and how to compose the final answer. The Perception teacher supervises the video-grounded answers to those questions. At inference, the student executes this adaptive workflow end-to-end, without external spatial tools. Across three spatial reasoning benchmarks, EgoP 2C students built from Qwen3-VL-8B and SenseNova-SI-1.3-8B outperform their base models by an average of 9.2 and 7.5 percentage points, respectively. Across both backbones, EgoP 2C reduces inference FLOPs by 65.8-66.3% and achieves 1.29 -1.49 × speedups over their shared Dual-LoRA teacher. 

# 1 Introduction 

Spatial reasoning in egocentric (first-person) video requires understanding object locations, distances, and spatial constraints (Li et al., 2026b; Horton et al., 2026). This capability can support wearable assistants (Ma-jumdar et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025), visual assistance for blind and low vision users (Xiao et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026), robot navigation and manipulation (Cheng et al., 2025; Driess et al., 2023), and augmented reality (Yuan et al., 2025). However, camera motion, occlusion, and a limited field of view cause relevant objects to appear only briefly or partially, scattering evidence across frames (Yang et al., 2025a; Horton et al., 2026). To answer complex spatial questions, a model must determine which spatial facts are needed, extract the observations from this fragmented evidence (Li et al., 2026a), and combine them through multiple reasoning steps. For example, deciding whether the sofa fits between the two objects in Figure 1 requires locating the objects, estimating the sofa dimensions and available gap, and comparing these measurements. An incorrect spatial estimate can invalidate the answer even when the subsequent reasoning is sound. Prior work improves spatial understanding either by training vision-language models (VLMs) on spatial questions (Chen et al., 2024) or by providing estimated 3D object coordinates as textual input (Gholami et al., 2026). These models reason over the supplied video inputs or estimated object coordinates, but are not explicitly trained to recognize when those inputs are insufficient and seek further evidence. We introduce EgoP 2C (Egocentric Plan-Perceive-Compose), a framework that implements these three stages through two specialist roles: Planner and Perception . Given a video and a spatial question, the Planner either selects direct answering or generates subquestions for Perception and uses its answers to compose the final response. Neither path uses external spatial tools. Because training both roles with shared parameters can lead to conflicting updates, we first train separate Planner and Perception teachers using low-rank adaptation (LoRA) modules on a shared frozen VLM backbone (Hu et al., 2022). In this Dual-LoRA configuration, each adapter is trained through supervised fine-tuning (SFT) followed by reinforcement learning using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024). Executing the Dual-LoRA teachers requires adapter switching and repeated context processing, so we integrate their capabilities into one student while retaining separate planning and perception parameters. A mixture-of-experts (MoE) architecture provides role experts within a shared backbone. We construct this student through sparse upcycling (Komatsuzaki et al., 2023), which copies selected feedforward networks of a dense VLM into experts for planning and composition, perception, and direct answering. We initialize the Planner and Perception experts using their corresponding teachers’ supervision. The student executes the interaction within one checkpoint and reuses a common video prefix across roles. To train the initialized student on its trajectories, we extend multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) (Ma et al., 2026) by assigning specialist teachers to corresponding trajectory segments. The Planner teacher supervises planning and composition, and the Perception teacher supervises observations. The frozen base VLM supervises direct answers. We evaluate EgoP 2C initialized from Qwen3-VL-8B and SenseNova-SI-1.3-8B on three egocentric spatial reasoning benchmarks. Both students outperform their respective base models and reduce inference FLOPs and latency relative to the shared Dual-LoRA teacher system. In summary, our contributions are as follows: • We introduce EgoP 2C, a framework that internalizes a plan-perceive-compose process within a single VLM. The resulting model answers complex egocentric spatial reasoning questions by generating and answering subquestions without external tools. • We develop segment-level MOPD, assigning the Planner teacher to planning and composition segments, and the Perception teacher to observation segments within each student rollout. • On three spatial reasoning benchmarks, the Qwen3-VL-8B and SenseNova-SI-1.3-8B students consistently outperform their respective base models, with average gains of 9.2 and 7.5 percentage points, respectively. Both students also reduce inference FLOPs by 65.8-66.3% and achieve a 1.29 -1.49 × speedup over the shared Dual-LoRA teachers. 22 Related Work 

2.1 Egocentric Spatial Reasoning 

Egocentric spatial reasoning requires inferring object relations and scene geometry from partial observations captured by a moving camera (Yang et al., 2025a; Li et al., 2026b; Horton et al., 2026; Kulkarni and Fazli, 2026). Existing methods train VLMs on object relations (Chen et al., 2024), distance estimation (Cai et al., 2026b), depth understanding (Cai et al., 2025), and camera pose estimation (Cai et al., 2026a). Others represent geometry explicitly through textual maps of estimated object coordinates (Gholami et al., 2026) or descriptions of camera trajectories and scene structure (Hua et al., 2026). Spatial agents obtain evidence through visual programs (Marsili et al., 2025; Cho et al., 2026; Luo et al., 2026), vision and geometry tools (Chen et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026b; Dai et al., 2026), or world simulation (Zhu et al., 2026). Recent methods also transfer spatial knowledge through synthetic viewpoint changes (Zhang et al., 2026a), depth and camera geometry supervision (Tang et al., 2026), cognitive-map distillation (Li et al., 2026c), and supervision from tool interactions (Zhou et al., 2026). EgoP 2C focuses on integrating separately trained planning and perception capabilities into one VLM, with specialist teachers supervising the model’s own trajectories. 

2.2 Mixture-of-Experts Models 

Sparse MoE models increase parameter capacity while limiting computation by activating a subset of experts for each token (Fedus et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2024; Dai et al., 2024; Muennighoff et al., 2025). Sparse Upcycling (Komatsuzaki et al., 2023) constructs an MoE from a pretrained dense checkpoint by copying its feedforward layers into experts. Branch-Train-MiX (Sukhbaatar et al., 2024) and FlexOlmo (Shi et al., 2025) combine experts trained on different data domains, allowing specialization before integration. BAR (Morrison et al., 2026) extends modular expert training to post-training, combining domain experts through parameter merging and lightweight router training. In multimodal settings, MoSE (Xu et al., 2025) organizes experts around skills such as perception, prediction, and planning, using skill supervision to train the routers. To integrate separately trained capabilities, EgoP 2C initializes role-specific experts through supervision from specialist teachers. Each segment activates the expert assigned to its role. 

2.3 On-Policy Distillation 

Recent distillation methods improve language and vision-language models using teacher feedback on student-generated sequences (Gu et al., 2024; Ko et al., 2024, 2025; Yuan et al., 2026). In on-policy distillation (OPD), this feedback is computed on sequences sampled from the current student (Agarwal et al., 2024). MOPD (Ma et al., 2026) extends this approach to multiple specialist teachers, selecting one teacher for each student trajectory according to the task domain. Other methods adapt teacher supervision at the token or step level (Wang et al., 2026a; Xu et al., 2026). ViGOS (Wang et al., 2026b) supervises visual descriptions and subsequent reasoning with the same frozen model under different contexts. H-OPD (Yin et al., 2026) combines vision-language and text-only teachers through confidence-weighted token-level supervision. EgoP 2Cinstead assigns Planner and Perception teachers to their corresponding segments in the trajectories generated by the student, using their token-level feedback for segment-level MOPD. 

# 3 Method 

Given an input x=( v, q ), which consists of an egocentric video v and a spatial question q, the task is to predict the corresponding answer a. Under our protocol, the question is answered either directly or by following a plan-perceive-compose process. The Planner first selects a mode b ∈ { DIRECT , PLAN }. With DIRECT , the model answers the original question without requesting observations. With PLAN , the Planner requests spatial observations over one or more rounds before composing the answer. At round j, it generates a request uj containing one or more self-contained spatial subquestions. Perception answers each from the video, producing a list of textual observations oj . The Planner uses the accumulated requests and observations to either request further subquestions or compose the final answer. Perception receives only the video and its current subquestion. 3.1 Specialist Teachers 

The Dual-LoRA teachers share a pretrained VLM backbone, πB (Figure 2(a)). Its vision encoder, visual-language aligner, and base language parameters remain frozen. Separate LoRA adapters in the language model (Hu et al., 2022) define the Planner policy, πPC , for both planning and composition, and the Perception policy, πV. Direct answers use πB with both adapters disabled. Planner SFT covers the initial DIRECT /PLAN choice. For examples in PLAN mode, we also supervise subquestion generation and final answer composition. Perception SFT covers answers to those subquestions. For examples in DIRECT mode, we supervise only the Planner’s mode-selection token, excluding the model’s answer from the SFT loss. During the data construction process, a language model generates subquestions and answer composition without access to the correct final answer. Because an annotation for the original question does not necessarily provide answers to its subquestions, we obtain supervision for each subquestion from ground-truth answers when available or a strong perception model’s weak labels otherwise. The final answer is revealed only afterward to filter incorrect trajectories. Section 4.1 summarizes the data sources, generation models, and sample counts. After SFT, we apply GRPO in two stages, first to Perception and then to the Planner. Without ground-truth subquestion answers, Perception GRPO uses the change in the 4frozen Planner’s margin for the correct final option as a weak reward. Both this setting and Planner GRPO use original multiple-choice questions with known correct options.  

> Perception GRPO.

For each video and subquestion, the current Perception policy samples a group of answers. A verifier scores each against the ground-truth subquestion answer when available. Otherwise, we use unknown 

as a placeholder for a missing subquestion answer. Utility is the change in the frozen SFT-trained Planner’s margin for the correct final option when this placeholder is replaced by the sampled answer. The video and other observations remain fixed. This utility reward does not independently verify the subquestion answer. The Planner evaluates composition without route selection or further questions.  

> Planner GRPO.

We freeze the trained Perception teacher and optimize the Planner starting from its initial route choice. The frozen backbone answers under DIRECT , while the Planner queries Perception and composes the answer under PLAN . Final correctness rewards the initial choice in both branches and the Planner’s subsequent outputs in the interaction branch. Auxiliary terms measure improvement over direct answering by πB and penalize invalid outputs. Base and Perception answer tokens are excluded from Planner updates. Appendix B.1 defines the rewards and training masks. These teachers specialize in distinct roles, but their separate execution requires adapter switching and repeated context processing. We next transfer their capabilities to one student. 

3.2 MoE Initialization

Each role r ∈ { PC , V, G} has an expert Er for planning and composition, perception, or direct answering, respectively. These roles execute the direct or plan-perceive-compose workflow in Figure 2(d). P C and V also use role-specific attention residuals, while the shared backbone and EG remain frozen. The common video prefix uses the frozen base path and shares its KV cache across roles (Appendix B.3). We first train the P C and V experts and attention residuals separately. # 4 Experiments 

We evaluate EgoP 2C on egocentric spatial reasoning benchmarks and analyze inference efficiency, teacher specialization, and the effects of MoE initialization and segment-level MOPD. 

4.1 Experimental setup 

Implementation. The Planner and Perception teachers share the SenseNova-SI-1.3-Qwen3-VL-8B backbone (Cai et al., 2026b), with separate rank-16 LoRA modules in the language model. Students use Qwen3-VL-8B (Bai et al., 2025) or SenseNova-SI-1.3-Qwen3-VL-8B as the backbone, abbreviated as Qwen3-VL and SenseNova-SI, respectively. Both student models use 12 MoE layers and rank-16 attention residuals. Student SFT runs for three epochs in each of the training stages with a learning rate of 5 × 10 −5 and a batch size of 16. MOPD runs for a total of 600 updates with a learning rate of 3 × 10 −5 and 16 trajectories per update. We conduct training on eight NVIDIA H100 GPUs. Appendix B provides additional implementation details on model parameterization, training objectives, and configurations. 

Training Data. We construct training samples from Aria Digital Twin (ADT) (Pan et al., 2023), Ego-Exo4D (Grauman et al., 2024), VSI-590K (Yang et al., 2025b), SPAR-7M (Zhang et al., 2025), and Ego4D (Grauman et al., 2022). The videos used for model training are disjoint from those included in the evaluation benchmarks. For teacher SFT, the DeepSeek-V4-Pro API (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2026) generates subquestions and composes the final answer. Subquestion answers come from ground-truth annotations when available; otherwise, they come from the Qwen3.5-397B API (Qwen Team, 2026). Benchmarks. We evaluate EgoP 2C on three egocentric spatial reasoning benchmarks. VSI-Bench (Yang et al., 2025a) covers spatial relations, distance estimation, and route planning in indoor scenes. EgoProx (Li et al., 2026b) tests egocentric navigation and object interactions using spatial relations and action context. We use its single-answer multiple-choice tasks and exclude Chain of Actions, which requires structured action-sequence prediction. R3D-Bench (Horton et al., 2026) tests spatial reasoning from wearable video, including comparing and combining object distance and dimension measurements. Evaluation Settings. We sample videos at 1 fps with a maximum of 32 frames per input, and cap each model generation at 2,048 tokens. Dual-LoRA and EgoP 2C may ask at most six Perception subquestions per question. The same sampled frames are used within each controlled comparison. For the S-Agent variants, we keep the external tools, prompts, and execution settings fixed and vary only the agent.

To test whether additional QA training alone accounts for the reported performance gains, we train a SenseNova-SI baseline model with SFT + GRPO on the same original questions and subquestion QA data as the Dual-LoRA teacher. During the SFT stage, the baseline is trained only on answers and receives no supervision on intermediate planning and interaction sequences. GRPO uses both the Perception training samples and the original multiple-choice questions used for Planner GRPO. At inference time, the baseline answers directly, without generating subquestions. Appendix C.3 shows statistically significant performance gains for both Dual-LoRA and EgoP 2C SenseNova-SI over this baseline. EgoP 2C SenseNova-SI improves by 1.4, 6.5,and 4.4 points on VSI-Bench, EgoProx, and R3D-Bench, respectively. These results support that the value of plan-perceive-compose goes beyond directly learning answers from the same QA data.  

> Inference Efficiency.

We compare both EgoP 2C students with their shared SenseNova-SI Dual-LoRA teachers across all three benchmarks. All models use vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) with the same hardware, video sampling, resolution, batch size, and generation limits. Each system follows its own routing and stopping decisions. Measurements cover the complete interaction, including visual encoding and all generation steps. Table 2 shows that EgoP 2C Qwen3-VL and EgoP 2C SenseNova-SI reduce FLOPs by 66.3% and 65.8% and achieve 

1.29 × and 1.49 × speedups over Dual-LoRA, respectively. Both students generate fewer tokens and have lower end-to-end latency per generated token, despite making slightly more Perception calls (2.6 and 2.7 vs. 2.5 per question). Peak GPU memory increases from 19.4 GB for Dual-LoRA to 25.2 and 25.3 GB for the two students. The efficiency gains also persist when comparing subsets in which each student and Dual-LoRA generate the same number of subquestions for each original question (Appendix C.4).

# 5 Conclusion 

We presented EgoP 2C, a framework that learns planning and composition separately from perception and integrates these capabilities into one VLM for egocentric spatial reasoning. Dual-LoRA teachers guide the initialization of dedicated MoE experts. Segment-level MOPD then assigns each specialist to the corresponding segments of the student’s own interactions, supervising both the observations it produces and the reasoning that uses them. Experiments show consistent improvements over both general and spatially trained backbones. Future work will explore adaptive frame selection for longer videos and training objectives that balance answer accuracy with inference cost. 10 References

# A Training Data Generation 

Section 4.1 summarizes the data sources, generation models, and sample counts. Here, we describe how annotations and generated trajectories form role supervision, followed by the detailed data composition. 

A.1 Data Construction 

ADT (Pan et al., 2023) provides camera and object poses together with geometry and identity annotations, from which we derive answers to spatial subquestions about distance, direction, size, location, and identity. Ego-Exo4D (Grauman et al., 2024), VSI-590K (Yang et al., 2025b), and SPAR-7M (Zhang et al., 2025) supply original questions and final answers, including multiple-choice questions. Ego-Exo4D also provides annotated subjective questions, and Ego4D (Grauman et al., 2022) contributes additional Planner samples. Annotations for the original question support trajectory verification but do not independently verify every intermediate observation. We construct demonstrations for both DIRECT and PLAN . For DIRECT , the frozen teacher backbone answers the original video and question with no LoRA active. For PLAN , the planning model generates subquestions and receives observations before deciding whether to request further evidence or compose the final answer. Available subquestion annotations are inserted as answers to the corresponding subquestions. When an observation must be generated, the visual model receives only the video and subquestion, without the original answer options or other planning history. Generated observations without independent annotations serve as weak supervision. We compare each generated final answer with the task annotation and allow up to three retries for an incorrect 

PLAN trajectory. If the resulting PLAN answer is correct, we retain it as a PLAN example regardless of the frozen base’s answer. If PLAN remains incorrect but the frozen base answers correctly, we retain a DIRECT 

example. We discard examples for which both routes are incorrect. From retained PLAN trajectories, we extract subquestion-answer pairs for Perception and planning or composition targets with their preceding histories for the Planner. Appendix D describes the generation prompts and their conversion into training conversations.

B.1 Teacher Training 

Training order. Section 4.1 specifies the teacher backbone and adapter configuration. Both roles first undergo SFT. We supervise the Planner’s initial DIRECT /PLAN choice and, for PLAN , its subquestions and final composition. The frozen base’s direct answer is excluded from Planner supervision. During Perception GRPO, the current Perception policy samples a group of answers to each subquestion. Objective annotations provide verifier rewards where available. For answers without a ground-truth subquestion answer, the frozen SFT-trained Planner, denoted by ¯πPC , provides utility scores during training as detailed below. Only the Perception adapter is updated in this stage. After training Perception, we freeze the resulting policy and optimize the Planner through the complete interaction in Equation 14. Each rollout begins with the current Planner’s mode choice. A DIRECT rollout obtains its answer from the frozen base. Each role uses its own SFT policy as the GRPO KL reference. For DIRECT , the Planner loss and KL masks cover only its initial choice. For PLAN , they also cover its subquestions and composition. Context boundaries. Perception receives the video, the current self-contained subquestion, and the preceding tokens of its own answer. Completed-sequence scoring can process known target tokens in parallel, while the interaction itself remains autoregressive. Teacher adapters are activated separately for scoring, with a fresh decoder prefill for the corresponding role context. Shared frozen visual features are cached, but decoder KV states are not reused across teacher adapter changes. 

B.3 Inference 

Routing and interaction. The initial tool name determines the mode: answer_with_base selects DIRECT ,while ask_perception selects PLAN . For PLAN , the remainder of the initial request contains the first subquestion or subquestions. Both modes are available on every benchmark. The execution loop parses 

answer_with_base to activate G for direct answering, or ask_perception to activate V for the requested observations. Each request uj contains one or more <tool_call> blocks, each closed by </tool_call> . Each block contains an ask_perception call with one sub_question argument. Independent subquestions may be issued in the same request. A subquestion that depends on an earlier observation is generated in a subsequent Planner turn, with the required information included explicitly in its wording. After all calls in the request have completed, the subquestions and their corresponding observations are appended to the Planner’s history in request order. Execution then returns to PC, which can request further evidence or compose the final answer. No external teacher or spatial tool is called during student inference.

C.4 Additional Inference Efficiency Analysis 

For each student, we select the evaluation questions on which it and Dual-LoRA naturally generate the same number of subquestions. The Qwen3-VL and SenseNova-SI subsets contain 1,110 and 2,118 questions, respectively. Each subquestion corresponds to one Perception call. The Qwen3-VL and SenseNova-SI subsets average 2.3 and 2.1 Perception calls per question, respectively. Dual-LoRA costs are reported on the questions selected for each student. On the Qwen3-VL matched subset, Table 14 shows that the student reduces FLOPs by 68.5% and latency from 8.8 to 5.8 seconds, a 1.52 × speedup. It generates 16.7% fewer tokens, and end-to-end latency per generated token decreases from 29.5 to 23.3 ms. On the SenseNova-SI matched subset, the student reduces FLOPs by 68.2% and latency from 8.6 to 4.9 seconds, a 1.75 × speedup. It generates 21.1% fewer tokens, while latency per generated token decreases from 31.0 to 22.4 ms. Both students therefore retain an efficiency advantage when the number of subquestions is matched per original question.
