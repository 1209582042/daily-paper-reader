## ABSTRACT 

We study how vision-language models (VLMs) can learn to think and actively explore in 3D space. Instead of providing multi-view images, videos, or 3D rep-resentations to the model, we preserve the native visual encoding of VLMs, i.e., a single image, one at a time, just like humans, and treat the 3D representation only as an environment with which the model can interact through actions (e.g., move left) and observations (rendered images). Each rollout begins from an initial first-person view and a visual question. The model can then iteratively reason, navi-gate, and reorient its viewpoint before committing to a final answer. Following an initial trajectory-supervised finetuning, we apply reinforcement learning (RL) with rewards driven primarily by final-answer correctness. Crucially, RL provides no direct supervision over which viewpoints the model should visit or which ex-ploration actions it should take. Instead, the model must learn an effective explo-ration policy solely from the extent to which its interactions improve performance. Across six 3D benchmarks, our model ACT3D achieves an average accuracy of 53.2% that improves its base Qwen model by 22.0% and surpasses proprietary Gemini 3.6 Flash. ACT3D also outperforms existing open-source 3D methods while using 50-70% fewer input frames, and generalizes to unseen domains with an average improvement of 5.9% over its supervised initialization. Further anal-ysis reveals adaptive, question-conditioned exploration. Performance gains over the base model increase from 0.6% on questions answerable from the initial view to 16.3% on those requiring extensive exploration. ACT3D searches for occluded targets, avoids unnecessary exploration, and acquires complementary viewpoints for spatial comparisons. These findings demonstrate that outcome-based RL en-ables VLMs not only to reason over visual evidence, but also to learn when and how to acquire it. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Despite substantial progress in 3D vision-language models, much of the existing work studies spatial reasoning under observations that are specified a priori , providing models with complete RGB-D scans (Azuma et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2025), multi-view images (Yang et al., 2025), videos (Zheng et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026a), or explicit 3D scene representations such as point clouds and meshes (Hong et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2024b). Yet under partial observability, a fundamental challenge remains: the model must not only reason over the evidence currently available, but also determine how to act in order to acquire the evidence that is missing. This motivates the central question of our work: can a vision-language model learn to actively explore and acquire task-relevant visual evidence as an integral part of the 3D reasoning process? 

We address this question by first formulating reasoning in 3D as an interactive visual decision pro-cess under partial observability. Rather than providing the VLM with a complete 3D representation, the 3D scene is kept external to the VLM and serves as an interactive environment that the model accesses through sequential action-observation interactions. Starting from a fixed initial egocentric view and a visual question, the model alternates between reasoning and interaction, iteratively ac-quiring observations through navigation and viewpoint control before committing to a final answer. ## 2 RELATED WORK 

Prior work on 3D vision-language models operates over observations assumed to be complete and directly presented to the model, including 3D representations such as point clouds and object-centric features (Hong et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2024a;b; Jin et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024b; Fu et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024c; Kang et al., 2025), fixed multi-view images or videos (Zhu et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025; Qi et al., 2025), and representations enhanced through reconstruction supervision (Wang et al., 2025a). A parallel line of work improves spatial reasoning in general-purpose VLMs through large-scale spatial supervision (Chen et al., 2024a; Yang et al., 2026a) and reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (Ouyang et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a). Despite advances in spatial reasoning, these approaches largely assume that the visual evidence required to answer a question is provided to the model, rather than actively acquired during inference. In contrast, Act3D keeps the 3D scene representation external to the VLM as an interactive environment: the model receives only egocentric 2D observations and learns to navigate and acquire task-relevant visual evidence through interaction, jointly with reasoning. 

Multimodal agentic reasoning. 

Reinforcement learning (RL) (e.g., GRPO (Shao et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026b)) has emerged as an effective paradigm for improving reasoning capabilities (Guo et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026b; Yu et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026b; Li et al., 2026c). It has further been applied to agentic systems with multi-turn interaction and tool use (Jiang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026d; Wang et al., 2025c; Jin et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025), as well as to multimodal reasoning (Feng et al., 2025a;b; Li et al., 2026b; 2024; Huang et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2025). These methods primarily manipulate or revisit visual evidence already contained in the provided input. In contrast, our model learns to explore the 3D environment, actively acquiring new visual evidence while jointly optimizing reasoning and interaction in a single rollout. 

Exploration in embodied and 3D environments. EmbodiedQA introduced navigation-based ques-tion answering (Das et al., 2017), later extended to open-vocabulary evaluation by OpenEQA (Ma-jumdar et al., 2024). Recent benchmarks further show that active observation can outperform passive viewing and expose failures in spatial exploration and long-horizon planning (Hong et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026c;a; Wei et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a). We abstract embodiment to direct camera control over a scanned 3D scene and jointly learn exploration and reasoning for question answering. More closely related are methods that actively select viewpoints. Chain-of-View per-forms training-free coarse-to-fine view selection through a discrete camera-action interface (Zhao et al., 2026a). Think3D equips models with 3D reconstruction tools and optimizes their use with RL (Zhang et al., 2026b). ViewSuite distills self-exploration into a view graph (Wang et al., 2026a). 

## 3 METHOD 

We study how a VLM can learn to reason in 3D under partial observability. Learning such a policy poses two complementary challenges. The model must first acquire a basic interaction competence in 3D environments before it can learn from the interaction with environments despite the absence of ground-truth exploration trajectories. We therefore adopt a two-stage training procedure. We first perform trajectory-supervised fine-tuning using curated interaction demonstrations to initialize a coherent reason–act–observe policy. We then apply reinforcement learning with verifiable reward 

to optimize the policy with rewards driven only by final performance, without supervision of any intermediate viewpoints or actions. This allows the model to learn task-directed exploration end-to-end, supervised only by how much the evidence it acquires improves its answers. 3.1 REASONING IN 3D UNDER PARTIAL OBSERVABILITY 

Problem formulation. We formalize reasoning in 3D as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP) (Kaelbling et al., 1998). A task instance w = ( M, q, y ⋆) ∼ D consists of a reconstructed 3D scene M, a question q, and the ground-truth answer y⋆ = g(M, q ) ∈ Y . At step 

t, the environment state is st = ( M, c t), where ct = ( et, f t) specifies the camera position et ∈ R3

and viewing direction ft ∈ S2, and c0 is a fixed initial viewpoint of the scene. We write ˆz for the vertical axis of the world frame; the camera up vector is held parallel to ˆz, so the camera carries no roll and ct has five degrees of freedom. The scene M remains fixed and hidden throughout the 3Preprint episode, while the camera state evolves under the model’s actions. The policy observes the rendered egocentric view ot = Render( M, c t) and, after each action, the resulting camera configuration ct.

## 4 EXPERIMENT 

4.1 SETUP 

Models and training. 

All experiments use Qwen3.5-9B (Qwen Team, 2026) as the base model. We consider two training configurations: ScanQA-only and mixed-data. In the ScanQA-only setting, SFT uses 2,550 teacher trajectories, followed by GRPO on 25,563 ScanQA questions. In the mixed-data setting, SFT uses 5,712 trajectories from ScanQA (Azuma et al., 2022), MP3D (Chang et al., 2017), and ESI (Hong et al., 2026), while GRPO is performed on 51,543 questions from ScanQA, MP3D, and curated QA data from ScanNet and ARKitScenes (Baruch et al., 2021). Both settings follow the same two-stage training procedure, and we evaluate base model, SFT initialization, and RL-trained policy to isolate the contribution of each training stage. For SFT, we train for two epochs with a batch size of 16 and a learning rate of 10 −5. GRPO uses 64 prompts per step, a mini-batch size of 16, and G = 8 rollouts per prompt, with a learning rate of 2 × 10 −6, sampling temperature of 0.7, clipping parameter ϵ = 0 .2, and KL coefficient 

β = 0 .005 , without entropy regularization. We set (λjudge , λ EM , λ fmt , λ int ) = (0 .3, 0.2, 0.2, 0.05) 

and Nmax = 12 . A correct rollout that observes the annotated answer object receives the maximum interaction reward of λint Nmax = 0 .60 , whereas one that does not observe the object accumulates the reward per interaction, up to the same limit. Training is conducted on NVIDIA H200 GPUs with asynchronous rollouts using vLLM, a context length of 24 ,576 tokens, and a maximum of twelve interaction steps per episode. Prompt and environment-observation tokens are excluded from the training loss. Full prompts are provided in Appendices C.1 and C.2. 

Evaluation. We evaluate all models under a unified interactive protocol. Each model is initial-ized with a single 640 × 480 render of the scene and is allowed up to twelve interactions to ac-quire additional observations; all active baselines are evaluated using the same interaction harness and budget. We use Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct (Qwen Team, 2025) as the answer judge through-out evaluation. We evaluate on six benchmarks spanning diverse indoor environments and spatial reasoning settings: SPBench-SI and SPBench-MV (Li et al., 2026a), ARKitScenes (Baruch et al., 2021), ScanQA (Azuma et al., 2022), SQA3D (Ma et al., 2023), and MP3D (Chang et al., 2017). Together, these benchmarks cover settings ranging from single-room visual question answering to multi-scene spatial reasoning. We compare Act3D with a broad range of existing 3D reasoning methods and general-purpose VLMs. For passive perception, we include Video-R1 (Feng et al., 2025a), ViLaSR (Wu et al., 2026), SpatialLadder (Li et al., 2026a), SpaceR (Ouyang et al., 2025), and Ross3D (Wang et al., 2025a), alongside InternVL3.5-38B (Wang et al., 2025b) evaluated with fixed visual observations. For ac-tive interaction, we evaluate open-source VLMs, including Qwen2.5-VL-72B (Bai et al., 2025), Qwen3.5-9B/27B (Qwen Team, 2026), and InternVL3.5-38B, as well as the proprietary Gemini 3.6 Flash (Google DeepMind, 2026). These models are evaluated under the same observation–action interface and interaction budget, whereas passive methods retain their respective input and inference protocols. We additionally report the base model and SFT checkpoints to isolate the contributions of different training stages. 4.2 MAIN RESULTS

It further outperforms Gemini 3.6 Flash by 1.0% under the same interaction protocol, as well as all evaluated open-source 3D reasoning methods, including both active interaction and passive perception approaches. ## 5 CONCLUSION 

We presented Act3D, a framework for learning to think and explore in the 3D world by treating 3D representations as interactive environments rather than assumed complete inputs. Starting from a single observation, Act3D iteratively reasons, acts, and acquires visual evidence through interaction with the environment. Building on supervised trajectory initialization, verifiable RL further opti-mizes the policy without explicit supervision of exploration actions or viewpoints. Across six 3D benchmarks, Act3D achieves the highest average accuracy of 53.2%, improving upon its SFT initial-ization by 4.6% and outperforming existing active and passive 3D reasoning methods. Further anal-yses show that these gains extend beyond the training benchmark and are particularly pronounced on questions requiring more extensive exploration. Qualitative results also reveal question-conditioned exploration strategies that adapt to newly acquired observations. Together, these findings demon-strate the potential of outcome-based RL to learn not only how to reason over visual evidence, but also how to actively acquire the evidence needed for 3D reasoning.

We use the notation of Sec. 3.1. To characterize whether the observations acquired so far are suffi-cient to determine the answer, we define the consistency set         

> K(ht) = M′: Render( M′, c s) = os,∀s≤t,

(12) where c0, . . . , c t is the camera path induced by the actions in ht. K(ht) contains all scenes indistin-guishable from the true scene under the interaction history ht. We say that q is answerable from ht

when all scenes consistent with the acquired observations induce the same answer:      

> g(M′, q ) = g(M′′ , q ),∀ M ′,M′′ ∈ K (ht).

(13) Because the environment is deterministic, each additional observation can only further constrain the set of compatible scenes,   

> K(ht+1 )⊆ K (ht),

(14) and can therefore reduce uncertainty about the answer. Correspondingly, treating the interaction history as a random variable, conditioning on additional observations cannot increase the expected uncertainty about the answer:        

> H(y⋆|ht+1 )≤H(y⋆|ht).

(15) Unlike the set inclusion, which holds along every trajectory, this inequality holds only in expectation over histories: a particular observation may leave the uncertainty unchanged or even increase it. Successful interaction therefore requires selecting viewpoints that are informative for the current question. This formulation exposes a fundamental limitation of passive perception. A model restricted to the initial observation o0 cannot recover task-relevant information absent from that view. Its Bayes-optimal passive accuracy is
