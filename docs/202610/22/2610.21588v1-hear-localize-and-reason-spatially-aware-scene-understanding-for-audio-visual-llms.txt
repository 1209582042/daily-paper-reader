Abstract
Audio-visual large language models (AV-LLMs) have made strong progress in
multimodal understanding, yet most treat audio as monaural semantic content,
limiting their ability to reason about where sounds originate and how they relate to
the visual scene. Recent spatial audio-visual studies address parts of this problem,
but often focus on specific abilities, such as spatial correspondence or direction and
distance reasoning. In this work, we introduce HLR-AVSceneQA, a comprehensive
benchmark with a hear-localize-reason hierarchy that evaluates whether models can
recognize what is heard, localize sound sources in egocentric and allocentric views,
and use these localized sources to reason about how they relate to the visual scene.
We further introduce HLR-LLM, which augments a strong audio-visual foundation
model with a binaural spatial audio branch alongside its monaural semantic audio
pathway. We train HLR-LLM with a curriculum that incorporates final-stage chain-
of-thought supervision for multi-step spatial reasoning. Experiments show that
existing AV-LLMs remain limited in spatial grounding and relational reasoning,
while HLR-LLM substantially improves spatial audio-visual scene understanding
without compromising semantic audio-visual perception. Code and benchmark
data are available at https://hlrllm.github.io/.

Introduction
Spatial audio and vision provide complementary cues for understanding a scene. We may hear
someone calling from another room, infer that a ringing alarm clock is hidden below the bed by
combining its direction with the visible layout, or decide where to move to get closer to a sound by
relating auditory distance cues to nearby objects. Such everyday situations show that audio-visual
scene understanding requires more than recognizing a sound event or matching it to a visible object.
Accordingly, a spatially aware agent must infer where the sound originates, whether it is visible, why
it may be hidden, and how its relation to the scene changes as the agent moves.
Recently, audio-visual large language models (AV-LLMs) [1, 2, 3, 4, 5] have shown strong progress
in diverse multimodal understanding tasks, such as audio-visual question answering [6, 7], temporal
grounding [8], and multimodal reasoning. However, most existing AV-LLMs treat audio primarily as
monaural semantic content, focusing on what is heard rather than where it is heard. As a result, they
struggle to reason about auditory sources in the physical scene, especially when a source is outside the
camera view, occluded by objects, located in another room, or spatially ambiguous among multiple
candidates. Spatial audio, such as binaural or multi-channel recordings, offers the direction and
distance cues needed to overcome this limitation and support spatially grounded scene understanding.
Despite this growing need for spatially grounded audio-visual understanding, two key challenges
remain. First, existing spatial audio-(visual) benchmarks typically focus on specific subproblems,
such as audio-only [9, 10, 11] or speech-centric settings [12], egocentric or allocentric direction and
distance reasoning [13], or precise RGB-D-based 3D grounding [14]. Existing evaluations therefore
remain fragmented across modalities, assumptions, and task formats, making it difficult to assess a
single AV-LLM’s ability to jointly handle semantic understanding, spatial grounding, and relational
reasoning. Second, although spatial audio-visual tasks require multi-step reasoning, many current
training settings rely on direct-answer supervision [15, 14]. This gives models insufficient structure
for decomposing complex spatial queries into interpretable intermediate steps that connect auditory
semantics, spatial cues, and visual context.
In this work, we introduce HLR-AVSceneQA, a comprehensive benchmark for spatial audio-visual
scene understanding. As shown in Fig. 1, HLR-AVSceneQA follows a hear-localize-reason hierarchy:
spatial hearing evaluates what is heard, spatial grounding evaluates source localization in egocentric
and allocentric views, and spatial relational reasoning evaluates how localized sound sources relate to
the visual scene. By treating localized sound-source information not only as a prediction target but also
as spatial evidence for downstream reasoning, HLR-AVSceneQA enables systematic evaluation of
spatial grounding and scene-level reasoning. Unlike panoramic-input settings [15], HLR-AVSceneQA
uses normal-field-of-view observations, requiring models to reason about visible, occluded, and
off-screen sources from partial visual evidence. We construct the benchmark with a simulator-based
pipeline [16], producing diverse, controllable spatial audio-visual scenes with reliable annotations.
We further introduce HLR-LLM, a spatially aware AV-LLM that extends audio-visual semantic
understanding with explicit spatial audio modeling. Built on an existing AV-LLM [4] and augmented
with a spatial audio branch, HLR-LLM improves spatial grounding and reasoning while preserving
strong semantic perception. To address the multi-step nature of spatial audio-visual reasoning, we
incorporate chain-of-thought [17, 18] supervision in the final stage of curriculum training, encouraging
the model to connect auditory, spatial, and visual cues rather than directly predicting an answer.
Experiments show that existing AV-LLMs, including commercial models that rely on monaural audio,
struggle with spatial grounding and reasoning in our benchmark. In contrast, HLR-LLM preserves
strong semantic hearing ability, including sound event and speech recognition, while substantially
improving spatial grounding and relational reasoning. Transfer experiments on real-world spatial
audio-visual recordings [19] further suggest that pretraining on HLR-AVSceneQA provides useful
spatial priors for adaptation to real-world data. Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We introduce HLR-AVSceneQA, a comprehensive benchmark that evaluates spatial hearing and
grounding both directly and as intermediate evidence for downstream spatial relational reasoning.
• We propose HLR-LLM, a spatially aware AV-LLM that augments a strong foundation model with
a spatial audio pathway to improve grounding and reasoning while preserving semantic perception.
• We demonstrate that final-stage chain-of-thought curriculum training improves multi-step spatial
reasoning by connecting auditory, spatial, and visual cues through intermediate reasoning steps.

Related work
Spatial audio understanding. Spatial audio provides geometric cues about sound sources beyond
monaural semantic content. While monaural audio understanding mainly targets semantic tasks,
such as automatic speech recognition [20, 21], sound event recognition [22, 23, 24], and audio
captioning [25, 26, 27], spatial audio formats, such as binaural audio and first-order ambisonics
(FOA), capture direction- and distance-related cues. Early work on spatial audio explores sound event
localization and detection (SELD), where models jointly identify active sound events and estimate the
direction of arrival [28, 29, 30]. More recent work connects spatial audio with language models for
sound localization and reasoning [9, 10, 31], learns spatially aware audio-text representations through
contrastive objectives [11, 32], and adapts FOA inputs for far-field speech understanding [12, 33].
These works highlight the value of spatial hearing, but remain largely audio-only or speech-centric,
leaving open how spatial auditory cues should be grounded in and reasoned over a visual scene.
Spatial audio-visual benchmarks and tasks. General audio-visual benchmarks [34, 35, 36, 37, 38]
evaluate broad multimodal abilities, but do not focus on spatial sound-source understanding. Recent
benchmarks have begun to address specific spatial capabilities: SAVVY-Bench [13] evaluates dynamic
egocentric/allocentric direction and distance reasoning, SpatialSceneQA [14] focuses on RGB-D
and FOA-based 3D grounding, and Hear You Are [15] studies sound-object correspondence with
panoramic images. As summarized in Table 1, these benchmarks cover complementary aspects of
spatial audio-visual understanding, but focus on different subsets of capabilities. HLR-AVSceneQA
instead provides a unified hear-localize-reason evaluation that spans spatial hearing, grounding,
multi-source scenes, and scene-level relational reasoning, including whether localized sound-source
information can support broader reasoning about the surrounding scene. It further uses normal-field-
of-view egocentric observations, requiring models to reason about visible, off-screen, and hidden
sound sources from partial visual observations.
Spatial audio-visual large language models (AV-LLMs). General AV-LLMs [1, 2, 3, 4, 5] support
video understanding, temporal localization, and audio-visual question answering, but mostly rely
on monaural audio. Spatially aware AV-LLMs add spatial auditory cues to visual inputs. Hear You
Are LLM [15] uses spatial audio and panoramic images, SAVVY [13] builds dynamic spatial maps
with a training-free pipeline, and JAEGER [14] integrates RGB-D observations with FOA audio for
3D grounding and reasoning. While promising, these systems are often tailored to specific tasks,
such as direction/distance QA or precise 3D grounding, and do not fully inherit the broad semantic
and reasoning abilities of general AV-LLMs. They are also commonly trained with direct-answer
supervision despite the multi-step nature of spatial audio-visual reasoning [15, 14]. HLR-LLM
addresses these gaps by extending a strong AV-LLM with a spatial audio branch and chain-of-thought
supervision [17, 18], improving spatial reasoning while preserving general audio-visual capabilities.

3.1
Stage I: Spatial audio-visual scene synthesis
We synthesize each sample in an indoor 3D scene from an agent’s egocentric viewpoint, where the
agent pose defines both the RGB camera and the acoustic receiver. We populate the scene with diverse
candidate objects and designate up to two objects as active sound sources. Each sample includes a
synchronized RGB observation, binaural audio, object layouts, source assignments, and geometric
metadata for QA generation. Examples of rendered scenes and metadata are shown in Fig. 2.
Scene and acoustic simulator. We use SoundSpaces 2.0 [16], which renders geometry-aware
room acoustics and spatializes dry monaural clips into binaural audio. For 3D environments, we
use HM3DSem [39], which provides 216 indoor spaces and 3,100 rooms with dense object- and
room-level semantics. These semantics support realistic rendering, layout-aware QA generation, and
filtering of physically implausible placements. We use 145 scenes for training and 36 for testing.
Audio source curation. We use non-speech sounds from VGGSound [40], which contains over
200,000 in-the-wild clips across 310+ classes. Since some classes, such as sea waves or people
cheering, are not naturally grounded to a single indoor object, we manually exclude them. We
further use ImageBind-based text-audio similarity to filter out likely mislabeled clips [41]. For speech
audio, we use LibriSpeech [42], which provides clean utterance-level transcripts for far-field speech
recognition. We follow the official train/test splits of both VGGSound and LibriSpeech.
Visual object curation. We build a 3D mesh pool of sounding objects to insert into scenes. We collect
available meshes from Objaverse-XL [43], and supplement categories with insufficient coverage

Spatial audio-visual scene assembly and structured metadata. Given a room from HM3DSem,
we insert 2 to 6 candidate object meshes based on available free space and room scale. We activate up
to two sound sources and attach them to selected objects. Some audio-object pairs are semantically
matched, while others are intentionally spatially aligned but semantically mismatched. We further
include sources that are fully visible, occluded by an object, placed in an adjacent room, or outside
the agent’s field of view, introducing realistic ambiguity and preventing reliance on category matching
alone. The final RGB image is rendered from the agent viewpoint with a 90◦horizontal field of view
at 1280×720 resolution, paired with binaural audio. For each sample, we export structured metadata,
including agent pose, object identities and 3D coordinates, source-object assignments, visibility state,
occlusion type, and pairwise geometry, to generate QA pairs with accurate labels.
3.2
Stage II: Benchmark task design and QA construction
Given each rendered scene and its metadata, we generate QA pairs for three task families. To
improve linguistic diversity while keeping evaluation stable, we vary question templates but preserve
controlled answer formats. Most tasks use constrained outputs, such as multiple choice or discretized
bins, while speech transcription and scene captioning are open-ended. Representative examples are
shown in Table 2, and additional construction and quality-control details are provided in Appendix C.
Task family 1: Spatial hearing. This family evaluates semantic perception from spatial audio
through sound event recognition and far-field speech recognition, which identify audible event
categories and transcribe spoken content as the foundation for grounding and reasoning.
Task family 2: Spatial grounding. This family evaluates sound-source localization in egocentric
and allocentric frames, each with direction and distance estimation. Egocentric grounding localizes
the source relative to the agent’s current viewpoint, while allocentric grounding localizes it from a
transformed viewpoint, defined by standing at a reference object and facing another object. This tests
both source localization and viewpoint-aware spatial reasoning.
Task family 3: Spatial relational reasoning. This family extends source localization to scene-level
reasoning by evaluating how localized sound sources relate to the visual scene. Visibility asks whether
the source is visible from the current view, and correspondence asks which visible object is spatially
aligned with the sound, where the answer may be semantically mismatched or None. Hidden-source
localization identifies whether a non-visible source is hidden behind an object, in an adjacent room,
or outside the field of view. Loudness change asks whether the source becomes louder or quieter
after the agent moves to a target object; proximity reasoning asks which candidate object is nearest to
the source; and scene captioning summarizes sound events, directions, and visibility states, including
multiple emitters when present.
Chain-of-thought supervision. We further provide chain-of-thought (CoT) answer variants for
allocentric grounding and spatial relational reasoning. The CoT rationales are deterministically
generated from structured simulator metadata using task-specific templates, while GPT-4 [46] is

4
Method
We introduce HLR-LLM for spatial audio-visual scene understanding. Our goal is to enable spatial
audio-visual grounding and reasoning with binaural spatial cues while retaining the semantic percep-
tion ability of a strong base AV-LLM. To this end, HLR-LLM introduces a spatial audio pathway to
the base AV-LLM, where a spatial audio encoder and projector provide binaural spatial cues alongside
visual context and monaural semantic audio. We train the model with a curriculum that incorporates
chain-of-thought supervision to guide multi-step spatial reasoning.
4.1
HLR-LLM architecture
Figure 3 (a) shows the architecture of HLR-LLM. The model consists of the base visual and monaural
audio encoders, a newly introduced spatial audio encoder, a lightweight spatial audio projector, and
the language model. The monaural audio branch preserves semantic hearing abilities, such as sound
event recognition and speech understanding, while the spatial audio branch provides direction- and
distance-sensitive cues for grounding and reasoning. The language model integrates visual, monaural
audio, spatial audio, and text tokens to generate task-specific responses.
Spatial audio tokenization. Given a binaural audio signal b ∈R2×T , we encode it with a pretrained
spatial audio encoder fspa [9] and map the resulting features into the language model embedding
space using a trainable MLP projector pspa: Zspa = pspa(fspa(b)). The projected embeddings Zspa
serve as spatial audio tokens that provide acoustic localization cues to the LLM.
Integration with the AV-LLM. We build HLR-LLM on Qwen2.5-Omni [4], which supports text,
image, and audio inputs. We introduce a new media token, <spatial_audio>, whose placeholder
positions are replaced with the projected spatial audio tokens Zspa. The resulting multimodal input
sequence is X = [E(q), Zvis, Zaud, Zspa], where E(q) denotes text embeddings, Zvis visual tokens,
Zaud monaural audio tokens, and Zspa spatial audio tokens. The language model generates the answer
y conditioned on this sequence, i.e., p(y | X). This design preserves the base model’s semantic
audio-visual ability while explicitly injecting spatial acoustic cues.
4.2
Curriculum training for spatial audio-visual reasoning
Spatial audio-visual scene understanding requires low-level perception of what is heard and where
it comes from, as well as high-level spatial reasoning over the scene. Figure 3 (b) summarizes the
6


three-stage curriculum used to train HLR-LLM, which progresses from spatial audio alignment to
spatial audio-visual instruction tuning and final-stage CoT tuning.
Stage 1: Spatial audio alignment. We first pretrain the spatial audio projector using a spatial audio-
only QA dataset [9]. This aligns frozen spatial audio encoder features with the LLM embedding
space and initializes the model to verbalize spatial audio properties, such as sound event and location.
Stage 2: Spatial audio-visual instruction tuning. We then fine-tune the model on the training
split of HLR-AVSceneQA. This stage teaches the model to jointly use visual tokens, monaural audio
tokens, and spatial audio tokens across spatial hearing, grounding, and relational reasoning tasks.
For example, recognizing a ringing sound can rely on the monaural audio branch, while determining
whether the source is visible or hidden requires combining spatial cues with visual evidence.
Stage 3: Chain-of-thought tuning. Finally, we continue instruction tuning with CoT-formatted
answers for multi-step spatial tasks. The model learns to produce a compact rationale that connects
auditory cues, spatial estimates, and visual evidence before giving a normalized final answer. This
encourages the model to decompose spatial audio-visual queries into interpretable reasoning steps
rather than relying on direct answer prediction.
Training objective. At each stage, we minimize the standard auto-regressive cross-entropy loss over
the target answer tokens. Stage 1 updates only the spatial audio projector, while Stages 2–3 update
the spatial audio projector and LoRA parameters of the language model. All visual, monaural audio,
and spatial audio encoders remain frozen throughout training.
4.3
Implementation details
We follow the audio preprocessing of BAT [9]. Each source clip is loudness-normalized and convolved
with the room impulse response generated by SoundSpaces to produce binaural audio. The resulting
two-channel waveform is sampled at 32 kHz. We use a window size of 1024, a hop size of 320, and
128 mel bins to compute spectrogram features. The spatial audio branch receives the binaural signal,
while the monaural audio branch receives its channel-averaged version. For visual and monaural
audio inputs, we use the default preprocessing of Qwen2.5-Omni without modification. For LoRA
fine-tuning, we use rank 16, alpha 16, dropout 0.05, a learning rate of 2 × 10−4, and weight decay of
0.01. We train for 2 epochs on four NVIDIA A6000 GPUs with a per-device batch size of 4.
5
Experiment
5.1
Experimental setup
Datasets and metrics. We primarily evaluate models on HLR-AVSceneQA for comprehensive
spatial audio-visual scene understanding. Except for far-field ASR, which is evaluated by word
error rate (WER), and scene captioning, which is evaluated with CIDEr-D [47], METEOR [48], and
SPIDEr [49], all other tasks are evaluated using accuracy. We also construct QA formats on the
STARSS23 [19] dataset to evaluate adaptation to real-world spatial audio-visual recordings, covering
sound event recognition, egocentric direction and distance prediction, and visibility prediction. We
restrict the evaluation to these tasks because STARSS23 does not provide the object- and scene-level
annotations required to reliably define hidden-source, allocentric, and other relational reasoning tasks.
All fine-tuned models are trained on the corresponding training split and evaluated on the test split.
Unless otherwise noted, we report mean ± standard deviation over three runs.
Competing methods. We compare HLR-LLM with both zero-shot and fine-tuned audio-visual
models. For zero-shot evaluation, we consider several open-source AV-LLMs [1, 2, 3, 4, 5] and
commercial models [50, 51], which mainly use visual input and monaural audio. We also compare
with SAVVY [13], which uses multichannel audio but focuses on spatial grounding tasks. For fine-
tuned baselines, we initialize Hear You Are LLM [15] and Qwen2.5-Omni [4] from their respective
public checkpoints and fine-tune them on the HLR-AVSceneQA training split. Although Hear You
Are LLM is originally trained with panoramic images, we adapt it to the same normal-FOV inputs and
HLR-AVSceneQA supervision by fine-tuning its visual pathway, projectors/adapters, and LLM while
keeping the audio encoder frozen. We additionally fine-tune a dual-channel variant of Qwen2.5-Omni,
which separately encodes the left and right binaural channels through the original audio pathway and
feeds both audio token streams to the LLM without an explicit spatial audio branch.

5.2
Main results on HLR-AVSceneQA
Tables 3 and 4 summarize the main results on HLR-AVSceneQA. In Table 3, zero-shot AV-LLMs
and commercial models show reasonable spatial hearing ability, but perform poorly on direction
and distance grounding, indicating that monaural audio with visual input is insufficient for spatial
localization. Baselines with spatial audio improve some grounding tasks, with SAVVY [13] per-
forming well on egocentric direction and Hear You Are LLM improving over zero-shot models,
but they remain limited in broader grounding and reasoning. Fine-tuning Qwen2.5-Omni, our base
model, substantially improves sound-event recognition and spatial grounding, yet accurate local-
ization remains challenging without explicit binaural cues. Dual-channel Qwen2.5-Omni further
improves localization by processing left and right channels separately through the original audio
pathway, confirming the value of binaural spatial cues. HLR-LLM outperforms this baseline across
all egocentric and allocentric grounding metrics, demonstrating the benefit of explicitly modeling
binaural spatial information rather than exposing the LLM to separate left- and right-channel tokens.
A similar trend appears in Table 4. Zero-shot models perform reasonably well on visibility, which
can often be inferred from visual evidence, but struggle with tasks requiring spatial alignment and
relational reasoning. Fine-tuned baselines improve substantially, highlighting the importance of task-
specific learning for spatial audio-visual understanding. HLR-LLM achieves the best performance
across all spatial relational reasoning tasks and consistently outperforms the dual-channel Qwen2.5-
Omni baseline, further supporting the benefit of explicit binaural modeling for scene-level reasoning.

5.3
Ablation study
Table 5 examines the contribution of each modality and training strategy. Removing vision (a) still
retains egocentric direction and distance performance, since binaural audio provides direct spatial
cues. However, it substantially reduces allocentric grounding and proximity reasoning, where visual
object locations are required. Interestingly, the model without vision still performs relatively well on
hidden-source reasoning, suggesting that binaural cues provide useful evidence for distinguishing
non-visible source configurations. The visual-only model (b) performs poorly across most tasks,
showing that vision alone is insufficient for reliable sound-source localization, especially when
sources are off-screen or hidden. Removing binaural audio (c) also causes a large drop in grounding
tasks, showing that monaural audio lacks the spatial cues needed for accurate localization. At the
same time, its relatively stronger proximity performance suggests that visual context and semantic
audio can still support some object-level relational reasoning.
The training ablation shows how each stage contributes to spatial reasoning. Stage-2 training (d),
which includes Stages 1–2 but omits CoT tuning, already provides strong performance. Joint training
with CoT (e), which mixes Stage-2 instruction data with CoT-formatted data, improves egocentric
grounding, correspondence, and hidden-source reasoning, but does not consistently improve all
grounding tasks. In contrast, our final model (f), which applies CoT tuning after Stage-2 instruction
tuning, performs best across all grounding metrics and most relational reasoning tasks, with clear
gains in allocentric grounding, correspondence, and proximity. These gains are most evident on tasks
requiring multi-step spatial reasoning, such as transforming viewpoints or comparing object–source
relations. Overall, the ablation indicates that binaural audio supports spatial localization, vision
supports object-level grounding, and final-stage CoT tuning improves complex spatial reasoning.

6
Conclusion and discussion
We introduce HLR-AVSceneQA, a comprehensive benchmark for spatial audio-visual scene under-
standing organized around a hear-localize-reason hierarchy. By covering spatial hearing, egocentric
and allocentric grounding, and spatial relational reasoning, HLR-AVSceneQA evaluates whether
AV-LLMs can move beyond recognizing audio-visual content to connect what is heard, where it
originates, and how the source relates to the surrounding scene. We also propose HLR-LLM, which
augments a strong AV-LLM with a binaural spatial audio branch and a staged curriculum with
final-stage CoT supervision, improving spatial reasoning while retaining strong semantic audio-visual
perception. Real-world adaptation experiments on STARSS23 further show that HLR-AVSceneQA
pretraining provides useful spatial priors for downstream adaptation. These results highlight the
importance of explicit spatial audio modeling and structured training for spatially aware AV-LLMs.
Our work also has limitations. HLR-AVSceneQA is built with a simulator-based pipeline, which
provides controllable annotations but cannot fully capture the acoustic complexity and visual diversity
of the real world. The benchmark focuses on indoor scenes, leaving dynamic agents, moving sound
sources, and richer embodied interaction for future work. Future research should extend spatial
audio-visual benchmarks to more realistic and dynamic settings and develop AV-LLMs that can
actively perceive, move, and reason in spatial environments.

A.1
Implementation details
HLR-LLM is built on Qwen2.5-Omni with an additional binaural spatial audio pathway. For the
spatial branch, we use a pretrained Spatial-AST encoder following BAT [9]. Spatial-AST produces
audio tokens together with class tokens trained for semantic classification, direction prediction, and
distance prediction. We use these tokens as spatial audio features and map them into the language
embedding space with a lightweight projector. The projector is an MLP with a linear–GELU–linear
structure, which has been shown to be effective for extending LLMs to multimodal inputs [52, 53].
Training follows the three-stage curriculum described in the main paper. In Stage 1, we train only the
spatial alignment modules, including the spatial audio resampler and projector, while freezing the
language model, visual encoder, monaural audio encoder, and spatial audio encoder. In Stages 2–3,
we LoRA-finetune the language model and continue updating the spatial alignment modules while
keeping the encoders frozen. We apply LoRA to all linear layers with rank 16, alpha 16, and dropout
0.05, and use LoRA+ with a larger learning-rate ratio for the LoRA B matrix. We train with bfloat16
precision, FlashAttention, learning rate 2×10−4, warmup ratio 0.05, weight decay 0.01, and gradient
clipping at 1.0. Training is performed for 2 epochs on four NVIDIA A6000 GPUs with per-device
batch size 4 and gradient accumulation 4.
A.2
Details on comparison model experiments
Since spatial AV-LLMs are still limited, we first evaluate existing AV-LLMs that accept visual input
and monaural audio [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 50, 51]. For these models, the binaural audio is converted to
15


monaural during preprocessing. For SAVVY [13], we follow the official code to process our inputs
and evaluate only the applicable spatial grounding tasks, matching the scope of the original paper.
For fairer comparisons, we additionally fine-tune three baselines using the same direct-answer
HLR-AVSceneQA training split.
Hear You Are LLM. We fine-tune Hear You Are LLM [15], the closest existing trainable model
that uses both visual input and binaural audio for spatial audio-visual reasoning. Although it was
originally trained with panoramic observations, HLR-AVSceneQA is defined under normal-field-of-
view (normal-FOV) inputs, where sources may be visible, occluded, off-screen, or located in another
room. We therefore adapt Hear You Are LLM to the same 1280×720 normal-FOV images, 32-kHz
binaural audio, QA prompts, answer targets, and evaluation protocol used for HLR-LLM. Its SigLIP
image encoder can directly process the normal-FOV images as patch tokens without architectural
modification. During fine-tuning, we apply LoRA to the image encoder (rank 16, alpha 32) and update
the projectors/adapters and LLM, while keeping the audio encoder frozen. We retain the original Hear
You Are architecture and do not add the spatial audio branch used in HLR-LLM. Accordingly, this
comparison evaluates two adapted spatial AV-LLMs under matched data and evaluation conditions,
rather than providing a fully architecture-matched comparison.
Qwen2.5-Omni.
We fine-tune Qwen2.5-Omni [4], our monaural-audio base model, on HLR-
AVSceneQA. This provides a controlled baseline for evaluating spatial reasoning without explicit
binaural spatial modeling.
Dual-channel Qwen2.5-Omni. We additionally fine-tune a dual-channel variant of Qwen2.5-Omni
to test whether simply providing both binaural channels is sufficient for spatial reasoning. Instead of
downmixing the binaural input to mono, the left and right channels are separately encoded through
the original monaural audio pathway, and both resulting audio token streams are provided to the
LLM. Unlike HLR-LLM, this variant does not introduce a dedicated spatial audio encoder or spatial
projector. Thus, it isolates the benefit of explicit spatial-audio modeling from simply exposing the
base model to both audio channels.
A.3
STARSS23 preprocessing and QA construction
STARSS23 [19] is an audio-visual dataset of real-world spatial recordings with synchronized video,
first-order ambisonic (FOA) audio, and frame-level spatio-temporal annotations of sound events.
We use STARSS23 as a real-world benchmark complementary to our simulator-generated HLR-
AVSceneQA. Starting from the official STARSS23 development split, we follow the official train/test
partition and divide each recording into non-overlapping 10-second clips. Clips without a correspond-
ing video stream are removed, resulting in 1,317 train clips and 1,116 test clips. For each clip, we
extract a rectilinear egocentric image from the midpoint video frame. We also convert the FOA audio
into binaural audio using the CIPIC subject-048 HRTF convention [54]. This makes the real-world
recordings compatible with the binaural audio interface used by our spatial audio-visual models while
retaining the original STARSS23 annotations.
We convert STARSS23 azimuth annotations into our egocentric convention, where 0◦is front, 90◦is
right, 180◦is back, and 270◦is left. We construct QA pairs for sound-event recognition, egocentric
direction prediction, egocentric distance prediction, and visibility prediction. We distinguish static
and dynamic sources using angular and distance variation of active annotation frames, and use only
static sources for spatial QA. Following psychoacoustic studies of auditory motion and distance
discrimination [55, 56], a source is treated as static when its angular variation is within 5◦and its
relative distance variation is within 20%.
For each retained static source, we compute representative azimuth and distance values from its active
annotation frames and discretize them into the same egocentric bins used in HLR-AVSceneQA. We
remove ambiguous QA items, including cases where frame-level annotations cross bin boundaries or
multiple same-class sources make the queried source unclear. Since STARSS23 does not provide
object-level semantic masks, visibility is defined geometrically by whether the representative source
direction falls inside the canonical rectilinear camera field of view.

Ablation study

shows that binaural audio is essential for spatial grounding. Removing the binaural pathway in (c)

Increasing the training data generally improves spatial reasoning, particularly relational reasoning
and scene captioning, although some grounding tasks exhibit non-monotonic variation.

Details on HLR-AVSceneQA
We provide implementation-level details on HLR-AVSceneQA, including the dataset construction
pipeline, task characteristics, question and answer formats, quality-control procedures, and additional
details that were not introduced in the main paper.
C.1
Scene initialization, agent placement, and scene generation
Simulator setup.
We generate each sample in Habitat-Sim with acoustic rendering using the
SoundSpaces simulator [16]. The agent is initialized with synchronized RGB, semantic, depth,
panoramic RGB, and binaural audio sensors. The RGB sensor is a pinhole camera used as the
main visual input. The semantic and depth sensors are used for visibility checking and geometric
supervision, while the panoramic RGB sensor is used for visualization and debugging. The binaural
audio sensor is co-located with the visual agent and uses a two-channel receiver at a 32 kHz sampling
rate with material-aware indirect propagation. We set the sensor height to 150 cm and the camera
field of view to 90◦.
We select valid rooms from HM3D scenes [39] by checking that the semantic region contains a floor,
a valid room-level region of interest, and sufficient navigable positions. In the standard setting, we
place the agent near a room corner and orient it toward the room center, which increases the chance
that inserted objects and room semantics are visible. Since this placement may bias sound sources
toward the front view, we also sample agent poses closer to the room center. This makes it possible to
construct scenes in which one reference object remains visible while another object or sound source
falls outside the camera view.
Coordinate system and azimuth definition. We use Habitat coordinates for camera, agent, and
source placement, and convert positions into metric distances when constructing QA labels. For
azimuth, we annotate angles around the horizontal plane, ranging from 0 to 360◦. In our setup, 0◦
indicates the front direction, 90◦indicates the right direction, 180◦indicates the back direction, and
270◦indicates the left direction relative to the agent. We do not consider elevation in this manuscript,
as human spatial-audio localization is generally known to be more accurate and robust in azimuth
than in elevation [57].
For both direction and distance, we discretize continuous spatial values into bins to make training and
evaluation more stable. For azimuth, we use 8 bins, each covering 45◦. Angles increase clockwise
from straight ahead: front corresponds to 337.5◦–22.5◦, right to 67.5◦–112.5◦, back to 157.5◦–
202.5◦, and left to 247.5◦–292.5◦. For distance, we use 10 bins, with a 1-meter interval for 0–1 m,
0.5-meter intervals from 1 to 5 m, and a final bin for distances beyond 5 m. The full bin definitions
are shown in Table S4. This discretization reduces ambiguity from small geometric differences and
facilitates stable training and evaluation.
3D object meshes and scene insertion. We insert 3D object meshes into HM3D rooms to synthesize
controlled layouts containing both sounding and non-sounding objects. The object pool is built
from curated meshes, including Objaverse-XL assets [43], as well as generated meshes for sound
categories whose object coverage is insufficient. For generated sounding-object meshes, we first use
Stable Diffusion 2 [44] for text-to-image synthesis, and then use TRELLIS [45] for image-to-3D
mesh generation. The size and brightness of each object category are manually determined to ensure
plausible rendering in indoor scenes. Examples are shown in Fig. S6.
When inserting objects into a room, we reject placements that collide with room geometry, overlap
existing objects above a horizontal IoU threshold, or violate minimum agent-to-object or object-
to-object distance constraints. These filtering steps help ensure that inserted objects are physically
plausible and visually distinguishable.

Spatial reasoning scenarios. We intentionally include several scenarios that require audio-visual
spatial reasoning beyond semantic matching. Occluded sound sources place the active source behind
a visible object or behind a wall, so the source may project into the camera view while having no
visible semantic pixels. Ambiguous sound sources among same-category instances include multiple
objects of the same category, requiring the model to determine which instance is spatially aligned
with the sound. Semantically mismatched but spatially aligned cases attach a sound event to an
object that does not semantically match the event, preventing models from relying only on category
correspondence. Adjacent-room sound sources move the source into a neighboring room connected
through the scene geometry, creating cases where the source is not visible but can still be heard from
a distant location.
C.2
Task definitions and details
Here we provide additional details on the task definitions, their descriptions, and the intention of each
task, using the example in Fig. S7. In this scenario, Fig. S7 (a) shows the agent’s view, which is the
model input with a normal field of view. Several instances are inserted, including a dog, a piano, a
baby, and two alarm clocks. There are two sound events: one is a man speaking, saying “Turn off
the alarm,” coming from the agent’s front-left direction and originating from an adjacent room. The
other is an alarm ringing from the blue alarm clock, coming from the front direction, 3.4 meters away.
Based on this example, we describe the goal and intended evaluation behavior of each task.
Furthermore, to improve linguistic diversity and reduce overfitting to fixed question formats, we
generate paraphrased templates for each task. We create approximately 15 paraphrases per task and
show three representative prompts below. The full set of templates will be included in the dataset
release.
Spatial hearing: sound event recognition. This task evaluates semantic audio perception. The
model is asked to identify the audible sound event from a set of candidate sound categories. A total of
10 candidate sound categories are provided as options. The target sound category is always included,
while distractors are sampled from semantically related categories, sound categories associated
with scene objects, and random categories. When multiple sources are present, the prompt may
additionally specify the source direction and distance to indicate which source should be recognized.
• Q: What sound do you hear from the front, 3.4 meters away?
• A: alarm clock ringing.
• Paraphrase 1: Which sound is coming from the front, 3.4 meters away?
• Paraphrase 2: Identify the sound event from the front direction at 3.4 meters.
23


Spatial hearing: far-field automatic speech recognition. This task evaluates far-field automatic
speech recognition (ASR) from spatialized audio. Since speech may contain important information
that the agent should understand, the model is required to recognize speech as part of spatial
hearing. Because speech can originate from both nearby and distant locations, far-field ASR is
more challenging than clean or near-field ASR. We ask the model to return only the exact quoted
transcription, without any additional explanation.
• Q: Transcribe what the person said. Output only the transcription inside quotes.
• A: “Turn off the alarm.”
• Paraphrase 1: Write the exact phrase spoken. Return only the quoted transcription.
• Paraphrase 2: Provide the exact spoken words inside quotes.
Spatial grounding: egocentric direction prediction. This task evaluates egocentric sound-source
direction estimation. The answer is computed from the vector pointing from the agent to the sound
source, with its azimuth measured relative to the agent’s current forward direction. We ask the model
to answer using a discretized 8-bin azimuth label as shown in Table S4. If multiple sources are
present, we specify the queried sound source.
• Q: From this camera perspective, which direction bin does the source of alarm clock ringing fall
into?
• A: front.
• Paraphrase 1: Based on the current view, which direction is the alarm clock ringing coming from?
• Paraphrase 2: From this viewpoint, identify the direction bin for the source of alarm clock ringing.
Spatial grounding: egocentric distance prediction. This task evaluates egocentric source-distance
estimation. The answer is computed as the horizontal distance between the agent and the active sound
source, and is then converted into a discrete distance bin, as shown in Table S4. If multiple sources
are present, we specify the queried sound source.
• Q: From this camera perspective, what is the distance to the source of alarm clock ringing?
• A: F.
• Paraphrase 1: Based on the current view, how far away is the source of alarm clock ringing?
• Paraphrase 2: From this viewpoint, select the distance bin for the source of alarm clock ringing.
Spatial grounding: allocentric direction prediction. This task evaluates direction reasoning from
a different viewpoint. The prompt asks the model to imagine standing at a reference object and
facing another object, as shown in Fig. S7 (b). The source direction is then recomputed in this new
coordinate frame. It is intended to evaluate whether the model can reason from a different perspective
and understand the 3D spatial structure of the current scene. The task is generated only when valid
visible reference and facing objects are available. We avoid prompts where the reference and facing
objects have identical final category names, which would make the instruction ambiguous. As in the
egocentric case, the answer is a discretized 8-bin azimuth label. If multiple sources are present, we
specify the queried sound source.
• Q: If you stand by the piano facing the baby, which direction is the source of alarm clock ringing
coming from?
• A: front-left.
• Paraphrase 1: Standing next to the piano and facing the baby, which direction does the alarm
clock ringing come from?
• Paraphrase 2: From a position at the piano facing the baby, identify the direction bin of the alarm
clock ringing.
Spatial grounding: allocentric distance prediction. This task evaluates distance reasoning from a
different viewpoint. Similar to allocentric direction prediction, the prompt asks the model to imagine
standing at a reference object and facing another object. The source distance is then recomputed
from the reference object’s position. This task evaluates whether the model can reason about source
distance from a different location in the scene. We avoid prompts where the reference and facing
objects have identical final category names, which would make the instruction ambiguous. As in the
24


egocentric case, the answer is a discretized 10-bin distance label. If multiple sources are present, we
specify the queried sound source.
• Q: If you stand by the piano facing the baby, what is the distance to the source of alarm clock
ringing?
• A: B.
• Paraphrase 1: Standing next to the piano and facing the baby, how far away is the alarm clock
ringing?
• Paraphrase 2: From a position at the piano facing the baby, select the distance bin for the source
of alarm clock ringing.
Spatial relational reasoning: visibility. This task evaluates whether the model can determine if
the sound source is visible from the current viewpoint. This task becomes difficult when the scene
contains the same or similar categories, but the actual sound comes from outside the current viewpoint.
For example, one may incorrectly assume that the man speaking sound in Fig. S7 comes from the
baby, since the baby is also human-related. However, with sufficient spatial audio perception, the
model should distinguish that the baby is not making the sound and that the sound source is not
visible. The ground truth is computed from semantic rendering: a source is considered visible if the
number of semantic pixels corresponding to the source object exceeds a configured threshold. If
multiple sources are present, we specify the queried sound source.
• Q: From the current camera perspective, is the source of man speaking visible?
• A: no.
• Paraphrase 1: Based on the current view, can you see the source of man speaking?
• Paraphrase 2: From this viewpoint, is the man speaking source in view?
Spatial relational reasoning: correspondence. This task evaluates audio-visual spatial correspon-
dence. The model is asked to identify which visible object is spatially aligned with the sound source.
The correct answer may be semantically different from the sound category, since the task focuses
on spatial alignment rather than category matching. This task becomes challenging when multiple
visible objects exist, as in Fig. S7. Without spatial audio understanding, the model may confuse
whether the yellow alarm clock is ringing. If no visible object corresponds to the sound source, the
answer is None. When multiple visible inserted objects share the same category, we disambiguate
them using orientation labels, such as “alarm clock on the front-right.” If multiple sources are present,
we specify the queried sound source.
• Q: From the current camera perspective, which object is spatially aligned with the sound of alarm
clock ringing?
• A: alarm clock in front.
• Paraphrase 1: Which visible object corresponds to where the alarm clock ringing comes from?
• Paraphrase 2: Which object best matches the spatial origin of the alarm clock ringing?
Spatial relational reasoning: hidden-source localization. This task evaluates reasoning about
non-visible sound sources. It is asked when the source is not reliably visible in the current image. The
answer includes five options: hidden behind the wall, hidden behind a specific object, in an adjacent
room, out of the field of view in front, and out of the field of view behind the agent. This task goes
one step beyond visibility prediction. The model must reason where the source may be if it is not
visible. It should decide whether the sound comes from a distant place, such as another room, or
whether it should be in the field of view but is occluded by an object, which requires accurate visual
perception. The ground-truth label is determined using visibility, projection, adjacent-room metadata,
and occluder information. If multiple sources are present, we specify the queried sound source.
• Q: You hear a man speaking. Where is the source of this sound?
• A: in an adjacent room.
• Paraphrase 1: Given the sound of man speaking, where is its source?
• Paraphrase 2: A man speaking is audible. Where could the source be situated?
25


Spatial relational reasoning: loudness change. This task evaluates relative acoustic reasoning
under hypothetical movement. The prompt asks whether the sound would become louder or quieter
if the agent moved to a specified object. Here, louder or quieter is defined as a controlled relative
movement judgment based on whether the source becomes closer or farther, rather than an estimate
of absolute perceived loudness. This assesses whether the model can decide whether a movement
would bring the agent closer to or farther from the sound source. It also goes one step beyond
allocentric distance prediction. The model must estimate the source distance from the changed
location and compare it with the current source distance. The label is computed by comparing the
current agent-to-source distance with the candidate-object-to-source distance. If the candidate object
is closer to the source than the current agent position, the answer is louder; otherwise, it is quieter.
If multiple sources are present, we specify the queried sound source.
• Q: If you move to the piano, will the sound of man speaking become louder or quieter?
• A: louder.
• Paraphrase 1: When at the piano, will the sound of man speaking be louder or quieter?
• Paraphrase 2: Upon moving to the piano, does the man speaking become louder or quieter?
Spatial relational reasoning: proximity reasoning. This task goes one step beyond loudness
change and evaluates source-to-object spatial reasoning. The model is given a set of candidate objects
and must select the one closest to the sound source. This requires comparing the source distance
to each candidate object, similar to evaluating loudness change for each object, and then ranking
the candidates to select the closest one. The ground truth is computed using the Euclidean distance
from the source to each candidate object. As with other distance-based relational tasks, we exclude
adjacent-room sources to avoid cases where direct Euclidean geometry does not reflect the effective
acoustic propagation path. Tied cases are skipped to avoid ambiguous supervision. If multiple sources
are present, we specify the queried sound source.
• Q: Which object is closest to the source of alarm clock ringing?
• A: bed.
• Paraphrase 1: To which object is the alarm clock ringing nearest?
• Paraphrase 2: Which object is at the shortest distance from the source of alarm clock ringing?
Spatial reasoning: scene captioning. This task evaluates compositional spatial audio-visual scene
description. The model is asked to describe each sound source using complete sentences and include
the sound event, direction, visibility, and hidden-source location when applicable. The prompt
explicitly instructs the model not to describe unrelated scene content. When multiple sources are
present, the model must provide a separate description for each source.
• Q: Describe only the sound source or sound sources.
• A: The sound event is alarm clock ringing. It is coming from the front direction. The sound source
is visible in the scene.
• Paraphrase 1: Summarize only the sound source information.
• Paraphrase 2: For each source, state the sound event, direction, visibility, and hidden location if
applicable.
C.3
Chain-of-thought supervision
We additionally construct chain-of-thought-style supervision to encourage the model to connect audio
perception, visual grounding, and spatial reasoning before producing the final answer. Both the final
answers and CoT rationales are deterministically derived from structured simulator metadata through
task-specific templates rather than free-form generation. GPT-4 [46] is used only to paraphrase
question wording for linguistic diversity. This is especially important for tasks that cannot be solved
from a single cue alone, such as allocentric reasoning, hidden-source localization, loudness change,
and proximity reasoning. In these tasks, the model must first identify the sound event, estimate its
direction or distance, relate it to visible or non-visible objects, and then convert this intermediate
spatial evidence into the requested answer. We keep the reasoning brief and require the model to end
with a normalized final-answer line.

C.4
Quality control
Automatic quality control. We apply several filtering and retry rules to avoid noisy supervision.
First, rooms are skipped if they do not have a valid floor, contain too few insertion slots, or lack
enough valid navigable points. Second, object placements are rejected if they collide with room
geometry, overlap existing objects above the IoU threshold, violate minimum distance constraints,
or fail semantic visibility checks. Third, generated 3D meshes and assets from Objaverse-XL are
manually inspected by human annotators. We retain only sufficiently high-quality objects, filtering
out low-quality meshes and implausible categories. We also find that, in some cases, sounding objects,
reference objects, facing objects, or candidate objects used in task options can be too small to be
clearly visible. We therefore apply a pixel-count threshold and remove QA pairs that ask about
objects that are hardly visible. Finally, proximity questions with tied nearest objects are skipped.
Human verification. We additionally conduct a human verification study on 150 samples, covering
1,144 QA pairs, with three human annotators. Annotators verify whether the target sound is audible,
whether its approximate spatial location can be identified, whether candidate and reference objects
mentioned in the questions are observable, and whether the annotated source visibility and hidden-
source location are consistent with the audio-visual scene. As shown in Table S5, 1,133 of 1,144 QA
pairs pass human verification, corresponding to an overall pass rate of 99.0%. All spatial-hearing and
egocentric-grounding questions achieve a 100% pass rate, while the remaining task categories achieve
pass rates between 96.8% and 100%. These results provide human validation that our metadata- and
template-based construction pipeline produces reliable spatial audio-visual QA annotations across
the benchmark tasks.

Broader impact
HLR-AVSceneQA and HLR-LLM aim to support spatially aware audio-visual understanding for
embodied AI, assistive agents, robotics, and accessibility applications. The ability to recognize what
sound is present, localize where it originates, and reason about how it relates to the current scene is
especially important for agents that navigate indoor environments and assist in daily situations. For
example, such systems may help localize alarms, identify speakers, or reason about events outside the
camera view. Our benchmark is simulator-based and does not contain private real-world recordings.
However, future deployment of spatial audio-visual systems should follow strict privacy, consent, and
data-minimization practices, especially when operating in real homes or shared indoor spaces.
E
LLM usage
We use LLMs in three ways. First, the proposed HLR-LLM is built on a large language model as
the reasoning module that integrates unified inputs, including visual, monaural audio, and binaural
spatial audio tokens. Second, we use an LLM to paraphrase question templates to improve linguistic
diversity and reduce the risk of models learning fixed template patterns. Finally, we use an LLM
to assist with editing and grammar checking, table refinement (Table S4), figure generation and
refinement (Fig. S8, S9, S10, S11), and development of the data-viewer web page.
