# Abstract 

Existing video multimodal large language models (MLLMs) organize observations primarily in time, but embodied spa-tial reasoning requires organizing them in space. Conse-quently, their visual representations can remain locally con-sistent across nearby frames while lacking a persistent spa-tial context that is stable across viewpoints and time. We ex-ploit depth and camera pose, which are naturally available to embodied agents, to explicitly establish this spatial cor-respondence. We introduce Cambrian-BEV, which extends Cambrian-S with a parameter-free geometric tokenization scheme that back-projects visual patch features from each frame into a shared bird’s-eye-view (BEV) coordinate frame and averages features that fall within the same spatial cell. Thus, observations of the same physical location, regard-less of viewpoint or time step, are aggregated into a com-mon spatial token. In addition to providing a persistent scene representation, this formulation acts as an efficient compression mechanism: 128 frames containing approxi-mately 100K DINOv3 patch features are consolidated into a fixed 27 ×27 grid of only 729 BEV tokens, cutting LLM prefill time by 2.9× and peak memory by 3.6×. Cambrian-BEV requires no architectural modification beyond BEV to-kenization and uses a frozen DINOv3 vision encoder with a trainable multimodal projector and LLM. On VSI-Bench, our model reaches 63.48, +8 .5 points over an identically fine-tuned frame-token control with 12 .6× fewer visual to-kens, with particularly large gains on room size ( +24 .5)and relative direction ( +34 .1). On ReVSI, it reaches 44.68, 

+4 .5 over the corresponding control. 

# 1. Introduction 

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have become highly capable at recognizing and describing visual content, yet they remain substantially weaker at reasoning about the geometry of a scene. In video-based spatial question an-swering, a scene is typically represented as a sequence of frame-level visual tokens [21, 28, 33] (Fig. 1, top). Each token is indexed by the image coordinates of a particular viewpoint, rather than by the physical location it observes. Consequently, overlapping views produce redundant evi-dence, while the language model must implicitly estab-lish cross-view correspondence, reconcile revisited regions, and infer metric relationships from a long token sequence. VSI-Bench highlights this limitation: although local spa-tial awareness emerges in modern MLLMs, constructing and reasoning over a coherent scene-level representation re-mains a major bottleneck [31]. Increasing the number of frames can provide broader visual coverage, but also in-creases the visual context approximately linearly, creating a particularly unfavorable trade-off for compact models in-tended for embodied deployment. This trade-off is unnecessary in many embodied settings. Robots, augmented-reality devices, and mobile scanning platforms commonly provide, or can estimate, depth, cam-era intrinsics, and camera pose. These signals are also avail-able in indoor datasets such as ScanNet, ScanNet++, and ARKitScenes [2, 8, 35]. Given this information, an image patch can be associated with a location in a common 3D coordinate frame without learning a view transformation. Spatial mapping and BEV perception have long used this principle to align observations across viewpoints [4, 13, 24]. Recent spatial MLLMs have also begun to incorporate cam-era pose, reconstructed geometry, BEV renderings, or ex-plicit 3D memories [9, 12, 25, 32, 40]. However, these methods often retain frame-indexed visual tokens, depend on learned geometric modules, render object-level maps, or maintain variable-length spatial memories. We instead ask whether known geometry can be used directly as a visual tokenization rule, converting a long video into a fixed set of scene-indexed features before language reasoning begins, so the model reasons over the scene from above rather than frame by frame. We build on Cambrian-S [33], a video MLLM whose spatial instruction tuning on the VSI-590K corpus improves spatial reasoning but leaves this frame-indexed interface in place. We show that comparable frame-token performance does not imply comparable BEV per-formance, identifying spatially localized dense features as a key requirement for geometry-aware visual tokeniza-tion. • Strong spatial reasoning with a compact language backbone. Cambrian-BEV reaches 63.48 on VSI-Bench and 44.68 on ReVSI, with robust improvements on met-2ric questions (room size, distances) and, given an egocen-tric grid frame, on relative direction, demonstrating the value of explicit geometric alignment for efficient embod-ied video understanding. 

# 2. Related Work 

## 2.1. Spatial Reasoning in Video MLLMs 

Spatial question answering initially focused on benchmarks and models that operate on explicit 3D scene representa-tions [1, 14, 23, 43]. Subsequent methods introduced spatial awareness into 2D vision-language models through spatial instruction tuning, depth prediction, and RGB-D condition-ing [3, 5, 7]. More recently, video-based benchmarks such as VSI-Bench and ReVSI have shown that modern MLLMs remain particularly limited in metric, configurational, and cross-view reasoning [31, 38]. Geometry-aware video MLLMs address these limitations using 3D positional en-codings, pretrained reconstruction features, spatial instruc-tion tuning, pose-grounded camera tokens, or combinations of implicit and explicit geometry [9, 16, 20, 32, 33, 39, 40]. Despite substantial progress, many of these approaches re-tain observations as frame- or view-indexed tokens and in-corporate geometry as auxiliary information. Repeated ob-servations of the same physical region can therefore remain represented by separate tokens, leaving the language model to infer their correspondence. Our approach instead uses available depth and camera pose to establish this correspon-dence before language reasoning by organizing visual fea-tures in a shared spatial coordinate frame. 

## 2.2. Allocentric and BEV Scene Representations 

Allocentric representations provide a persistent coordinate system in which observations from different viewpoints can be spatially aligned. Semantic MapNet and Trans4Map project egocentric visual features into top-down memo-ries for semantic-map prediction [4, 6], while VLMaps and ConceptFusion associate geometric maps with open-vocabulary visual-language features for navigation and multimodal retrieval [15, 18]. Recent work increasingly ex-poses structured allocentric representations to multimodal language models. GPT4Scene renders a BEV map and uses consistent object identifiers to connect top-down and egocentric observations [25]; Struct2D combines BEV ren-derings with object-level metadata [42]; and Video2Layout and Map2Thought reconstruct metric layouts that support explicit spatial computation [10, 17]. Cog3DMap instead constructs a variable-length 3D token memory, recurrently merging semantic and geometric features associated with overlapping spatial locations [12]. These methods demon-strate the value of allocentric representations, but typically render symbolic maps, reconstruct object-level layouts, use learned mapping modules, or maintain variable-size 3D memories. In contrast, we directly back-project dense vi-sual patch features into a fixed metric BEV grid and aver-age all features assigned to each cell. The geometric pro-jection and aggregation are parameter-free and require no object detector, semantic-map supervision, map decoder, or learned view transformation. The resulting BEV cells are supplied to the MLLM as spatially aligned visual tokens. 

## 2.3. Token-Efficient Video Modeling 

The visual context of a video MLLM grows rapidly with the number of processed frames, motivating representa-tions that preserve long-range evidence under a bounded to-ken budget. Prior approaches maintain compact working and episodic memories [22], fold streaming observations into persistent memory structures [30], or incrementally compress geometry-aware features through spatial cluster-ing or voxelization [12, 36]. Structured scene graphs and 3D memories provide another form of compression by re-taining object-level entities and relations rather than dense frame features [11, 34, 37]. Such methods generally reduce context through learned memory updates, selective reten-tion, object abstraction, or adaptive spatial grouping. Our method instead uses deterministic geometric correspon-dence as the compression criterion. Features are merged when their back-projected locations fall within the same physical BEV cell, regardless of the frame or viewpoint from which they were observed. Consequently, the token budget is bounded by the BEV grid resolution rather than video length, making spatial alignment and video compres-sion two outcomes of the same operation. 

# 3. Methodology 

Our goal is to replace the frame-sequential visual context of a video MLLM with a compact, metrically structured one while modifying the model as little as possible. Figure 2 shows the pipeline. The base model’s frozen encoder and projector produce patch tokens (Sec. 3.1); a parameter-free geometric module fuses them across frames into a single BEV grid (Sec. 3.2); and the LLM consumes this grid in place of image tokens (Sec. 3.4). Section 3.3 analyzes the encoder property that makes this fusion viable.

## 3.2. Parameter-free BEV projection 

The BEV module assigns each patch token a 3D location by closed-form geometry and then pools tokens by ground-plane cell. All geometric computation is deterministic given depth and pose, and nothing is learned. The module is dif-ferentiable with respect to the input features, so gradients from the language loss backpropagate through the pooling operation to the projector (Sec. A). Because pooling oper-ates on projector outputs , the resulting BEV tokens already live in the language embedding space, and no adapter of any kind is required (Sec. 3.4).

Vertical structure. Collapsing the height axis means that a cell can mix an object with the wall, floor, and ceiling behind it. Measured on our final 27 ×27 , ±5 m grid, an ob-ject in the 0.3–1.2 m height band typically shares its cell with 1.5–2.7 × its own feature mass, and up to 169 × in the worst case. We therefore support an optional per-scene floor-relative height filter (Sec. B). Whether removing the diluting mass helps is an empirical question; it does not (Tab. 2e, Sec. C), and the final model uses no height filter. The output is a grid B ∈ RHb×Wb×D , flattened row-major into HbWb = 729 BEV tokens. This is a fixed-size, scene-indexed set of BEV tokens, 12 .6× smaller than the frame-token context it replaces, in which neighboring to-kens are metric neighbors in the world. 

## 3.3. Fusion needs locally grounded features 

BEV aggregation implicitly assumes that a patch token de-scribes the physical surface at that patch. If each token is instead a mixture of scene-global context, then rearranging tokens by 3D position is meaningless, and averaging tokens from different views of the same place blurs whatever struc-ture they carried. Whether the assumption holds is a prop-erty of the encoder’s training objective. Language-supervised encoders such as SigLIP2 [29] re-ceive their contrastive signal through an attention-pooling head over patch tokens. The objective constrains only the pooled global embedding, leaving individual patch to-kens free to distribute evidence across the image. Prob-ing SigLIP2 directly confirms this (Fig. 3). We read its patch tokens in the way most favorable to it, carrying each through the value pathway of the pooling head so that it becomes the per-patch analogue of the embedding the text tower is trained against; the raw pre-head tokens are less lo-calized still (Sec. H). Even so, the resulting text-similarity maps scatter evidence for a queried object across the im-age, whereas those of DINOv3 follow the object’s extent. DINOv3 [27], in contrast, is trained with dense patch-level self-distillation and adds Gram anchoring , a regularizer that preserves the pairwise similarity structure of patch features throughout training. Its features are therefore designed to preserve stronger dense feature structure. This leads to a testable prediction. If BEV fusion de-pends on feature locality, then two encoders that perform equally as conventional frame-token models should diverge once their tokens are geometrically fused, with the locally grounded encoder benefiting more. We test this by train-ing both encoders through the identical four-stage recipe (Tab. S3) followed by the identical BEV fine-tuning stage (Sec. 4.3). 

## 3.4. Integration into the language model 

The 729 BEV tokens are spliced into the embedded se-quence at the position of the <image> placeholder, ex-actly where the frame tokens would have gone. The LLM architecture, tokenizer, and prompt format are untouched. The final model introduces no new BEV-specific parame-ters, BEV tokens are already language-embedding vectors, and an interposed MLP adapter is not only unnecessary but harmful ( −12 .0 points, Tab. 2d). We also evaluate richer in-terfaces, namely a learnable empty-cell token, a small self-attention BEV encoder with axial 2D rotary embeddings, and temporally chunked (iterative) BEV sequences. None improves on the plain parameter-free grid (Tab. 2, Sec. C). 

Training. Starting from the stage-4 (spatial IT) check-point, we fine-tune the projector and LLM with the vision encoder frozen, using the standard next-token loss on an-swer tokens only. Because the BEV grid is a per-scene quantity, training batches are scene-grouped so that the vi-sion encoding and projection are computed once per scene and shared across its QA pairs. Hyperparameters are given in Sec. 4.1. 

# 4. Experiments 

## 4.1. Setup 

Training data. Models are fine-tuned on the subset of VSI-590K [33] whose scenes provide registered depth and pose, which we refer to as VSI-288K-Depth : 288,478 QA pairs over 4,955 scenes (1,201 ScanNet, 855 ScanNet++, 2,899 ARKitScenes), with 128 uniformly sampled frames per scene. Depth is raw sensor depth (16-bit, millimeter) and poses are the datasets’ ground-truth trajectories; we per-form no reconstruction or pose estimation. VSI-590K cov-ers counting, distance, size, direction, and appearance order 5but contains no route-planning questions. Route planning, one of the eight VSI-Bench categories, is therefore a held-out task for every fine-tuned model below.

Implementation details. The fifth stage shared by Cambrian-BEV and the control runs for 2 epochs (2,176 steps) with the projector and LLM fully trainable (no LoRA), both at learning rate 2×10 −5 with a cosine sched-ule, 3% warmup, and no weight decay. The global batch is 256, organized as 8 QA pairs per scene. As in stages 1–4, training runs under bf16 autocast with fp32 master weights (Sec. G). Training takes about 6 hours on 2 ×H100 80 GB GPUs. 

Benchmarks and metrics. Our primary benchmark is the full VSI-Bench [31] suite of 5,130 questions over 288 real indoor scene videos from ScanNet, ScanNet++, and ARKitScenes, in eight categories (Tab. 1). The four numerical categories are scored by Mean Relative Ac-curacy (MRA), the fraction of relative-error thresholds in {0.50 , 0.55 , . . . , 0.95 } that a prediction satisfies; the four multiple-choice categories by accuracy, with the three relative-direction splits averaged into one category as in the official protocol. The overall score is the unweighted mean of the eight. We additionally evaluate on ReVSI [38], an expert-re-annotated rebuild of VSI-Bench (Sec. 4.4). Train-ing and evaluation are disjoint at the scene level: none of the 288 VSI-Bench scenes occurs among the 4,955 train-ing scenes, so every benchmark scene, on VSI-Bench and ReVSI alike, is unseen at every training stage. Generation is greedy with at most 16 new tokens, and answers are parsed with the official lmms-eval rules; unparseable numeric an-swers score 0. All models receive up to 128 frames per scene; ours additionally receives the sensor depth maps, in-trinsics, and poses shipped with the benchmark scenes. 

Reproduction gap. Our reproduced frame baselines fall 

∼10 points below the official 0.5B release. The cause is our use of bf16 autocast, adopted for compute budget, in place of the official full-fp32 training. This gap only strength-ens the comparison: our BEV model starts from the weaker base and still surpasses the official release by nearly 12 points. Section G gives the details.

Token efficiency. Profiled on one H100 (Sec. E), the 

12 .6× shorter visual context cuts prefill 2.9× (16 vs. 46 ms per question) and peak memory 3.6× (2.5 vs. 9.1 GB). Building the BEV grid costs 1.9 ms per scene, 0.5% of the vision encoder it already requires. The efficiency gain is therefore confined to the part of inference that depends on the visual context: prefill and memory. # 5. Conclusion 

We introduced Cambrian-BEV, a parameter-free geometric tokenization that uses depth and camera pose to aggregate video features into a shared BEV grid. By replacing 9,216 

frame tokens with 729 BEV tokens, our method shrinks the LLM visual context 12 .6×, cutting prefill time 2.9×

and peak memory 3.6×, while improving spatial reasoning on VSI-Bench and ReVSI. We further showed that frame-token performance does not predict performance under geo-metric rearrangement: two encoders that tie as frame-token models diverge substantially once their patches are fused in BEV, making dense feature locality a necessary selec-tion criterion for geometry-aware MLLMs. The represen-tation has clear limits: a single time-collapsed grid dis-cards visitation order, the one category where frame to-kens remain clearly ahead, and flattens vertical structure, so thin or stacked objects share a cell with the surfaces behind them; simple remedies such as temporal chunk-ing, frame–BEV hybrids, and height filtering did not close these gaps (Sec. C). The method also assumes calibrated depth and pose and is best suited to static, room-scale in-door scenes. Future work will pursue temporal encod-ings that preserve grid resolution, height-aware pooling, ro-bustness to estimated geometry, and larger or dynamically expanding grids for longer trajectories and larger back-bones.

# A. BEV projection details 

This section gives a complete specification of the parameter-free BEV module of Sec. 3.2, including the co-ordinate conventions, default values, and degenerate cases that the main text only summarizes. All choices below are those of the final model unless stated otherwise; the alterna-tives are the ablation arms of Tab. 2. 

Inputs and rescaling. A scene contributes T =128 

frames sampled uniformly over the recorded trajectory (linspace over the frame indices, so frame 0 of the video is always the first sampled frame; scenes shorter than 128 frames contribute all of their frames). Each frame pro-vides an RGB image, a 16-bit depth map in millimeters, intrinsics Kt, and a world-to-camera pose T w2ct . RGB and depth are resized to the encoder resolution of 448 ×448 

without preserving aspect ratio (bicubic for RGB, nearest-neighbor for depth so that no interpolated depth values are created), and the intrinsics are rescaled to match: the first row of Kt is multiplied by 448 /W native and the sec-ond row by 448 /H native , so the anisotropic resize intro-duces no geometric error. The encoder (DINOv3 ViT-L/16) yields a 28 ×28 patch grid per frame, so a scene produces 

128 ×784 = 100 ,352 candidate points, each carrying one projector-output token fp ∈ R896 .

Depth sampling and validity. The depth of patch (i, j )

is read at the patch center (u, v ) = (( j+ 12 )P, (i+ 12 )P ),

P =16 , by bilinear interpolation of the resized depth map (border padding). A patch is discarded if its sampled depth is non-finite, non-positive, or outside [0 .1, 100] m; missing sensor depth is stored as zero and therefore falls in this category. A patch whose center lands in a depth hole is dropped even when its RGB content is valid. We also im-plemented a masked average-pooling variant that averages all valid depth pixels inside the 16 ×16 patch footprint; it is more robust to speckle but was not used in any reported run. 

Common world frame. The three capture sources ship poses in different conventions. We convert all of them to a single right-handed, gravity-aligned world frame with Z

up and an OpenCV camera ( X right, Y down, Z forward). ScanNet and ScanNet++ scan frames are already Z-up; ARKitScenes poses are expressed with an OpenGL cam-era ( Y up, Z backward) in a Y -up world and are converted with diag(1 , −1, −1) on the camera side and a 90 ◦ rotation about X on the world side (the shipped .align.json 

transforms do not give a clean gravity-aligned frame and are ignored). Numerics and gradients. All geometry (depth sampling, unprojection, pose transform, rotation, centering, binning) runs in fp32 with autocast disabled and under no grad ; it produces only indices, weights, and masks. The scatter it-self is applied to the tokens in their training dtype (bf16), while the per-cell counts and splat weights are accumu-lated in fp32, because a bf16 counter cannot exceed 256 :above that value the representable numbers are two apart, so adding one rounds back to 256 . Because scatter-add fol-lowed by count normalization is linear in the tokens, the gradient that reaches a patch token is the gradient of its cell divided by the cell count (or scaled by its splat weight). The module therefore has no parameters but is fully differen-tiable with respect to the projector, which is trained through it. 

Degenerate cases. (i) A first camera pointing straight up or down has a vanishing ground-plane heading; the normal-ization in Eq. (S1) is guarded with a small ϵ, but the result-ing yaw is arbitrary; handheld and head-mounted record-ings start roughly level, and we apply no further guard. (ii) Pose drift or gross pose errors make the centroid meaning-less and can push every point outside the ±5 m window, leaving all 729 tokens empty (Fig. S2c); the model then an-swers from its prior. (iii) Scenes in which a large fraction of patches have no depth (reflective or distant surfaces) lose those patches entirely rather than receiving an approximate location. 

# B. Height-filter details 

The floor-relative height filter of Sec. 3.2 estimates a per-scene floor anchor as the 2nd percentile of valid world-frame point heights and drops points outside [zmin , z max ]

above it. A per-scene anchor is necessary because absolute height references differ across capture sources: ScanNet-family scenes are floor-referenced whereas ARKitScenes scenes are device-referenced, so no global threshold works. The band evaluated in Tab. 2 is [0 .05 , 2.0] m, which removes floor speckle and ceiling mass; it costs 5.9 points on the VSI-Bench average and is therefore disabled in the final model.

85 .0 → 70 .5), and it is worst on ReVSI as well ( −9.2). Pooled projector outputs are already language-space vec-tors, and randomly initialized modules only disturb them under the short fine-tuning budget. 

(e) Alternative representations. Three natural modifica-tions all underperform the single grid. Temporal chunking :16 per-chunk grids plus a global grid leave relative direction at world level ( 45 .2); the first-pose benefit does not trans-fer to chunked grids. Hybrid frames+BEV : prepending up to 128 frames to the first-pose grid scores 56.1, 7.4 points below BEV-only at 13 .6× the token budget, with relative direction back at world level ( 47 .2 vs. 82 .7). The only cat-egory the hybrid wins is appearance order, which is exactly what frames in temporal order supply. Height filtering : a floor-relative 0.05–2.0 m band costs −5.9 and drops rela-tive direction from 82 .7 to 51 .0, even though the vertical-dilution analysis of Sec. 3.2 predicts a gain. We hypothe-size, without testing it here, that the floor and ceiling mass keeps the full scene footprint represented in the grid, and that this geometric context outweighs the dilution it causes. 

# D. Grid range 

The ±5 m range of the BEV grid is a data-driven choice. For each of the 4,955 scenes of VSI-288K-Depth we back-projected 128 uniformly sampled frames with the final model’s geometry (DINOv3 at 448 ×448 , patch 16, first-pose orientation), centered the points on their centroid as in Sec. 3.2, and measured the fraction of points falling inside a symmetric ±R window for R ∈ { 3, 4, 5, 6, 7.5, 10 } m(Tab. S1). At ±5 m the grid retains 98.9% of all observed points, with a median per-scene retention above 99.8% on every source dataset, and 96% of scenes retain at least 95% of their points. Shrinking the window discards scene con-tent quickly: ±4 m drops 2.9% of points and ±3 m 8.7%, with ScanNet++ scenes losing 13%. Widening it buys lit-tle: ±7.5 m recovers a further 0.9% of points, but at a fixed 

27 ×27 resolution it enlarges each cell from 0.37 to 0.56 m, so the same tokens carry a coarser description of the scene.

The bilinear-splatting variant of Tab. 2 raises this to 6.4 ms (the scatter alone is 6.1× slower, 5.3 vs. 0.9 ms), still 1.7% of scene en-coding. Per question, the 12 .6× shorter visual context cuts LLM prefill by 2.9× (15 .7 vs. 45 .9 ms; the +30 ms gap is stable to ±0.3 ms across two independent runs) and peak memory by 3.6× (2.5 vs. 9.1 GB). The saving is sub-linear in tokens because at these lengths prefill is not yet attention-dominated. Decode is unchanged ( 211 ms for 15 steps for both models, with run-to-run drift larger than the model-to-model difference), since for a 0.5B LLM it is bound by per-step launch overhead rather than by context length, and it dilutes the end-to-end gap to 1.13 × (236 vs. 267 ms per question). Because small launch-bound kernels are the most hardware-sensitive, the ratios depend on the accelerator: on an H200 NVL the same code gives a 2.1× prefill saving, so ratios should be quoted with the hardware. The representa-tion therefore costs nothing to build on top of the encoder it already requires and removes 66% of prefill time and 72% of peak memory; further per-question savings would have to come from the decode loop, not from the visual repre-sentation.

# G. Reproduction gap 

Our reproduced frame baselines sit ∼10 points below the official 0.5B release. The training data are the complete released sets at every stage; the difference is numerical pre-cision. The official recipe trains all four stages in full fp32, whereas we keep fp32 master weights and optimizer states but run the forward and backward passes under bf16 au-tocast, a choice made to stay within our compute budget: on matched hardware full fp32 is 4.8–6.9 × slower per step at stages 1–2, and slower still at the long-sequence video stages, where fp32 has no fused attention kernel and forces gradient checkpointing; weighted by compute, the four-stage programme would cost roughly 7.5 × more. A con-trolled stage-2 comparison confirms the direction of the ef-fect (+1.55 on the image-benchmark average for full fp32), and the loss compounds through the later stages (Tab. S3). We expect our conclusions to carry over to full-precision training and to larger decoders because every comparison in this paper is made within a precision and a base model: the frames-only control and the BEV model share the same par-ent, data, schedule, and precision, so precision shifts both arms together. Moreover, the gains concentrate in room size and relative direction, where our 0.5B model already exceeds the official fp32 7B, and in absolute distance: these answers depend on metric information that frame tokens do not carry at any precision or model size, and the BEV tokens are an input-side representation that leaves the de-coder and the four-stage recipe unchanged. The gap only strengthens the comparison: our BEV model starts from the 

weaker base and still surpasses the official release by nearly 12 points. 

# H. Patch-level text-similarity probe 

This section specifies how the maps of Fig. 3 are produced. The probe asks one question of a frozen encoder: given a text concept, which patch tokens of an image carry evi-dence for it? It involves only the published encoders. No component of any model we train enters it, so it cannot be confounded by our fine-tuning. 

Why a joint-space probe. Patch tokens are comparable to text only in the space that the encoder’s own text tower was trained against. Each encoder is therefore probed with its own text tower and with the patch features that live in that tower’s space. Scoring both encoders through a third, shared text encoder would measure that encoder’s align-ment rather than the locality of the features our BEV grid pools. 

Encoders and text towers. For SigLIP2 we use 

google/siglip2-so400m-patch14-384 , with the pooled output of its text tower as the text embedding. For DINOv3 we use DINO.txt [19, 27] ( dinov3_vitl16_ dinotxt_tet1280d20h24l ), the text encoder and vi-sion head aligned to the same DINOv3 ViT-L/16 backbone as the models in Tab. 1. Its 1280 -d text embedding consists of a CLS-aligned half and a patch-aligned half; we keep the patch-aligned half. Both are the public base checkpoints rather than the towers of our fine-tuned models, because our trained projector maps patch features into the language model’s embedding space, to which no text encoder is com-parable. 

Dense image features. For DINOv3 the patch features are the projected patch tokens of the DINO.txt vision head, which is what its text tower was trained against. For SigLIP2 the raw last hidden state patch tokens sit one projection short of the text-aligned space and score as noise per patch. We therefore carry each token through the value pathway of the attention-pooling head, in the man-ner of MaskCLIP [41]: v = WV x, followed by the head’s output projection and residual MLP, v + mlp(ln( v)) . Atten-tion in that head only determines how much of each token’s value vector reaches the pooled embedding, so this quantity is the per-patch analogue of what the text tower is trained against. The choice favors SigLIP2: its raw pre-head to-kens give maps that are more scattered still. 

Preprocessing. Both encoders see the image shown in the first column of Fig. 3. For this probe the photograph is padded to a square with the processor’s mean color (the grey bars) rather than cropped or stretched, so that every patch keeps its aspect ratio; this is the standard Cambrian-S image preprocessing and is specific to the probe. Query embedding. The query is the bare noun in the row label (“tv”, “bicycle”). In both spaces every text prompt car-ries a large query-independent component, roughly “some image content”, and if it is left in place the maps of different queries share most of their spatial structure. We remove it. Let b be the L2-normalized mean embedding of 16 generic prompts ( a photo, an object, a thing, background, a surface, a room, an image, texture, the scene, an area, material, a place, something, indoor scene, empty space, clutter ); the query embedding t is replaced by normalize( t − b (t · b)) .In a held-out check this lowered the correlation between the maps of different queries from 0.43 to 0.29 . The same pro-jection is applied to both encoders, and the contrast in Fig. 3 is present with or without it. 

Display. The similarity map is upsampled bilinearly to the image resolution, colored with the jet colormap, and alpha-blended over the image with α = 0 .6. The color range of each panel is that panel’s 2nd and 98th percentile of cosine similarity; values outside are clipped, and the two percentiles are printed at the ends of the panel’s colorbar. Clipping is a display choice, not a change to the data: it keeps a few extreme patches from flattening the rest of the map. It is also why each panel has its own colorbar. The two encoders occupy different similarity ranges (Tab. S4), and a single shared bar would misrepresent one of them. 

Panel statistics. Table S4 gives, for each panel, the cosine distribution over all patch tokens and Moran’s I, the spatial autocorrelation of the map over 4-neighbouring patches ( +1 

for smooth regions, 0 for a spatially random map; shuffling the patches of any of the four maps gives |I| < 0.002 ). The distribution statistics justify the per-panel colorbars: the two encoders differ in mean, floor, and spread, so one shared range would clip one of them. They do not by themselves speak to locality, since they are unchanged under any spa-tial permutation of the patches. Moran’s I does: it is 0.33 

for both SigLIP2 maps and 0.74 to 0.75 for both DINOv3 maps. Neighbouring SigLIP2 patches are only weakly cor-related, which is the speckle visible in Fig. 3; neighbouring DINOv3 patches agree, which is the compact response over the object. 

Images. The two images are natural photographs from the held-out image validation split used by our visualizer, not VSI-Bench scenes; the probe is run on ordinary pho-tographs on purpose, to show that the property belongs to the encoders rather than to scan data.
