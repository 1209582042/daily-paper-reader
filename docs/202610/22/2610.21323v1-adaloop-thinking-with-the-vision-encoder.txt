## ABSTRACT 

Most vision-language models (VLMs) treat the vision encoder as a static feature extractor: it encodes an image once, independently of the question, and leaves subsequent reasoning to the language model. Yet different questions may require different visual operations and processing depths. We present AdaLoop, a frame-work for question-conditioned visual recurrence in VLMs. AdaLoop adapts vi-sual computation to the question and reuses the encoder’s transformations across multiple iterations, with the pretrained vision weights frozen. We also introduce VisRO, a procedural dataset and generation pipeline for training and evaluating vision-centric reasoning. On six VisRO tasks, AdaLoop outperforms standard fine-tuning and question-conditioned single-pass baselines with matched training data and optimizer updates. These gains hold across three VLM families on both in-distribution and out-of-distribution examples. Our findings suggest that pre-trained vision encoders can contribute to reasoning through task-directed, iterative visual computation. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Most vision–language models (VLMs) encode an image once, independently of the question, and pass the resulting visual tokens to a language model for subsequent reasoning. The same visual computation serves every question, whether the answer depends on a single local feature or on relating distant parts of the scene. A fixed pass may not perform the computation a task requires: useful visual features may lie at intermediate encoder layers [9] or be lost along the way [19, 67]. The encoder thus acts only as a feature extractor, even though vision architectures can themselves learn to reason when trained from scratch [24, 63]. Visual reasoning, then, need not be left entirely to the language model. For a pretrained VLM, an appealing possibility is to reuse the visual transformations already learned by its vision encoder. We draw inspiration from human vision, where processing is directed by the task [68] and recurrent [34, 58]. We investigate how to equip a pretrained vision encoder with both capabilities through (i) question conditioning , which modulates the encoder’s features according to the task [54], and (ii) looping (or recurrence) , which reapplies weight-tied transformations to an evolving state [16, 20, 85]. Introducing these mechanisms into a pretrained VLM raises two questions. First, each encoder layer was trained to operate at a particular feedforward depth, so simply looping a span of its frozen layers yields little benefit (Section 5.2). Second, looping produces a sequence of visual states rather than a single output, and it is not obvious how to present the sequence to the language model. We approach this as a design problem and present AdaLoop, a framework for adaptive visual recur-rence in pretrained VLMs (Figure 1). AdaLoop creates a perception loop by repeatedly applying a span of the vision encoder to an evolving visual state, while keeping the original vision weights frozen. It uses question-conditioned adaptive layer normalization (AdaLN) [53, 54] to modulate the shared transformations at each iteration. We compare three ways for the language model to use the resulting sequence of states: reading only the current state, accumulating states across iterations, and interleaving states with answer drafts. All variants train only on triples of (image, question, an-swer), using the same final-answer target across visual iterations to support adaptive inference-time compute.

Evaluating vision-centric reasoning requires diagnostic tasks with systematic difficulty control and scalable training data. We introduce VisRO (Visual Routines) to support this research effort. VisRO is a procedural dataset and generation pipeline encompassing tasks like grouping, counting, connec-tivity, spatial tracing, and amodal perception—all of which demand careful visual inspection with minimal reliance on language priors or external knowledge (Figure 3). Our pipeline provides pro-grammatically verifiable answers, controllable difficulty, and controlled distributional shifts in task structure or visual appearance for out-of-distribution evaluation. VisRO serves as reusable infras-tructure for the community, and our AdaLoop experiments use six tasks from the collection. Experiments with three VLM families, Qwen2.5-VL [8], PaliGemma 2 [65] and LLaVA-1.5 [44] show that with matched training data and optimizer updates, AdaLoop’s components provide com-plementary gains. Question-conditioning establishes strong single-pass controls, and looping adds a further 2.8–4.6 percentage points of in-distribution VisRO accuracy, for a total improvement of about 11 –15 points over standard fine-tuning. On Qwen2.5-VL, at matched parameter count and approximately matched training FLOPs, AdaLoop achieves competitive VisRO performance with better general visual question answering (VQA) capabilities than longer-trained single-pass con-trols. Our results suggest that existing visual transformations can be steered by the question and reused for iterative reasoning, even with the original weights frozen. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Task-directed vision. Task-directed processing has roots in visual routines , combinations of basic operations selected by the task [68]. Language can supply this task signal through feature-wise mod-ulation [54] or instruction-aware feature extraction [14], and prior work conditions vision encoders on language through normalization [15, 43], cross-attention [1], or token injection [19, 32, 48]. AdaLoop applies such conditioning to encoder layers that are reused across iterations. 

Recurrent and looped architectures. Recurrence has a long history in computational mod-els [40–42, 55]. Looped Transformers increase effective depth by sharing parameters across it-erations [16, 21, 37]. Most are trained from scratch, either as task-specific reasoners for prob-lems such as Sudoku, mazes, and ARC [7, 31, 63, 70] or as language models [20, 85]. Related work studies their scaling [2, 13, 36, 56, 59, 73], architectural design choices [26], adaptive stop-ping [6, 22, 84], and iteration-dependent modulation [29]. Other work retrofits recurrence into pre-trained language models [5, 49, 52], and recent multimodal systems loop all or part of their language backbones [51, 76, 80]. AdaLoop instead loops layers of the vision encoder in a pretrained VLM, and studies how the language model should use the resulting visual states (Appendix D). 

Visual reasoning in VLMs. Visual reasoning methods differ in where intermediate visual compu-tation takes place and how it is controlled. External image operations and visual prompting provide selected or annotated visual evidence for language-model reasoning [25, 60, 62, 74, 77, 83]. Other approaches perform intermediate computation within the language model using generated visual la-tents [38, 69, 72] or learnable visual tokens [39]. Language model states or generated sub-questions can also guide further visual processing [18, 78, 81]. AdaLoop carries an internal vision-encoder state across iterations, updating it under the guidance of the original question. 

Visual reasoning benchmarks. Existing benchmarks assess broad multimodal capabilities [11, 47], compositional reasoning [27, 30], and reasoning about text in images [64]. BLINK and CV-Bench probe core perceptual abilities [17, 66], while BabyVision emphasizes visual reasoning with 2Preprint limited reliance on language knowledge [10]. Scalable training resources for diverse vision-centric reasoning tasks remain limited. VisRO complements existing benchmarks with a procedural gen-eration pipeline supporting both training and evaluation, with explicit controls over difficulty and distribution shifts. 

## 3 METHOD 

AdaLoop introduces question-conditioned recurrence into a pretrained vision–language model. We denote its vision encoder, connector, and language model by E, M , and G, respectively. Given an image x and a question q, AdaLoop repeatedly applies a group of consecutive layers in E to update a visual state, with each update conditioned on q. The later encoder layers and the connector 

M convert each updated state into visual tokens for G. We study two design choices: how to condition the visual updates on the question, and how the language model should use the visual tokens produced across iterations. 3.1 LOOPED VISION ENCODER 

We organize the visual computation into three parts: a prelude P, a loop body R, and a coda C. The prelude processes the image once to produce an initial visual state z0. The loop body consists of consecutive encoder layers, augmented with question conditioning and reused across iterations. Let 

zt denote the visual state after iteration t. The coda comprises the remaining encoder layers and the vision–language connector, mapping zt to a sequence of visual tokens It for the language model,

Adaptation and recurrence are complementary. As we analyze in Appendix A, AdaLN and looping affect visual computation at different granularities. Intuitively, AdaLN provides question-dependent control within each block and feature channel, modifying both the inputs and residual contributions of visual transformations. Also, AdaLN’s scaling of a residual update can locally approximate the first-order effect of repeating the transformation (Appendix A.2), suggesting layer-wise control over effective depth. Meanwhile, looping explicitly repeats a span of blocks, recom-puting their updates as the visual state evolves. Together, they combine fine-grained adaptation with successive applications of the shared computation, and our experiments show benefits from both. 3.2 CONNECTING VISUAL ITERATION TO LANGUAGE

## 4 VIS RO: A D ATASET FOR VISION -C ENTRIC REASONING 

To study when iterative visual computation helps, we introduce VisRO (Visual Routines), a proce-dural dataset and generation pipeline for training and evaluating vision-centric reasoning. Inspired by children’s visual puzzles and cognitive tests, the collection covers grouping, counting, connectiv-ity, tracing, and recognition under occlusion, with minimal reliance on language priors or external knowledge. We use six tasks from this collection, denoted VisRO-Core6 , for our controlled experi-ments. Figure 3 shows these tasks and representative out-of-distribution (OOD) examples. 

Controlled difficulty and distribution shifts. Each core task provides controls over scene com-plexity, such as object counts, path lengths, overlap, and crossings. We use four difficulty levels for training and in-distribution evaluation. OOD tests include harder instances and shifts in scene generation rules or visual appearance. All VisRO-Core6 images are rendered at 448 × 448 . With fixed preprocessing, this lets us vary scene complexity while keeping the per-image visual token budget constant within each backbone. 

Generation and evaluation. Each generator renders a structured scene and computes ground-truth answers from its underlying representation. Answers are evaluated using task-specific, pro-grammatic scoring rules, without an LLM judge. The underlying scenes can also support spatial supervision, such as object locations and paths through the scene. Our AdaLoop experiments use only image–question–answer triples. Appendix E describes the broader task collection and task-specific generation procedures.

(Sec. 5.4) Under approximately matched training compute, how does recurrence compare with non-recurrent alternatives?

We study the language interface, visual modulation, and loop placement in AdaLoop. The ablations use Qwen2.5-VL with frozen vision weights, with training data and optimizer updates matched within each comparison. We use Variant A except when comparing language interfaces. 

Language interfaces. All three interfaces improve both ID and OOD accuracy over single-pass AdaLN, and reading only the current state (A) performs best (Table 3a), so explicitly retaining earlier visual states or answer drafts in the language context is not required to benefit from visual recurrence. Retaining them does help beyond the training depth, however: B and C degrade less than A when run for more iterations (Figure 5b). One possible explanation is that the retained history lets the language model draw on earlier visual states, which are unaffected when later iterations drift beyond the trained regime. 

Modulation and recurrence. Table 3b separates the contributions of modulation, question con-ditioning, and recurrence. Without modulation, looping raises ID accuracy only from 49.9% to 50.9%. Adding question-independent AdaLN improves the loop to 60.9%, partly through its 16M additional trainable parameters. Conditioning the same modules on the question further raises accu-racy to 64.6%, without adding parameters. With question conditioning held fixed, looping improves over single-pass AdaLN by 4.6 ID and 2.2 OOD percentage points. Thus, question information and recurrent computation each contribute beyond the addition of trainable modulation parameters.

The early span gives the largest overall relative gain and leads on four tasks, while the cross-band and late spans give the largest relative gains on Maze and Path, respectively. Recurrence and deep supervision enable AdaLoop to use adaptive computation at inference time. We examine how this benefit varies with problem difficulty and whether useful updates continue beyond the depths seen during training. 

Adaptive computation within trained depths. Using the same checkpoint ( Ttrain = 3 ), we com-pare adaptive stopping ( p = 1 , Ttest = 3 ) with stopping after the first iteration. As shown in Figure 5a, adaptive stopping improves accuracy in most difficulty groups across all three backbones. Its mean stopping depth also increases with difficulty on every backbone, although neither the model nor the stopping rule sees difficulty labels. This shows that adaptive stopping using answer stability allocates more iterations to harder inputs. 

Behavior beyond trained depths. Figure 5b shows that Variants B and C largely maintain accu-racy beyond Ttrain = 3 , whereas A degrades more as depth increases. Although average accuracy stays roughly flat, this stability leaves room for later iterations to correct individual examples: Fig-ure 6 (Variant C) illustrates such cases, where predictions on Glyphs and Metro Map become correct at depth four and remain correct through depth six. 5.4 COMPUTATIONAL TRADE -OFFS

Training cost. At Ttrain = 3 , deep supervision at every depth costs approximately 2.9× the training FLOPs per update of single-pass AdaLN. Sampling one supervised depth according to the normalized loss weights reduces the estimated cost to approxi-mately 1.3×, while preserving the weighted objec-tive in expectation. We therefore compare depth-sampled and deeply supervised AdaLoop, each trained for 40k updates, with single-pass AdaLN trained for 50k and 120k updates, respectively; total training FLOPs match to within 6% (Figure 7b). AdaLoop thus uses 20% and 67% fewer optimizer updates, and correspondingly fewer passes over the training data.

At the higher budget, deeply supervised AdaLoop is slightly below the 120k-update control on VisRO but outperforms it on all four VQA benchmarks. Extending single-pass AdaLN training from 40k to 120k updates lowers all general benchmarks (e.g., MMBench from 75.4% to 71.6%), consistent with forgetting. These results suggest that recur-rence offers a different way to spend training compute: more computation per example rather than more updates over the same data. ## 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduced AdaLoop, a framework for question-conditioned visual recurrence, and VisRO, a procedural dataset and generation pipeline for vision-centric reasoning. Across three VLM fam-ilies, question conditioning and recurrence provide complementary gains. With training data and optimizer updates matched, AdaLoop improves over both supervised fine-tuning and question-conditioned single-pass controls. These gains are obtained with the pretrained vision weights frozen. Our findings suggest that a pretrained vision encoder need not be a fixed front end: its existing visual transformations can be adapted and reused for iterative reasoning. We hope this work encourages further study of how vision modules can contribute to multimodal reasoning. ACKNOWLEDGMENTS

## A ADA LN AS TASK -C ONDITIONED VISUAL COMPUTE ALLOCATION 

This section gives an intuitive account of why question-conditioned adaptive layer normalization (AdaLN) is useful when adapting a pretrained vision encoder. The purpose is not to establish an exact equivalence between feature modulation and recurrent computation. Rather, a local analysis shows that AdaLN provides a fine-grained, first-order mechanism for reallocating the visual trans-formations already present in the encoder, while recurrence provides the complementary ability to recompute those transformations at an updated visual state. Throughout, “compute allocation” refers to the relative influence of transformations already available in the encoder rather than to arithmetic: modulation changes which pretrained updates dominate without changing the FLOPs of a single pass. The scale γℓ is multiplicative in the current visual activity, the gate αℓ rescales the resulting update channel by channel, and the shift βℓ injects a channel-wise bias shared across visual tokens. Thus, the two multiplicative terms preserve the image- and token-dependent structure of zℓ,t and can selectively amplify or suppress transformations that are already active; the additive term does not. This qualitative distinction does not by itself prove that scaling must be more effective, but it is consistent with our preliminary observations, on an earlier version of our data, that the scale component accounts for most of the gain from AdaLN. Recurrence should be most useful when solving a task that requires successive updates to the internal relational organization of a scene, such as propa-gating reachability through a maze or forming and consolidating visual groups before counting them. Whether a task demands iterative state construction depends on how much structure a single pass already resolves, not on the task’s label. A.3 WHY ADA LN AND RECURRENCE ARE COMPLEMENTARY 

The analysis above separates two axes that ordinary network depth conflates. AdaLN provides 

allocation : it selects, per layer and per channel, how strongly a pretrained transformation contributes for a given question. Recurrence provides recomputation : it reevaluates that transformation at the state produced by the preceding iteration. They also differ in granularity: AdaLN acts per channel and continuously, by (13), whereas a loop acts per span and in discrete steps. This view also clarifies why a uniform, unconditioned loop can be a poor way to reuse a pretrained hierarchy. Different blocks implement transformations at different levels of abstraction, different questions need not benefit from repeating all of them equally, and a single loop count imposes the same coarse allocation on every block in the recurrent span. AdaLN’s conditioning removes that constraint. The frozen blocks and the question-derived modula-tion are both reused at every iteration, so the loop body is a single shared operator; but because the modulation depends on q, that operator becomes a family indexed by the question: 

xt+1 = L xt; {αℓ(q), β ℓ(q), γ ℓ(q)}ℓ∈B 

, (15) where L denotes the shared loop body and B its set of blocks. Training learns the map q 7 →{αℓ(q), β ℓ(q), γ ℓ(q)} rather than a single update rule, so different questions emphasize different feature dimensions of the same pretrained span. The updates applied at successive iterations differ because, while L is fixed across t, its input xt is not. 

Takeaway. AdaLN and looping are not competing approximations to the same operation. AdaLN selects and reweights the pretrained visual computation appropriate for a question, which locally resembles a channel-wise, fractional allocation of effective depth. Looping supplies what a sin-gle modulated pass cannot: repeated evaluation at the evolving visual state, including the higher-order term JF F of (14) associated with propagation and feedback. Their combination realizes task-conditioned iterative visual computation under final-answer supervision alone.

## E VIS RO: T ASKS , G ENERATION , AND EVALUATION 

VisRO is a procedural dataset and generation pipeline with 24 visual reasoning tasks spanning count-ing, discrimination, connectivity, geometric transformations, and spatial reasoning. This appendix describes the tasks, their difficulty controls, and the data generation and evaluation protocol. E.1 TASK COVERAGE AND DEFINITIONS 

Table 9 groups the 24 tasks by the visual operations they require: discrimination and grouping, paths and connectivity, planar transformations and composition, and spatial transformations and structure. Figures 3, 11, and 12 together illustrate all tasks. Questions specify both the requested operation and the answer format. The table and figures illustrate representative formats. Some difficulty extensions use different outputs, including per-line counts, queried-cell values, and constructive tangram assemblies. E.2 DIFFICULTY LEVELS AND OOD E VALUATION 

Difficulty levels. Training and in-distribution (ID) evaluation use four task-specific difficulty lev-els: easy, medium, hard, and xhard. ID evaluation uses independently generated test examples from these four levels. Difficulty is controlled through generation parameters such as object count, over-lap, path length, crossings, and distractor similarity. Table 10 compares xhard, the highest ID level, with the OOD settings for representative tasks. Tier names describe task-specific settings and do not imply equal difficulty across tasks. Difficulty controls also account for the rendered problem. For example, near-matching icons in-crease comparison demands, while cube-net distractors can differ in face orientation. These changes must preserve the visual cues needed to determine the answer. Rendering resolutions are task-specific and specified by the generator configurations. 

The OOD split. Out-of-distribution (OOD) examples introduce task-specific increases in scene complexity or shifts in generation rules and visual appearance. For example, 3D block counting increases the base dimensions, maximum height, and minimum cube count (Table 10). Flow Free holds out a path-construction program and rendering or material combinations; Metro Map uses four held-out rendering styles. Both tasks retain their ID question families in the OOD split. Figure 3 shows representative ID and OOD examples. E.3 DATA GENERATION AND EVALUATION 

Data generation and validation. Each generator samples and renders a structured scene, then constructs a question and computes its answer from the scene. Task-specific checks assess whether the image contains the information needed to answer the question and whether the answer agrees with the scene. Scene structure and spatial annotations support data generation and validation. AdaLoop training uses only images, questions, and final answers. 

Scoring. Scoring follows each task’s answer format, without an LLM judge. Labels, counts, and sequences use exact match after task-specific parsing and normalization. Ordered outputs must match the complete reference sequence, while pair sets are compared independently of their listing order. Constructive tangram answers are checked for allowed rotations, complete target coverage, and absence of overlap. Any valid assembly is accepted.
