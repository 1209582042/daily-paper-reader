ABSTRACT 

Do vision-language models use depth information to judge which object is closer, or rely on image-plane cues? We investigate this question through controlled depth–cue conflicts, representation analysis, and interventions. Among the many image-plane cues relevant to depth perception, we focus on apparent size and vertical position. We compare depth judgments under cue-consistent and cue-conflict conditions in both rendered scenes and real-world photographs. Across model families and scales, depth judgments systematically shift toward misleading cues. Yet the correct depth order remains decodable from the queried objects’ visual representations, including when the model answers incorrectly. Image and category controls support interpreting this signal as image-dependent depth information beyond the two target cues. Strengthening the model’s own depth readout through object-level write-back can correct conflict decisions without updating model parameters or using ground-truth depth annotations. Interventions show that the effect of this correction depends on the direction and target tokens of the written signal, while its strength varies across architectures and domains. Together, these findings reveal a mismatch between a model’s ability to represent depth and use this information when deciding the answer. VLMs can retain useful depth information while allowing competing image-plane cues to exert greater influence on their answers. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Picture a scene of you sitting in a living room. A mug is on the table in front of you, and a sofa is across the table. In the real world, any three-year-old can tell that the mug is closer to you than the sofa. However, for state-of-the-art vision-language models (VLMs), that answer is less reliable, despite their strength elsewhere (Fu et al., 2024). This paper asks why this happens and answers with a diagnosis. Humans often use statistical shortcuts, or cues, about the front-behind relationship between objects. For example, nearer things tend to sit lower in the frame and look larger. We hypothesize that VLMs may do the same. These cues are genuinely predictive in natural 2D images and are directly available from the image plane. A learner rewarded on ordinary photographs therefore has every statistical reason to use them. We ask whether VLMs actually use depth information when judging depth relations, or instead rely on these simpler 2D cues. To separate depth from projected-size and vertical-position cues, we construct two controlled diagnos-tic settings. The first uses rendered scenes in which size and vertical position are decorrelated from true depth. The second uses real-world photographs with sensor depth, from which we select cases where these 2D cues point to the wrong answer. Across eleven VLMs from five families, including multiple model scales and a checkpoint fine-tuned for spatial reasoning, performance drops sharply when the cues conflict with true depth. Neither model scaling nor spatial fine-tuning eliminates this pattern. When depth and 2D cues disagree, VLM behavior systematically follows the cues. Code is available anonymously. 1

2 RELATED WORK 

Shortcuts in spatial reasoning. Min et al. (2026) show that VLM depth judgments are sensitive to perspective cues, particularly vertical position. We examine the internal basis of this behavior by controlling apparent size and vertical position and testing whether depth remains represented when answers follow conflicting cues. 

Spatial representations and their influence. Liao et al. (2026) distinguish grounded, prior-driven, and inverted depth representations through probing and visual ablations. Our focus is the competition between image-dependent depth information and misleading image-plane cues. Kang et al. (2026) identify linear spatial directions and show that steering them can alter spatial judgments, including depth. We instead read depth from queried-object contrasts and use self-oriented write-back to test its influence under controlled cue conflicts. Following causal representation analyses (Belrose et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023a; Rimsky et al., 2024), our controls distinguish decodability from intervention effects on the final answer. 

Improving spatial reasoning. SpatialVLM (Chen et al., 2024) and SpatialRGPT (Cheng et al., 2024) improve spatial competence through additional supervision, while contrastive decoding meth-ods modify generation without updating model weights (Li et al., 2023b; Leng et al., 2024). 3.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

We study pairwise depth judgments in which a model decides which of two objects is closer to the camera. Real-world images contain many depth-relevant cues, such as texture and shadow; we focus on apparent size and vertical position. Our rendered setting controls their agreement with true depth, while our natural-image setting selects corresponding conflicts using sensor-derived depth order. We vary the relation of these cues to depth to create three conditions. In the consistent condition, both cues agree with true depth. In the conflict condition, the designated cue favors the opposite depth ordering. In the decorrelated condition, we balance cue directions across depth labels, so that the closer object is equally likely to appear larger or smaller, and higher or lower, than the farther object. VLMs’ behaviors are quantified with depth accuracy , which is the fraction of examples where the model answers the truly closer object, with 50% corresponding to random guessing. We first create rendered scenes where depth, object size, and vertical position can be set independently. We manipulate the physical size and image-plane position of the two objects while preserving their depth ordering, allowing each cue to agree with or oppose depth. Since object shape and perspective projection can make scene parameters differ from their final appearance, we measure projected size and vertical position directly from the rendered pixels and adjust each scene until the intended relation is satisfied. We also construct a real-world-image setting using photographs and sensor depth from SUN RGB-D (Song et al., 2015). Sensor depth is used as a pseudo-label to determine which object is truly closer, while projected size and vertical position are measured directly from the image. For each object pair, we compare the ordering implied by each 2D cue with the sensor-depth ordering. A pair is consistent when the cue favors the truly closer object and conflict when it favors the farther one. This yields an analogous depth–cue comparison in natural photographs, although other scene properties are not independently controlled. 3.2 VLM S FOLLOW 2D C UES WHEN THEY CONFLICT WITH DEPTH 

A consistent pattern emerges across all five model families. Depth accuracy drops sharply when either 2D cue conflicts with true depth (Figure 3). On rendered scenes, Qwen3-VL-8B falls to 42.8% under vertical conflict, and LLaVA-OneVision-1.5-8B to 7.4%, both below chance. Spatial fine-tuning does not remove the effect. SpaceThinker, a Qwen2.5-VL-3B checkpoint trained on synthetic spatial reasoning traces (Remyx AI, 2026), reaches only 51.8% and 32.4% under the two conflict conditions. Vertical position is generally the stronger cue. Its conflict produces a larger accuracy drop than size conflict in eight of eleven models, spanning all five families. The same pattern appears in natural photographs, though with smaller gaps because naturally occurring conflicts are less extreme than those in the rendered setting. Together, these results show that when 2D cues disagree with true depth, model predictions are systematically pulled toward the cue-supported answer. Classifier accuracy and intervention sign-readout accuracy are therefore evaluated separately. Full fitting details, including the regularization strength and stan-dardization procedure, are provided in Appendix E. The object-level readout is highly accurate. On Qwen3-VL-8B, probe accuracy remains above 97.3% at every language layer above the embeddings on rendered scenes and ranges from 82.9% to 91.7% on real-world photographs. We test whether this readout reflects visual depth information rather than the two target cues or object-category priors, such as expecting a cup to be closer than a sofa regardless of the image. On the decorrelated calibration split, neither apparent size nor vertical 5position alone predicts depth order. With the depth direction, question, and object-token positions held fixed, replacing the image sharply reduces depth-readout accuracy (Table 4). On real-image vertical conflicts, Qwen3-VL-8B falls from 82.6% with the original image to 40.3% with a grey image. Mismatched-image controls show a similar reduction. The readout also generalizes to unseen category combinations. On the real-world image held-out fold, Qwen3-VL-8B achieves 84.4% depth sign accuracy on pairs whose category combination is absent from the probe’s training fold, compared with 84.5% overall. These results show that the readout depends on the current image, even when apparent size or vertical position favors the wrong answer. Where depth becomes linearly accessible differs across architectures. On Qwen3-VL-8B, the pre-projection probe reaches 74.0%, and accuracy is already 99.7% at the language-model input embed-dings and remains near ceiling thereafter. On Gemma-4-12B, the corresponding readouts are 62.5% and 66.0%, with near-ceiling accuracy emerging after the first decoder block. We train two separate probes on the same answer states. The closer-object probe predicts which of the two queried objects is truly closer. The answer-letter probe predicts the letter assigned to that object in the answer options, regardless of whether the model itself answers correctly. For example, if the mug is closer than the sofa, the closer-object target remains the mug when the options are swapped from “A: mug, B: sofa” to “A: sofa, B: mug”, whereas the answer-letter target changes from “A” to “B”. This comparison tests whether the correct depth order is more accessible as an object relation or as an answer choice. On Render, probes struggle to identify the closer object from the answer state, with accuracy generally near chance. Yet probes can predict the correct answer letter from the same states: accuracy rises sharply around middle layers and peaks at 91.3% in Qwen and 85.3% in OneVision. The same distinction appears on Real, although answer-letter accuracy is lower, peaking at 75.3% and 67.2%, respectively. Thus, the answer state can support accurate decoding of the correct option even when the closer object’s identity is difficult to decode directly. Depth order is more consistently accessible from object-representation differences in both models. Decoding remains high throughout the stack on Render, while on Real it peaks in the middle layers and declines toward the final layers. Decodability does not guarantee a correct answer. In a separate evaluation on rendered vertical-conflict examples, Qwen’s object-level depth readout is correct on 99.0% of examples, while the model answers correctly on only 42.8%. Only the queried object tokens are directly modified at the selected layer. We test the intervention on Qwen3-VL-8B in both domains using 1) cue-avoidant prompting (in-structing the model not to use cues), 2) predicted depth input (providing external depth map to the model), 3) random directions of the v vector, and alternative write locations (answer token vs. object contrast) (Table 2). Neither explicitly discouraging the two cues nor supplying a predicted depth map reproduces the gains from object-level write-back. The effect is also specific to the internal representation being manipulated. Replacing the depth direction with random directions leaves conflict performance near the unmodified model. Writing the same depth direction at the answer token rather than antisymmetrically at the two queried objects likewise produces little selective improvement. 6 LIMITATIONS 

Our analysis focuses on pairwise depth judgments and two common image-plane cues, apparent size and vertical position. Real-world images contain additional depth-relevant signals that cannot be isolated as cleanly as in controlled rendering, which may contribute to the smaller effects observed there. The causal effect also varies across model families and domains. Extending the analysis to additional depth cues and more general spatial reasoning is an important direction for future work. 9REFERENCES

D DATA AND EVALUATION 

D.1 RENDERED SCENES 

Each scene contains two primitives in a room with a checkerboard floor. Depth, angular size, and image position are sampled independently, while physical size and world position are derived from them. The camera light, depth fog, floor, and walls retain valid depth cues. Objects appear on opposite sides of the image and do not occlude each other. The dataset has 500 scenes in each of four splits. In the consistent split, the closer object is larger and lower. In size conflict, it is smaller and vertical position is neutralized. In vertical conflict, it is higher and size is neutralized. In the decorrelated split, both cues are independent of depth. A scene is rerendered up to four times until the neutralized objects differ by at most 3% in mask area or one pixel in centroid row, as appropriate. Images are 448 × 448 . Depth ranges from 2.5 to 7.0 m with at least 1.0 m separation. Angular size ranges from 0.055 to 0.115 with at least 0.02 separation, and vertical position from −0.18 to 0.18 

with at least 0.08 separation. Masks below 60 pixels are rejected. The base seed is 0 and split i uses seed 1000 i.D.2 REAL PHOTOGRAPHS 

We rasterize SUN RGB-D polygons (Song et al., 2015) and define object depth as the median sensor depth inside each mask. A pair must differ by at least 0.4 m and a depth factor of 1.20. Box IoU is at most 5%; each mask covers at least 1500 pixels and at most one quarter of the image; neither box is within five pixels of the border. Each category appears only once in the image, and each image contributes at most three pairs. The neutralized cue is sign-balanced within each conflict split. D.3 EVALUATION 

We score each example by comparing the next-token logits for “A” and “B” at the final input position. Option order is randomized per item and recorded in the manifest. Each input contains one user message with the image and question, formatted using the model’s chat template with a generation prompt and no system prompt. 12 For rendered scenes, the question is: “Two objects are visible in the image. Which object is closer to the camera? A: the {a}. B: the {b}. Answer with only the letter A or B.” For SUN RGB-D, it is: “Which object is closer to the camera, the {a} or the {b}? A: the {a}. B: the {b}. Answer with only the letter A or B.” The harness tokenizes the bare answer strings without special tokens and verifies that each is a single token. For SUN RGB-D, object depth is the median refined sensor depth within the instance polygon; apparent size is mask area and vertical position is centroid row. Cue-consistent pairs require the closer object to have at least 1.25 times the area and appear lower by at least 7% of image height. Size-conflict pairs require its area ratio to be at most 1/1.25 , with vertical separation at most 6% of image height. Vertical-conflict pairs require it to appear higher by at least 7%, with an area ratio in [1 /1.35 , 1.35] . The decorrelated split balances the four combinations of size and vertical cue directions. The final splits contain 305 consistent pairs, 209 size-conflict pairs, 144 vertical-conflict pairs , and 289 decorrelated pairs. The accompanying image-level manifest records all 947 pairs, including scene identifiers, image and mask paths, object identities, depth order, option assignment, and measured cue values. Patch shuffling permutes visual-token positions while retaining local appearance information, in-cluding fog and shading. The grey-image control retains the original object masks and therefore preserves the positions used for token selection. Neither control removes every possible source of spatial information. E.1 PROBE FITTING 

We fit logistic regression using mean binary cross-entropy plus 10 −3∥w∥22, leaving the intercept unpenalized. This corresponds to λ = 2 × 10 −3 under the convention in Eq. 3. Regularization is fixed across models and domains rather than selected by search. Features are standardized using training-fold statistics, with standard deviations floored at 10 −6. Optimization uses full-batch L-BFGS for at most 200 iterations from zero initialization. Probe folds use an 80/20 random split; the deployed direction uses seed 0. An object token is selected when at least one quarter of its grid cell is covered by the segmentation mask. If no cell passes this threshold, we use the cell with the largest overlap. Object representations are unweighted means of the selected tokens. At each layer, the probe predicts depth order from d = cA − cB . Standardization is fitted on the training fold only. The classifier uses the standardized features and intercept in Eq. 2; the intervention uses the unit raw-space direction v and the offset-free sign of v⊤d. Classifier accuracy, sign accuracy, and answer accuracy are therefore different measurements. Probes are fitted on decorrelated calibration scenes. For real-image interventions, v is refitted on separate development images using Depth Anything 3 pseudo-depth (Lin et al., 2025). Evaluation uses sensor depth. The category-only baseline predicts the majority depth ordering for each unordered category pair observed in the probe’s training fold. Unseen category pairs receive a score of 0.5. On SUN RGB-D, this baseline covers 22 of 209 size-conflict pairs and 8 of 144 vertical-conflict pairs. We therefore also evaluate the depth readout on unseen category combinations. On the held-out probe fold, these account for approximately 92% of pairs, and Qwen3-VL-8B achieves 84.4% sign accuracy on them versus 84.5% overall, using the fixed intervention layer ( ℓ = 8 ). Grey-image and mismatched-image controls test image dependence. Fixed masks leave position information in the grey-image control, and patch shuffling preserves local fog and shading.
