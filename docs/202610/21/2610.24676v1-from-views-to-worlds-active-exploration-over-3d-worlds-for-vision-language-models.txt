Abstract 

Vision-language models (VLMs) have achieved remarkable progress in 2D visual understanding tasks, yet the passive view-centric perception paradigm limits their capacity for 3D spatial reasoning. Such reasoning often requires actively acquiring spatial evidence beyond the currently visible views, such as camera poses and cross-view spatial relationships, to infer 3D relationships that are not explicitly represented in 2D images. However, existing methods either lack active exploration or merely provide additional views during inference, without explicitly acquiring such spatial evidence beyond 2D images. In this paper, we propose View-to-World (V2W), an active exploration framework that enables VLMs to actively explore 3D worlds and lifts VLMs from passive view-centric perception to active world-centric spatial reasoning. Given a spatial reasoning task with multi-view images, V2W first reconstructs an explicit 3D world with geometry, camera poses, and language-grounded semantics, and exposes it to VLMs through visual and linguistic interfaces. Through multi-turn interaction with these interfaces, VLMs iteratively acquire spatial evidence from the constructed 3D world, enabling active world-centric spatial reasoning. V2W can be integrated with existing VLMs in a training-free manner and can be further enhanced by optimizing the exploration policy via reinforcement learning. Experiments on spatial mental modeling benchmarks demonstrate that V2W substantially improves VLM baselines in the training-free setting and achieves state-of-the-art performance with the learned exploration policy, highlighting the importance of active exploration for spatial intelligence. First, critical spatial evidence may be occluded or absent from the currently visible views, which requires active viewpoint changes beyond fixed-view inputs. Second, even with more viewpoints, 2D images alone fail to explicitly represent the cross-view 3D information, such as relative camera poses, cross-view entity correspondences, and object-level 3D spatial relations. Addressing these limitations requires models to actively acquire evidence over a coherent 3D world representation that integrates geometric, visual, and semantic information. However, most existing methods still rely on passively perceiving 2D inputs for spatial reasoning. Some methods attempt to incorporate geometric priors into VLMs, including encoding 3D geometry into the representation space [19, 24, 45, 65, 85, 88], fusing implicit 3D information at the feature level [17, 33, 80, 84], and introducing 3D priors through distillation [6, 25, 60] or latent reasoning [12, 31, 72]. Although these methods provide VLMs with additional 3D priors, they mainly improve the internal representation capability rather than provide VLMs with an explicitly interactive and actively explorable 3D world, and therefore still belong to the passive perception from fixed-view input. More recent methods [5, 70, 83] have further explored active perception by providing VLMs with additional 2D observations. Nevertheless, these methods remain largely view-centric, as additional observations are still presented as 2D views and lack cross-view 3D information needed for spatial reasoning. Such 3D information is not explicitly represented in 2D views, but is grounded in the underlying 3D world. We refer to reasoning over such information as world-centric spatial reasoning. Therefore, advancing VLMs toward 3D spatial reasoning requires moving from passive view-centric perception to active world-centric spatial reasoning, as shown in Fig. 1. In this paper, we propose View-to-World (V2W), a model-agnostic framework that enables VLMs to actively explore over an explicit 3D world representation. Given multi-view images, V2W first recovers scene geometry and camera poses with off-the-shelf 3D foundation models and incorporates language-grounded semantics to build a geometrically consistent, semantically aligned, and language-queryable 3D world. We denote it as a VLM-compatible 3D world, since the geometry, camera poses, and semantic evidence are exposed to VLMs through images and natural language rather than raw 3D representations. We design two interfaces to achieve the exposure: a visual interface that provides 2D observations from newly explored viewpoints and a linguistic interface that converts geometric and semantic information from the 3D world into explicit natural-language cues. V2W enables VLMs to actively explore the constructed 3D world through visual and linguistic interfaces and progressively acquire and integrate spatial evidence via multi-turn interaction, thereby performing spatial reasoning in an active world-centric manner. Experiments on spatial mental modeling benchmarks show that V2W improves existing VLMs in a training-free manner, revealing their inherent capability for active exploration. However, we also find that smaller VLMs often struggle to perform effective active exploration, exhibiting insufficient or uninformative exploration. To this end, we further propose to train the exploration policy through reinforcement learning, thereby improving the active exploration capability. With the learned exploration policy, V2W achieves state-of-the-2art performance, substantially outperforming both baseline methods and proprietary commercial models (e.g., surpassing GPT-4o by 48.5 percentage points on MindCube-Tiny), demonstrating the importance of active exploration capability for spatial reasoning. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We construct a VLM-compatible 3D world representation that is geometrically consistent, semantically aligned, and language-queryable, providing a 3D environment that lifts VLMs from view-centric perception to world-centric spatial reasoning. • We propose V2W, an active exploration framework that enables VLMs to interact with the constructed 3D world through visual and linguistic interfaces and progressively acquire world-grounded evidence via multi-turn exploration. • We introduce reinforcement learning to train the exploration policy of V2W, enabling more effective evidence acquisition from the constructed 3D world. • Experiments on spatial mental modeling benchmarks demonstrate that V2W substantially im-proves VLM baselines in the training-free setting and achieves state-of-the-art performance with the learned exploration policy. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Vision-Language Models for Spatial Reasoning 

Spatial reasoning is essential for intelligent perception and interaction. It allows agents to represent, organize, and transform objects and their spatial relationships in 3D environments [22, 27, 28, 75]. However, recent studies show that existing VLMs still struggle with spatial reasoning [1, 9, 10, 23, 48, 68, 69, 74, 78, 79, 81]. Prior efforts have attempted to improve the capability of VLMs mainly in two ways. The first line of work injects 3D priors into VLM representations. These methods encode 3D geometry into the representation space [19, 24, 45, 65, 85, 88], fuse implicit 3D information at the feature level [17, 33, 80, 84], or introduce 3D knowledge through distillation [6, 25, 60] or latent reasoning [12, 31, 72]. While effective, they often require large-scale aligned data and costly training, and they do not provide explicit geometric constraints. The second line of work provides 3D information externally in the VLM context [11, 16, 20, 38, 83], which offers a more direct and model-agnostic way to improve 3D spatial awareness. However, a key challenge is how to help VLMs understand and use this external 3D information effectively. In this work, we also provide 3D information externally, but we construct a VLM-compatible 3D world that supports active exploration, allowing VLMs to make better use of 3D information. 

2.2 3D Foundation Models and Scene Understanding 

Recent feed-forward reconstruction models [30, 35, 57, 61–63] have made rapid progress in 3D modeling. These models can recover 3D geometry from multi-view images and accurately estimate camera parameters and other 3D information. Their strong generalization ability further positions them as 3D foundation models. In parallel, visual foundation models, such as the SAM series [7, 29, 47], have demonstrated strong capabilities in scene parsing and understanding. Recent studies [32, 56, 58, 59, 87] further integrate 3D and visual foundation models to support semantic 3D scene understanding. While the above work mainly focuses on 3D representation and perception-oriented tasks, our work investigates how VLMs can benefit from such foundation models to improve spatial reasoning capability. 

2.3 Vision-Language Models with Active Interaction 

Recent VLMs have advanced from direct reasoning over given inputs to interactive reasoning [37, 71, 73]. Representative methods allow models to interact with external resources during inference through multi-turn reasoning, including tool-augmented VLMs [50, 53, 66, 67, 86] and visual programming methods [21, 38, 39, 54]. Such interaction reflects the active exploration capability of VLMs [52, 64, 89]. This capability is particularly important for spatial reasoning, since understanding 3D space often requires integrating observations from different viewpoints and reasoning about regions beyond the current field of view. Recent works have introduced active exploration into spatial reasoning through novel-view rendering [83] or generation [5, 70]. 3 Methods 

3.1 Overview 

Given multi-view images I = {Ii}Ni=1 and a spatial reasoning question q, V2W aims to produce the answer by enabling a VLM to actively explore an explicit 3D world. As shown in Fig. 2, V2W first constructs a VLM-compatible 3D world W by reconstructing the 3D scene and lifting question-relevant object semantics into 3D space (Sec. 3.2). Since VLMs only take images and text as input, W

is exposed through two interfaces: a visual interface that renders novel-view images, and a linguistic interface that provides textual cues about visible objects and relative camera poses. During inference, V2W formulates active exploration as a multi-turn interaction via function call (Sec. 3.3). We further train the exploration policy with supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning (Sec. 3.4), which strengthens the VLM’s ability to actively explore the 3D world. 

3.2 VLM-compatible 3D World Construction 

Active exploration requires an explicit 3D world that serves as an interactive environment for existing VLMs. Since VLMs typically take images and natural language as inputs, raw 3D representations such as point clouds, meshes, or 3D Gaussian Splatting cannot be directly used. Meanwhile, spatial reasoning often requires both geometry and language-grounded semantics. Therefore, we construct a VLM-compatible 3D world that integrates scene geometry, camera poses, and object-level semantics, and exposes them to VLMs through visual and linguistic interfaces. These interfaces convert explicit 3D information into rendered observations and natural-language cues, allowing VLMs to acquire spatial evidence without modifying their architectures.

Visual and Linguistic Interfaces. The constructed 3D representation contains both geometry and object-level semantics, whereas existing VLMs typically take only images and text as input. To bridge this modality gap, we expose the 3D world through two VLM-compatible interfaces: the visual and linguistic interfaces. The visual interface returns rendered images from queried camera poses, while the linguistic interface returns textual cues derived from object-level semantics and spatial relations. Given a queried camera pose, the visual interface renders the corresponding novel-view image as the visual observation for the VLM. To support object-level textual cues in the linguistic interface, we internally render object masks in the queried view. Specifically, for a target pose Eτ , the mask of object o is obtained by projecting its associated 3D points into the target view:

The linguistic interface also describes spatial relations between camera poses. Given two camera poses and the gravity direction, we project the camera centers onto the horizontal plane to describe their relative translation as forward, backward, left, right, or diagonal, and compare their orientations to describe the relative rotation as turning left or right. Together, the visual and linguistic interfaces provide the VLM with image observations and textual spatial cues through its native input modalities. 

3.3 Active Exploration via Function Calling 

The visual and linguistic interfaces of the constructed VLM-compatible 3D world are formulated as callable functions, enabling V2W to perform active exploration as a structured tool-calling process. Each function corresponds to a predefined exploration action. During inference, the VLM calls a function with specific arguments, receives the returned images or text from the visual or linguistic interface, and then decides whether to continue exploration or produce the final answer. 

Exploration Action Space. V2W defines an exploration action space with two types of actions: viewpoint exploration and 3D information query, as shown in Fig. 2. Viewpoint exploration actions change the current camera pose to acquire additional visual evidence from novel views, including horizontal rotations around the gravity direction, i.e., turn left, turn right, and turn around; translations on the horizontal plane, i.e., move forward and move backward; and view-centered adjustments, i.e., zoom in, zoom out, turn up, and turn down. After each viewpoint exploration action, the 3D world returns a rendered novel-view image through the visual interface. 3D information query actions provide textual spatial cues from the 3D world through the linguistic interface. These cues include visible object descriptions, which summarize the object categories observed from the explored viewpoint, and relative camera pose descriptions, which return the relative translation and rotation between two existing views in the gravity-aligned coordinate system. 5Multi-turn Interaction. 3.4 Exploration Policy Learning 

Although V2W can be applied to existing VLMs without training, smaller VLMs often struggle with active exploration. They may terminate prematurely, repeat uninformative actions, or select actions weakly related to the question. This suggests that effective exploration also depends on the VLM’s exploration policy. We therefore optimize this policy using supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL). 

Supervised Fine-tuning. To learn an effective exploration policy, we first perform supervised fine-tuning on demonstration trajectories before reinforcement learning. This stage teaches the model the function-calling format, valid exploration actions, and how to reason over accumulated evidence. We use Qwen3-VL-235B [2] as the teacher model to generate trajectories. Instead of generating a full trajectory at once, the teacher interacts with the VLM-compatible 3D world turn by turn. At each turn, it predicts the next reasoning step and function call based on the current interaction history, receives the returned visual or linguistic evidence, and appends the new step to the trajectory. After the interaction ends, we concatenate the reasoning steps, function calls, returned evidence, and final answer into a complete trajectory. Each trajectory follows the format: <think> reasoning 

</think> <action> function call </action> · · · <think> reasoning </think> <answer> final answer </answer> . Here, each <think> segment contains intermediate reasoning, each <action> 

segment specifies a function call with its arguments, and the final <answer> segment gives the answer. We filter out invalid trajectories with malformed function calls, failed executions, or incorrect answers, and fine-tune the VLM on the filtered data using a standard next-token prediction objective. 

Reinforcement Learning. After supervised fine-tuning, we optimize the exploration policy with reinforcement learning. We adopt Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) [49] as the policy optimization algorithm. For reward design, we first introduce three trajectory-level rewards, including a format reward that enforces valid function calls and answer formats, an answer reward that evaluates whether the final prediction is correct, and an exploration reward that checks whether the trajectory contains valid exploration actions. Since trajectory-level rewards provide only coarse supervision, we further introduce step-wise rewards to guide intermediate exploration decisions. For each training sample, we use Qwen3-VL-235B [2] to pre-generate question-conditioned preferred exploration actions. During training, the policy receives a step-wise matching reward when its selected action is consistent with the preferred action at each interaction step. We also apply a step-wise action penalty for failed function calls and uninformative actions, such as actions that lead to entirely empty new views from the visual interface. 4.1 Experimental Settings Benchmarks. Spatial mental modeling refers to internal representations of unseen space that support reasoning about layout, perspective, and motion [27, 28]. It is closely aligned with our goal of evaluating whether VLMs can perform human-like spatial reasoning from limited observations. We therefore evaluate our framework on two related benchmarks: (1) MindCube contains 21,154 questions over 3,268 images and focuses on inferring spatial layouts from limited observations, particularly through what-if questions that require mental simulation. Its training set contains 10K samples, and the commonly used MindCube-Tiny evaluation set includes 1,050 questions, comprising 600 Among, 200 Rotation, and 250 Around questions. Following prior work [5, 12, 38, 74], we primarily evaluate our method on MindCube-Tiny, and the overall accuracy is weighted by the number of questions in each category. (2) SAT similarly evaluates human-like spatial reasoning from sparse visual observations across diverse categories, including Egocentric Movement, Object Movement, Allocentric Perspective, Goal Aiming, and Action Consequence. It also includes questions that require mental simulation. Its training set contains 175K questions across 22K procedurally generated scenes, and the SAT-val evaluation set contains 4,000 questions. We evaluate our method on SAT-val, and the overall accuracy is averaged equally across the five categories, following the original SAT protocol [48]. 

Baselines. Our framework can be applied to existing VLMs in a training-free manner and also supports further optimization of the VLM exploration policy via RL. We therefore evaluate our framework under both training-free and training-based experimental settings. The baselines are grouped into four categories: (1) generalist open-source VLMs, including Qwen series: Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct [3], Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct [2], Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-Instruct [2], and the latest Qwen3.5-9B [46]; (2) proprietary commercial models, e.g. GPT-4o [41] and GPT-4.1 series [43]; (3) training-free spatial reasoning methods, e.g. pySpatial [38]; and (4) specialized spatial reasoning VLMs, e.g. 3DThinker [12] and SpatialDreamer [5], representing training-based methods. 

Implementation Details. We use off-the-shelf foundation models, including 3D foundation model Depth Anything 3 [35] and the segmentation model SAM 3 [7], to construct the VLM-compatible 3D world. During exploration, we set the maximum number of interaction turns to 4. For the training-free setting, all evaluations are conducted on a single H200 GPU. Since our framework is model-agnostic, we adapt V2W with different base models to align with the underlying VLMs used by different baselines. For the training-based setting, we train our method separately on the training set of each benchmark using 8 H200 GPUs. To ensure consistency with the main comparison methods [5, 12], we also adopt Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct [3] as the base model. In the SFT stage, we fine-tune all model parameters for one epoch using a learning rate of 1.0 × 10 −5, a cosine learning-rate schedule with a 0.1 warmup ratio, and a batch size of 64. In the RL stage, we implement the exploration policy training with the verl [51] framework, using a learning rate of 1.0 × 10 −6 and a batch size of 128. 

4.2 Quantitative Results 

We evaluate V2W under both training-free and training-based settings. First, in the training-free setting, we assess whether V2W can serve as a plug-and-play framework to directly enhance the spatial reasoning ability of existing VLMs without additional training. Second, in the training-based setting, we further investigate whether a learned exploration policy can lead to stronger spatial reasoning capabilities. Specifically, we evaluate training-free methods on MindCube-Tiny, and evaluate training-based methods on both MindCube-Tiny and SAT-val.

To address this limitation, we further optimize the exploration policy via reinforcement learning, enabling VLMs to better conduct multi-step exploration and aggregate spatial evidence. 4.4 Ablation Study 

In Table 3, we conduct ablation studies on MindCube-Tiny with Qwen2.5-VL-7B [3] as the base model (row 0). The results identify two factors underlying the performance gains. First, the world interface improves performance across different training stages. In the training-free setting, introducing both visual and linguistic interfaces improves the overall accuracy from 34.5% to 43.8% (rows 0-3). In the training-based setting, the full interface consistently improves overall accuracy across different training configurations, yielding gains of 6.1 points in the SFT-only configuration (rows 4-5), 14.4 points in the RL-only configuration (rows 6-8), and 16.0 points in the two-stage SFT-then-RL configuration (rows 9-12). These results highlight the importance of active world-centric spatial reasoning enabled by the world interface. Second, RL more effectively elicits active exploration than SFT. Without the world interface, RL-only achieves 69.5% overall accuracy, outperforming SFT-only at 65.2% (rows 6 vs. 4). With the full interface, RL-only further reaches 83.9%, substantially higher than SFT-only at 71.3% (rows 8 vs. 5). Nevertheless, the best performance is achieved by SFT-then-RL with the full world interface, reaching 90.8% overall accuracy (row 12). This suggests that SFT provides a useful imitation-learning initialization, while RL further optimizes the exploration policy, allowing the model to better exploit the world interface for active world-centric spatial reasoning. 5 Conclusion 

In this work, we propose View-to-World (V2W), an active exploration framework that enables VLMs to explore explicit 3D worlds for spatial reasoning. Unlike existing VLMs that passively perceive fixed-view inputs in a view-centric manner, V2W allows models to actively acquire and reason over 3D information from the underlying world, a paradigm that we refer to as active world-centric spatial reasoning. V2W constructs a VLM-compatible 3D world from sparse input views and exposes its geometry, camera poses, and language-grounded semantics through visual and linguistic interfaces, enabling VLMs to progressively gather world-grounded evidence through multi-turn interaction. Experiments show that V2W improves VLMs in a training-free setting, validating the effectiveness of active exploration over explicit 3D worlds. By further optimizing the exploration policy with reinforcement learning, V2W learns more effective exploration strategies and achieves state-of-the-art performance on spatial mental modeling benchmarks. These results highlight active world-centric spatial reasoning as a promising direction for advancing the spatial intelligence of VLMs.

We further analyze the inference efficiency of the training-based V2W (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) on MindCube-Tiny. As shown in Table 6, the overall average inference time is approximately 6.93 sec-onds per sample. The main computational cost comes from 3D world construction, which takes about 5 seconds, while the interface responses introduce only negligible overhead. The model response itself takes less than 2 seconds, indicating that V2W does not rely on lengthy free-form reasoning during inference. Considering that V2W substantially outperforms models of comparable scale in spatial reasoning performance, such a runtime is acceptable. Moreover, free-form reasoning in larger models may introduce substantial inference overhead. For instance, Qwen3-VL-235B [2] with CoT in Table 1 can require a thinking time comparable to, or even longer than, the total inference time of our entire pipeline, while still achieving lower performance. Rather than relying on lengthy free-form reasoning, V2W learns a more effective active exploration strategy through reinforcement learning, reducing redundant reasoning steps and unnecessary deliberation. Notably, we do not introduce any reward related to reasoning length, since we empirically find that longer reasoning does not necessarily improve the learned exploration strategy or the final answer accuracy. Instead of relying on long reasoning traces, V2W improves performance through more efficient exploration over the constructed 3D world, thereby achieving stronger spatial reasoning ability with acceptable inference efficiency. 

A.4 Additional Qualitative Results 

We provide additional qualitative results to further demonstrate the flexibility and effectiveness of V2W. As shown in Fig. 4, training-free V2W can be readily adapted to different base models, such as Qwen2.5-VL-7B [3], Qwen3-VL-8B [2], and GPT-4.1-mini [43], enabling them to actively explore 3D worlds without additional training. However, these models often require relatively long reasoning chains before or after exploration to determine the next action or integrate information obtained through the interface. In contrast, Fig. 5 shows that training-based V2W performs efficient multi-round exploration in the constructed 3D worlds with minimal redundant reasoning. When the query is explicit, it can accurately identify informative exploration actions without unnecessary steps. We attribute this efficient exploration strategy to reinforcement learning, which encourages the model to learn a more concise and goal-oriented exploration policy.
