## Abstract 

Spatial intelligence requires agents to actively acquire vi-sual evidence and integrate observations into a coher-ent representation of the three-dimensional world. How-ever, spatial question answering provides limited evi-dence of whether models can construct accurate met-ric maps through autonomous exploration. We introduce 

SpatialScout , a benchmark for evaluating vision-language model (VLM) agents through active object-level 3D map-ping. SpatialScout comprises 80 diverse indoor scenes containing 3,140 target objects. Given an object inven-tory, RGB observations, and camera calibration and poses, agents control a camera to explore each scene and estimate object centers in a shared coordinate frame. This task re-quires sustained coordination of viewpoint selection, cross-view association, geometric estimation, and map revision under bounded interaction budgets. Our evaluation jointly measures localization accuracy and target coverage, using object-scale overlap scores that penalize missing predic-tions. Experiments with frontier VLMs reveal substantial limitations: the strongest evaluated model, Claude Opus 5.5, achieves a mean overlap of 41.71%, with only 47.23% of targets exceeding an IoU threshold of 0.5 and 3.25% ex-ceeding 0.9. These results expose a substantial gap between recovering coarse object layouts and constructing com-plete, precise metric maps. SpatialScout provides a quan-titative testbed for developing agents that build reliable 3D spatial representations through active perception. Code is available at https://github.com/JesseZZZZZ/SpatialScout.

Evaluating this entire process is central to assessing the spa-tial capabilities of vision-language models (VLMs). Ex-isting spatial intelligence benchmarks widely adopt image-and video-based question answering (QA) to assess count-ing, direction, distance, and spatial relations [50, 55]. Recent benchmarks such as ESI-Bench [21] and S 3-Bench [51] introduce active in-teraction into spatial QA, while Theory of Space [62] ex-amines the construction of discrete cognitive maps. Our key advance is to make the construction of object maps in con-tinuous 3D space through active interaction a direct eval-uation objective for spatial intelligence. Models must au-tonomously acquire visual evidence and recover the 3D po-sitions of a target set in a common coordinate frame. This task couples active perception, cross-view association, ge-ometric estimation, and scene modeling: the current spa-tial estimate determines what to observe next, while the se-lected viewpoint shapes the evidence available thereafter. Through spatial construction beyond QA, feedback-driven active exploration, and end-to-end task evaluation, we ex-amine whether VLMs can transform local observations into accurate and complete metric spatial representations. To this end, we introduce SpatialScout , a benchmark for evaluating the spatial intelligence of vision-language agents through active object-level 3D mapping. Starting from an initial observation and a target inventory, an agent au-tonomously explores the scene and progressively constructs a 3D map of target object centers in a common coordinate frame. The benchmark has three core features: 1⃝ Ex-plicit spatial construction. Models must output a set of 3D object positions, enabling quantitative evaluation of spatial representation quality through localization accuracy and tar-get coverage. 2⃝ Closed-loop active exploration. Models select subsequent viewpoints based on existing observations and the current map, then update position estimates using new evidence, forming a feedback loop between the active acquisition of perceptual evidence and spatial understand-ing. 3⃝ Long-horizon end-to-end capability evaluation. 

Models coordinate observation planning, object recogni-tion, cross-view association, geometric estimation, and map updates across multiple interaction steps, with overall per-formance measured by final map quality. This sustained coordination goes beyond QA tasks with short reasoning chains and better reflects real-world spatial understanding. SpatialScout comprises 80 indoor scenes and 3,140 tar-get objects, with 30–40 targets per scene, spanning di-verse spatial layouts, object scales, and repeated category instances. The benchmark provides a unified set of camera-control and geometric assistance tools, along with camera intrinsics and poses, and requires models to infer object centers from autonomously acquired visual evidence. Eval-uation jointly measures geometric estimation error, local-ization accuracy relative to object scale, and target cover-age. We also record map updates under a limited interaction budget to analyze how object-level spatial representations emerge as exploration proceeds. We evaluate frontier VLMs, including GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5. The results show that models can re-cover parts of object layouts through autonomous explo-ration, but comprehensive coverage and precise localization remain challenging. Models differ substantially in target omissions and center localization accuracy, while the pro-portion of targets meeting stringent scale-relative accuracy criteria remains low. These findings indicate that recog-nizing objects or making coarse spatial judgments does not necessarily translate into constructing reliable metric maps. By exposing these limitations through measurable localiza-tion and coverage metrics, SpatialScout provides a unified benchmark for studying how models construct and under-stand the 3D world through autonomous interaction.

2.2. Benchmark Construction 

Scene diversity. Explicit geometric annotations and re-producible configurations support object-level scene evalu-ation [32, 64]. We assemble 80 indoor scenes rendered with SAPIEN [52] through ManiSkill3 [46]: 32 iTHOR [28], 12 RoboTHOR [8], 10 ArchitecTHOR [9], 6 Replica-CAD [45], and 30 ProcTHOR [9] scenes. The catalog spans individual rooms and apartments, retaining the source lay-outs and target placements; each source scene appears once. 

Complexity. Each scene contributes 30–40 targets. The catalog contains 12 scenes with 35 targets, and 68 with 40, totaling 3,410 instances. The target-region radius ranges from 1.95 to 9.23 m. Across the catalog, 1,489 targets (43.3%) belong to equivalence groups with multiple in-stances in the same scene. Target count controls inventory scale, while spatial extent and repeated categories vary the localization and association demands. 

Selection and initialization. Preparation prioritizes can-didates with known semantic categories, then orders them by distance to the initial camera, using identifier order to break ties. This rule aligns the inventory with recognizable categories and concentrates targets around the exploration origin. Candidates must have rendered geometry, and each scene must supply the requested target count. ## 3. Evaluation 

3.1. Evaluation Protocol 

Setup We evaluate seven models on a common set of 80 catalog scenes, with one episode per model–scene pair: GPT-6 Astra [38], GPT-6 Sol [39], Claude Opus 5.5 [2], Claude Sonnet 5.5 [3], Kimi-K3 [47], Qwen3.8-Max [41], and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash [11]. We impose no explicit limit on output or reasoning tokens. 

Interaction All agents use the ten tools in Table 2 through a shared interface [56]. Starting from one RGB image, the agent queries the inventory and calibration, acquires further views, and submits or revises object centers (see Fig. 3). Each accepted submission replaces the previous estimate for that identifier. The validator checks identifiers, finite coordinates, and the public bound ∥bpi∥2 ≤ r + 0 .5 m. All agents receive the same task prompt and tool descriptions. The prompt requests visually supported estimates and, for multi-view episodes, encourages submission after the first two views, followed by additions and revisions during ex-ploration. Submissions return the current accepted map, which the agent can also inspect with an empty batch. 

Camera movement Relative translations follow the cur-rent horizontal forward/right directions and world vertical; positive yaw is counterclockwise viewed from above, and positive pitch looks upward. New RGB observations are acquired through explicit captures; movement bounds are specified in Appendix A.

3.2. Evaluation Metrics 

We report mean center error (meters) after minimum-distance one-to-one matching within equivalence groups [7], together with inventory coverage. For scale-sensitive localization, we compute 3D IoU by trans-lating each candidate target’s reference box to the predicted center. Mean IoU uses the distance assignment; hit rates use a separate within-group hit-maximizing assignment at each strict threshold τ ∈ { 0, 0.5, 0.9}. Overlap scores are pooled across scenes and reported over all targets (omis-sions count as zero) and submitted targets. Appendix B.2 gives the scoring details. 

## 4. Results 

4.1. Quantitative Results 

Localization and completeness. Table 3 reports object-micro overlap scores for the common 80-scene compar-ison. Claude Opus 5.5 achieves an all-target mean IoU of 41.71%, compared with 33.16% for GPT-6 Astra; their submitted-object means are 46.69% and 41.76%, respec-tively. Claude Opus 5.5 has higher mean overlap both over the full inventory and among accepted estimates. The de-crease from submitted-object to all-target mean IoU shows how omissions reduce each model’s overall mapping score. 

Localization precision. Positive-overlap recall is 77.36% for Claude Opus 5.5 and 67.79% for GPT-6 Astra. At IoU 

> 0.5, recall is 47.23% and 35.15%, respectively; at IoU 

> 0.9, it is 3.25% and 1.62%. These differences distinguish coarse placement from precise center alignment relative to object extent. High-IoU matches remain uncommon even among submitted objects, with hit rates at IoU > 0.9 of 3.64% for Claude Opus 5.5 and 2.09% for GPT-6 Astra.

## 6. Related Work 

Agentic inverse graphics and scene construction. 

IR3D-Bench evaluates scene understanding by reconstruct-ing a scene from a supplied image [32]. BlenderGym studies code-based graphics editing, including how gener-ation, verification, and their compute allocation affect per-formance [17]. BlenderAlchemy combines edit genera-tion with visual evaluation [22], while VIGA and Blender-Bench examine interleaved execution, inspection, memory, and exploratory editing [60]. SceneActBench evaluates ex-ecutable multi-object outputs across several tasks; its recon-struction task builds furniture meshes in an initially empty scene from supplied calibrated views [64]. These works es-tablish construction and editing as meaningful tests of vi-sual understanding. Our setting emphasizes acquisition of new evidence from a hidden target environment and inspec-tion and revision of an explicit object-center map. 

Active reconstruction and next-best-view planning. 

Selecting informative observations has a substantial his-tory in three-dimensional perception. SCONE predicts sur-face coverage gain [18], and MACARONS couples RGB-based mapping with online coverage anticipation [19]. GenNBV learns a generalizable view-selection policy [6]. NARUTO, ActiveSplat, and ActiveGAMER integrate ex-ploration with neural scene mapping [5, 12, 29]; Fish-erRF uses information-based view selection for radiance fields [24]. More directly, AIR-Embodied uses multimodal reasoning to plan reconstruction actions [40]. AREA3D combines geometric perception and vision-language guid-ance, and introduces an evaluation covering object- and scene-level active reconstruction [54]. SightCAD connects feasible view selection to executable CAD generation for scene-embedded objects [4]. These systems provide im-portant planning and reconstruction references. Our bench-mark compares general-purpose agents under shared tools and observation and model-call budgets, recording how their evidence acquisition and map updates interact. 

Spatial and embodied benchmarks. VSI-Bench and SITE assess spatial reasoning from supplied visual ob-servations [50, 55]. OpenEQA includes both episodic-memory and active-exploration settings [34], and Explore until Confident studies exploration and stopping for em-bodied question answering [42]. E3VS-Bench evaluates viewpoint-dependent evidence acquisition through camera control [43]; ESI-Bench covers spatial tasks that require ac-tive observation and interaction [21]. S 3-Bench combines streaming spatial questions with memory and additional exploration [51]. EmbodiedBench evaluates visual agents across multiple environments and action levels [56]. Our evaluation complements these tasks by scoring the explicit object-center map maintained over an episode. Compared with QA pairs, our tasks require VLM agents to output the precise 3D coordinates of the objects. 

Memory, geometry tools, and process diagnosis. Con-ceptFusion and ConceptGraphs build semantically rich three-dimensional representations through multimodal fu-sion and cross-view association [16, 23]. 3D-Mem uses scene memory for embodied exploration and reason-ing [57], while SpaMEM separates perceptual grounding from temporal state maintenance [30]. DUSt3R and VGGT provide geometric inference tools [48, 49], and NeRF and Gaussian splatting offer scene representations for render-ing and reconstruction [26, 37]. Alongside BlenderGym, 3DCodeBench investigates inference-time computation in graphics tasks [14, 17]. Building on these perspectives, we control image acquisition and model calls with separate budgets. Shared tools, inventory-only reference policies, and replay of accepted-map updates support comparisons of final localization. 

Long-horizon agentic benchmarks. Long-horizon agen-tic tasks require sustained reasoning and repeated tool use, often over multiple interaction rounds, and remain a ma-jor challenge for deploying LLM-based systems in real-world settings. AgentBench and GAIA evaluate interac-tive decision-making and general-purpose tool use [33, 36]. WebShop and WebArena focus on web interaction, while VisualWebArena and OSWorld extend evaluation to vi-sually grounded web and desktop environments [27, 53, 58, 65]. The τ -bench and DySQL-Bench examine tool use in settings with evolving user interactions [44, 59]. SWE-bench, Terminal-Bench, NL2Repo, and RepoZero as-sess repository repair, command-line problem solving, and repository generation using executable checks [10, 25, 35, 63]. AgenticRAGTracer complements these benchmarks with hop-level diagnosis of agentic retrieval processes [61]. SpatialScout is one of the benchmarks evaluating the spatial understanding and long-horizon capability of VLM agents. 7. Conclusion 

We introduced SpatialScout, a benchmark for evaluating the spatial intelligence of VLM agents through active object-level 3D mapping. By requiring agents to au-tonomously select viewpoints, integrate visual evidence, and estimate object positions in a shared coordinate frame, SpatialScout evaluates the complete process of constructing metric spatial representations. Our experiments reveal that frontier VLMs can recover partial scene layouts but still struggle to achieve both comprehensive coverage and precise localization. Even the strongest evaluated model attains a mean IoU of 41.71%, with only 3.25% of targets exceeding an IoU of 0.9. These findings highlight the difficulty of translating visual observations into reliable geometric estimates through sustained interaction. Spa-tialScout provides a testbed for advancing active perception, cross-view reasoning, and persistent spatial memory, sup-porting the development of agents capable of building accurate and complete representations of 3D environments.

## C. Limitations 

SpatialScout currently relies on data generated using a physics simulation engine. Although our results support the conclusions drawn in this work, simulated environments may not fully capture the complexity and variability of real-world scenes.
