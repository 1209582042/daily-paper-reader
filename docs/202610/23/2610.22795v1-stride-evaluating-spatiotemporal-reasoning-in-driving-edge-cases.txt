ABSTRACT 

In autonomous driving, accurately perceiving surrounding objects is far from enough: failing to understand how they move and interact over time can lead to dangerous decisions. Evaluating vision-language models (VLMs) for au-tonomous driving therefore requires assessing their ability to connect spatial observations of past events with future consequences. Existing benchmarks usu-ally oversimplify the problems by assessing driving edge cases only through basic perceptual questions without spatiotemporal considerations, or evaluating tempo-ral understanding under simple scenarios, leaving the spatiotemporal reasoning required in complex driving scenarios insufficiently tested. To address this gap, we introduce STRIDE ( Spatio Temporal Reasoning In Driving Edge Cases), an object-centric benchmark for evaluating spatiotemporal reasoning in long-tail and safety-critical scenarios. STRIDE defines six task families: spatial perception, spatial understanding, temporal memory, temporal extrapolation, trajectory pre-diction, and scene-context awareness. It pairs multiple-choice questions covering a broad range of spatiotemporal judgments with related free-form visual ques-tion answering (VQA) that examines the same capabilities through detailed scene explanations and justifications of driving decisions. We curate STRIDE by pri-oritizing scenes with unusual object behaviors and complex traffic interactions, constructing reference answers from structured dataset annotations through au-tomated procedures, and applying limited language model assistance alongside human verification to guarantee annotation quality. Evaluation of 12 proprietary, open-weight, and driving-specialized models reveals substantial gaps: even GPT-6-Astra only achieves the highest average multiple-choice accuracy of 43.4%, compared with a random-choice baseline of approximately 20%. Our analysis further reveals that strong performance in describing driving scenes does not nec-essarily translate to accurate safety-critical judgments, and that models struggle more with reconstructing object motion than recalling past positions. These in-sights highlight STRIDE’s benchmarking and diagnostic value for uncovering spatiotemporal driving limitations that aggregate VQA performance can conceal. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Vision-language models (VLMs) demonstrate remarkable reasoning capabilities (Hwang et al., 2025; Chi et al., 2026), yet they still face significant challenges in the core demands of autonomous driving – spatiotemporal perception, behavior prediction, and safe decision-making (Schwarting et al., 2018; Li et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026). These limitations are particularly consequential in safety-critical scenarios that are underrepresented in driving data dominated by routine situations (Xu et al., 2026). Resolving these complex, long-tail hazards re-quires continuous spatiotemporal reasoning: the ability to integrate past observations with shifting spatial dynamics to anticipate emerging risks. Existing benchmarks for autonomous driving do not adequately assess whether VLMs can integrate spatial observations with past events to anticipate future consequences in challenging driving sce-narios. Edge-case benchmarks often emphasize recognition questions that require limited reasoning about motion history or future interactions (Chen et al., 2025; Chi et al., 2026), while temporal rea-soning benchmarks typically focus on comparatively simple scenarios, such as stationary vehicles and constant-speed straight driving (Cannons et al., 2025; Fruhwirth-Reisinger et al., 2026). As a result, VLMs’ ability to reason jointly about spatial and temporal relationships in scenarios involv-ing unusual behaviors and complex interactions remains insufficiently tested. These gaps motivate a unified benchmark that evaluates spatiotemporal reasoning in such scenarios. To address this gap, we introduce STRIDE (Figure 1), an object-centric benchmark for evaluating spatiotemporal reasoning in long-tail and safety-critical driving scenarios. We prioritize scenes with unusual object behaviors and complex traffic interactions, requiring models to locate nearby objects, reconstruct their past motion, and anticipate how their future behavior may affect the ego vehicle. To support fine-grained diagnosis of spatiotemporal reasoning, the benchmark comprises 39 manually designed question templates organized into six complementary task families: spatial perception, spatial understanding, temporal memory, temporal extrapolation, trajectory prediction, and scene-context awareness. Each evaluation combines object-centric temporal context with multiple-choice questions (MCQs) that test intermediate judgments and open-ended questions (OEQs) that assess their integration into driving explanations and decisions. Our evaluation of 12 proprietary, open-weight, and driving-specialized models highlights the diffi-culty of STRIDE. Qwen3.6-27B (Qwen Team, 2026) and GPT-6-Astra both achieve substantially lower MCQ accuracy than on the previous temporal reasoning benchmark (Fruhwirth-Reisinger et al., 2026) (Figure 1). Fine-grained analysis reveals difficulty connecting observations across frames into a coherent account of object dynamics: GPT-5.5 achieves 72% accuracy for previous relative position but only 18% for recent motion patterns. Our evaluation also reveals gaps that ag-gregate scores can conceal. High scenario explanation VQA scores coexist with weak judgments about drivable space and specific risks, suggesting that broad scene caption tasks may partly rely on generic driving knowledge. This concern echoes findings that models retain substantial performance even when visual inputs are replaced with black images (Xie et al., 2025). 2 RELATED WORK 

Driving VQA Benchmarks Driving vision-language benchmarks evaluate scene understanding through questions about road entities, vehicle behavior, and driving context (Caesar et al., 2020; Marcu et al., 2024). Beyond individual questions, subsequent efforts connect perception with pre-diction and planning through graph-structured QA, annotated reasoning chains, and counterfactual supervision (Sima et al., 2024; Nie et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). Spatially grounded evalua-tion has also expanded toward multi-view understanding and explicit 3D localization (Wang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026). However, reliability studies show that favorable QA or planning scores can reflect textual shortcuts or reliance on ego-state information, motivating closer examination of the capabilities underlying model performance (Li et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2025). 

Spatiotemporal and Long-Tail Evaluation Recent benchmarks increasingly assess temporal understanding through multi-frame traffic interactions, action recognition, event ordering, and tem-poral localization (Cannons et al., 2025; Fruhwirth-Reisinger et al., 2026). In parallel, corner-case and long-tail datasets broaden evaluation beyond routine driving, covering unusual objects, un-structured roads, and challenging agent interactions through perception, driving suggestions, and trajectory tasks (Chen et al., 2025; Chi et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026). These efforts establish comple-mentary foundations for evaluating temporal capabilities and scenario difficulty. 

3 STRIDE 

The STRIDE benchmark is designed to evaluate the spatiotemporal reasoning of autonomous-driving agents in long-tail scenarios with reliable ground truth and interpretable assessment. As summarized in Table 1, STRIDE combines spatial and temporal reasoning, edge-case evaluation, explicit grounding, and ego-trajectory prediction with both multiple-choice and open-ended QA. 3.1 TASK DEFINITION 

Autonomous driving is an anticipatory decision-making problem: an agent must understand the pre-vious and current scene, predict how it may evolve, and take preventive action before developing risks lead to dangerous interactions. Such anticipation of recognizing emerging conflicts and de-termining when and how the ego vehicle should respond depends jointly on spatial and temporal reasoning. Object positions, distances, and available driving space establish the current conditions for action, while changes across consecutive frames reveal motion trends and support predictions of future interactions. Motivated by these requirements, we organize STRIDE around the comple-mentary dimensions of space and time .3STRIDE 

Figure 2: Data generation pipeline of STRIDE. From left to right, dataset annotations and extracted signals are converted into structured labels. Manually designed hybrid format pair these labels with five-frame context to generate linked MCQ answers and verbalized OEQ references, followed by human review and curation. 

Space This dimension is divided into two hierarchical levels of increasing difficulty, from in-dividual object location to multi-object relation. Spatial perception evaluates basic object-level properties that are directly observable from the scene, including relative position, distance, direction of motion, and lane occupancy. Spatial understanding builds on these observations and evaluates whether models can interpret the driving significance of these spatial relationships. Tasks include determining whether an object restricts the drivable region, affects lane-change feasibility, constrains the ego vehicle’s motion, or poses a potential risk. 

Time This dimension extends this representation of the current scene backward and forward in time. Taking the current spatial configuration as the reference state, temporal memory evaluates whether a model can reconstruct how that state developed from previous observations. This in-cludes retrieving previously visible or disappeared objects, earlier object locations, occlusion and reappearance, and recent motion trends. In the opposite direction, temporal extrapolation eval-uates whether the model can anticipate how the current scene may evolve, including future object motion, lane occupancy, collision timing, and likely subsequent events. Together, these two task families test whether a model can connect the present scene to its recent history and possible future. 

Driving Context Above tasks primarily assess capabilities through questions grounded in a target object. To account for the ego vehicle’s own motion and the broader environment in which object interactions occur, we therefore include the evaluation on trajectory prediction and scene-context awareness . Trajectory tasks assess the ego vehicle’s past and future motion. Scene-context tasks assess environmental conditions, road structure, and surrounding activity that provide context for interpreting individual objects and their interactions. 3.2 DATA CONSTRUCTION 

Data Source and Annotation We construct STRIDE from two complementary sources: 

nuScenes (Caesar et al., 2020) and WOD-E2E (Xu et al., 2026). As seen in Figure 2(a), each of them provides different annotations. nuScenes is a multimodal autonomous-driving dataset com-prising 1,000 driving scenes of 20 seconds each, with six-camera surround-view coverage and 3D object annotations. We complement nuScenes with WOD-E2E, a more recent dataset curated to emphasize challenging long-tail driving scenarios, including unusual agent interactions, cut-ins, construction zones, encounters with vulnerable road users, un-expected obstacles, and other safety-critical situations. For WOD-E2E, we supplement the released annotations with object-centric signals derived through detection, tracking, and geometric distance estimation, as detailed in Appendix A.1. 

Data Sampling Strategy To keep evaluation computationally manageable while preserving sce-nario diversity, we construct a compact evaluation set through template-based curation. For each question template, we sample up to 50 valid instances from scenes in which the required annotations and visual evidence are available. Since the two source datasets provide different annotation types and scenario characteristics, not every template is applicable to both datasets due to missing anno-tations required. To address this issue and ensure that our ground truth is trustworthy, each template is instantiated only on the dataset or datasets that provide sufficient information to support reliable ground-truth generation. Within the eligible candidates, we prioritize informative and challenging examples using a combination of complementary signals. These include scene-intrinsic factors such as traffic-participant density and interactions, weather and visibility conditions, hazardous elements, unusual agent behavior, and overall scene complexity such as involvement of vulnerable road users. 

Hybrid Evaluation STRIDE combines multiple-choice and free-form VQA to assess shared spa-tial and temporal capabilities through complementary response formats (Figure 2(b)). Within each task family, questions are aligned around shared scene attributes, with the two formats evaluated independently. For example, the spatial understanding family pairs multiple-choice questions on physical constraints, available drivable space, lane-change feasibility, and risk with the free-form question: Describe the overall spatial situation of the ego vehicle in the scene, including existing physical constraints, available drivable space, risk level, and feasible maneuvers. The multiple-choice questions serve as intermediate judgments of individual attributes, which are then integrated in the linked open-ended question for a broader description or justification of the driving situation. This alignment supports complementary analysis of judgment accuracy and explanatory quality. 

Quality Control After construction, we remove question types lacking sufficient source annota-tions for reliable ground truth. OEQ references are further refined using human scores and VLM assisted error analysis to correct unsupported or incomplete answers. After human review of the selected subset, we replace weak references with stronger model-assisted answers based on human scoring of the reference answer and the corresponding model response generated by GPT-5.5. 4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

We evaluate STRIDE with a diverse set of VLMs spanning proprietary general-purpose models including GPT-6-Astra, Gemini-3.1-Pro, and Claude-Sonnet-5, open-weight models of different scales including Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking, Qwen3.6-27B, and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, and driving-specialized models including Dolphins and UniAD. This model suite allows us to compare both broad multimodal capabilities and domain-specific expertise under a unified evaluation protocol. 

Implementation Details All models are evaluated using the same benchmark inputs and prompt-ing protocol whenever their interfaces permit. Open-weight models are downloaded from 

HuggingFace and evaluated using vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) on NVIDIA A6000 GPUs, while proprietary models are accessed through their official APIs. Unless otherwise stated, we use deter-ministic or near-deterministic decoding settings to reduce evaluation variance. More details about experimental settings can be found in Appendix C. 

Evaluation Metrics. For multiple-choice tasks, we report accuracy. For open-ended questions, we report the mean score assigned by a vision-language judge on a 0–10 scale. For trajectory pre-diction, each predicted waypoint is compared with the ground-truth waypoint at the same timestep by Euclidean ( ℓ2) distance. We report two summaries of that distance: Average Displacement Error (ADE), the mean ℓ2 over timesteps, and Final Displacement Error (FDE), the ℓ2 at the final timestep. 4.2 MAIN RESULTS 

Table 2 reveals substantial limitations even in the strongest evaluated models. GPT-6-Astra achieves the highest MCQ accuracy on both nuScenes and Waymo E2E, yet reaches only 45.5% and 41.2%, respectively. Although above the random-choice baselines of 19.8% and 20.3%, these results leave more than half of the questions incorrectly answered, highlighting persistent difficulties with the benchmark’s spatial and temporal judgments. For OEQ evaluation, performance also varies across response formats and tasks. GPT-5.5 leads on nuScenes with a score of 6.68, while Claude-Sonnet-5 achieves the highest score on Waymo E2E at 7.01, closely followed by GPT-5.5 at 7.00. Meanwhile, the driving expert UniAD 2.0 excels at trajectory prediction on nuScenes, achieving an ADE/FDE of 0.56/0.79, compared with 0.60/0.98 for GPT-6-Astra and 0.65/0.97 for GPT-5.5. In summary, these results expose a key limitation: strong aggregate VQA performance does not establish reli-able spatiotemporal judgment or superior trajectory prediction. Improving the consistency of these capabilities remains an important direction for future work. 

5 ANALYSIS 

We examine the models in Table 2 across four capability categories using the selected per-task results in Table 3. Complete results can be found in Appendix Tables 9 and 10. Scene and risk interpretation are discussed under Section 5.2, while future-trajectory tasks are included under Section 5.4. 5.1 SPATIAL PERCEPTION 

Performance varies across spatial attributes and response formats. On nuScenes, the best relative-position and distance accuracies are 62% and 64%, compared with 38% for trajectory direction and 44% for occlusion state. Integrated spatial descriptions remain difficult: GPT-5.5 leads with 3.66/10, while Gemini-3.1-Pro scores 2.10/10 despite leading distance MCQs. On Waymo E2E, distance MCQ and OEQ leaders also differ: Gemini-3.6-Flash achieves 62% accuracy, whereas Claude-Sonnet-5 scores 6.28/10. Success on individual spatial attributes therefore does not consistently extend across response formats or to descriptions integrating multiple relations. 7STRIDE Output-format compliance further affects measured performance. For Qwen3.6-27B on nuScenes SP-3, only 34% of responses are of the valid format; accuracy is 22% overall and 64.7% among the 17 parsed responses. This suggests insufficient instruction-following, although performance on this selected subset cannot establish accuracy on the full task. 5.2 SPATIAL UNDERSTANDING 

Our benchmark further reveals that strong scene descriptions do not ensure reliable driving judg-ments. On nuScenes, GPT-5.5 scores 9.22/10 for scene description but only 22% for both drivable-region identification and lane-change clearance. Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking also exceeds all proprietary models on physical constraints, reaching 42%. On Waymo E2E, GPT-5.5 scores 8.08/10 for scene/risk/action explanations, alongside accuracies of 44%, 28%, and 48% for scene classification, risk identification, and preferred action. Fine-grained prompts and task-specific scoring criteria in STRIDE make these comparisons more informative by separating broad explanatory quality from precise driving judgments. As a result, differences be-tween OEQ and MCQ performance should not be interpreted as contradictory answers to equivalent questions, but rather as complementary evidence about distinct aspects of model capability. 5.3 TEMPORAL MEMORY 

Recalling earlier positions is easier than reconstructing motion on nuScenes. GPT-5.5 and GPT-5.6-Sol both achieve 72% for previous object position but only 18% and 16%, respectively, for recent motion, near its 16.7% chance baseline. Memory of currently occluded objects is also limited: Qwen3.6-35B-A3B leads at 36%, against a 25% baseline. These results suggest difficulty integrating observations into a consistent motion history. Waymo E2E shows a less uniform pattern: Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B-Thinking achieves 32% for re-cent motion but only 10% for previous position. Past ego-maneuver classification is comparatively stronger, reaching 74% for Claude-Sonnet-5. Strong ego-motion summaries therefore do not nec-essarily translate into reliable object-level memory, particularly when models must maintain object identities and reconstruct motion histories across multiple frames. 5.4 TEMPORAL EXTRAPOLATION 

Anticipating behavior and its driving consequences remains difficult. On nuScenes, the best future-object-motion accuracy is 40%, and the best scene-evolution OEQ score is 6.98/10. On Waymo E2E, the best accuracies for future object motion, ego response, and the next traffic event are 36%, 34%, and 34%. Trajectory evaluation also yields different rankings: Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B-Thinking achieves the lowest Waymo ADE/FDE of 11.83/6.18 despite weaker aggregate VQA performance. Parsing success is essential to interpreting displacement errors. On nuScenes TRJ-6, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B parses successfully on only 16% of examples; ADE is 0.32 on valid outputs but 8.19 overall. Dolphins parses 18% of outputs on each nuScenes waypoint task and none on Waymo. Its finite Waymo FDE of 8.60 reflects the stationary-at-origin fallback for invalid outputs, rather than successful waypoint generation. Conversely, GPT-5.5 achieves 100% parsing on Waymo TRJ-5 but still incurs an ADE of 23.44. Format and trajectory accuracy therefore require joint assessment. 5.5 A L OOK INTO FAILURE CASES OF GPT-6-A STRA 

Although GPT-6-Astra achieves the highest aggregate MCQ accuracy on both nuScenes and Waymo E2E split of STRIDE, its fine-grained results reveal several remaining limitations, most of which apply to all the VLMs we have tested. 

Weak spatial reasoning and temporal tracking across frames GPT-6-Astra performs substan-tially better at identifying an object’s state at a particular moment than at reconstructing how that state changes over time. As shown in Figure 4(a), it achieves 70% accuracy for previous object position (TM-2) on nuScenes, but only 20% for recent motion pattern (TM-3), a gap of 50 percent-age points. 6 CONCLUSION 

We present STRIDE, a benchmark designed to evaluate whether vision-language models can con-nect past observations, current spatial relationships, and future consequences in challenging driving scenarios. By combining object-centric temporal prompting, six complementary task families, and 9STRIDE linked MCQ–OEQ evaluation grounded in structured driving annotations, STRIDE enables fine-grained diagnosis beyond broad scene-description quality. Across 12 proprietary, open-weight, and driving-specialized models, our results show that current systems remain far from reliable: even the strongest model answers fewer than half of the multiple-choice questions correctly, while de-tailed analysis reveals pronounced gaps between explanation quality and safety-critical judgments, and between recalling past object positions and reconstructing motion history. Together, these find-ings highlight the need to evaluate driving VLMs through precise spatial and temporal judgments in addition to fluent scene-level explanations. We hope STRIDE will serve as a shared evaluation resource for the research community, supporting systematic comparison of models and diagnosis of their spatiotemporal limitations in challenging driving scenarios. Each instance contains five consecutive 360 ◦ views formed by stitching synchronized camera images and sampled at 2Hz, with four context frames followed by a query frame in which a bounding box identifies the target for object-centric questions. To support capability-level analysis, we manually design question templates aligned with the task families introduced in Section 3.1, each specify-ing the question wording and expected answer structure. For example, spatial-perception templates cover trajectory direction, relative position to the ego vehicle, distance, lane occupancy, occlusion state, and ground contact. Representative multiple-choice templates include What is the spatial re-lationship between <obj> and the ego vehicle? and What is the approximate distance between 

<obj> and the ego vehicle? , with discretized answer choices derived from the underlying an-notations. For each five-frame temporal unit, we select a valid target object and instantiate the corresponding template with the highlighted traffic participant in the query frame. A template is applied only when the required visual evidence and structured annotations are available. The same object may support multiple complementary questions, allowing us to probe different aspects of its spatial and temporal state while maintaining a consistent visual context. Free-response templates combine related capabilities into a more comprehensive query, such as describing the target ob-ject’s relative position, direction of motion, and approximate distance to the ego vehicle in a single response. A.3 OBJECT -CENTRIC TEMPORAL PROMPTING 

As illustrated in Figure 2(d), our prompting scheme is designed to reflect the inherently dynamic nature of autonomous driving. We first construct a unified 360 ◦ representation of each driving obser-vation by stitching the synchronized multi-camera views provided by the source datasets. This gives the model a more complete view of the surrounding environment rather than restricting evaluation to a single front-facing camera. While some existing VQA benchmarks evaluate models using a single static image, such a setting is insufficient for many driving-related questions. Unlike some other embodied agent tasks, autonomous driving is fundamentally temporal: properties such as whether a nearby vehicle is moving, accelerating, approaching the ego vehicle, or changing lanes often cannot be determined reliably from an isolated frame. We therefore exploit the short driving clips pro-vided by both source datasets and standardize their sampling rate to 2Hz. Each benchmark instance contains five consecutive observations separated by 0.5s. The first four frames provide short-term temporal context, while the fifth serves as the query frame. For object-centric questions, we visually highlight the target traffic participant with a bounding box in the query frame and ask the model to reason about that object using both its current spatial configuration and the preceding temporal con-text. Other questions may instead target broader scene-level properties. This formulation constrains the model’s attention to a clearly defined subject while preserving the temporal evidence needed 14 STRIDE for reasoning about motion and interaction. Compared with broad scene-level prompting, it also reduces ambiguity in both the question and its expected answer, making model behavior easier to interpret and evaluate consistently. A.4 TRUSTWORTHY GROUND TRUTH GENERATION 

To provide reliable supervision, STRIDE grounds its question templates in original dataset anno-tations, restricting their scope to questions supported by observable or annotated (Figure 2(e)). Ground truth is derived hierarchically from geometric signals to driving semantics: annotated lon-gitudinal and lateral offsets between the target object and ego vehicle are converted into distance and bearing, then mapped to labels such as front , front-left , and rear-right . Higher-level labels, such as risk, combine relative position, distance, motion direction, speed, and interaction with the ego trajectory. For example, an object directly ahead, approaching at high relative speed within a collision-relevant region, receives a higher risk label than one moving away. For free-form ques-tions, a language model acts solely as a verbalizer , composing the source annotations, derived labels, and corresponding multiple-choice answers into natural-language references. This pipeline supports scalable automated construction while minimizing unsupported or hallucinated supervision and pre-serving traceability from each answer to the underlying driving data. A.5 EVALUATION WITH LVLM J UDGES 

Traditional machine translation metrics such as BLEU (Papineni et al., 2002) and METEOR (Baner-jee & Lavie, 2005) primarily measure lexical or surface-level similarity and are therefore poorly suited for evaluating open-ended driving reasoning, where semantically equivalent answers may differ substantially in wording (Nie et al., 2024). We instead employ a scalable model-based judge for free-response evaluation, providing the judge with the visual observations, question, reference answer, and model response so that correctness can be assessed in the context of the underlying driving scene. We note that the choice of judge itself requires validation. For example, Chen et al. (2025) re-port that a text-only LLM judge achieved stronger human alignment than the vision-language judge evaluated in CODA-LM. Rather than assuming that multimodal judging is inherently superior, we compare conventional NLP metrics, text-only LLM judges, and LVLM judges against human anno-tations on a held-out subset of STRIDE. As shown in Table 4, contemporary LVLM judges achieve strong agreement with human evaluation while retaining direct access to the visual evidence re-quired by our spatial and temporal questions. We evaluate both proprietary and open-weight judges and adopt Qwen3-VL-32B as our default judge due to its competitive human alignment and greater reproducibility and accessibility.
