Voxel Programming

Language models’ abilities to interpret tasks and generate programs offer a promising basis for 3D
spatial reasoning. Yet following spatial instructions in 3D scenes requires programs to construct ge-
ometric references that language leaves implicit. In this work, we introduce VoxProg, which formu-
lates 3D object placement through voxel programming: a language model translates task interpreta-
tions into executable programs over local voxel views of scene surfaces. Because these views index
surface geometry on regular grids with explicit coordinates and neighborhood relations, an evidence
program can isolate task-relevant regions and boundaries directly from scene meshes, and a down-
stream pose program can measure the resulting geometry to compute a target SE(3) pose. Visual
and numerical feedback from execution lets the model inspect intermediate selections and candi-
date placements to iteratively revise its code. To evaluate open-ended placement across functional
use, geometric fit, and contextual arrangements, we introduce the 71-task VoxProg-Bench bench-
mark along with an evaluation suite pairing perceptual Intent Alignment and 3D Physical Validity.
Across VoxProg-Bench and external benchmarks, VoxProg consistently outperforms relation-based,
keypoint-based, and mesh-based baselines.

Introduction
Natural-language spatial instructions often specify goals whose geometric requirements must be inferred
from the scene (Song et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2024). Placing a bottle inside an open
display holder requires identifying the holder’s receiving structure and determining how the bottle should
be positioned and oriented to fit within it. Similarly, extending a row of cups requires inferring the direction
and spacing of the existing arrangement to derive the next placement target. Here, the geometric references
that define the goal are themselves outcomes of spatial computation over the 3D scene.
Existing approaches are limited by both the task requirements their interfaces can express and the geometric
computations their representations support. Predefined task interfaces couple task coverage to task-specific
implementations, limiting generalization to new spatial requirements (Gao et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2023b;
Ichter et al., 2023). Geometric representations present a different difficulty: sparse keypoints can leave
regions and local surface shapes under-specified (Huang et al., 2025b; Hua et al., 2025; Manuelli et al.,
2022; Sundaresan et al., 2023; Fang et al., 2024), while raw meshes require programs to organize irregular
surface elements into the regions and references a task needs. Open-ended spatial reasoning therefore
requires both programmatic computation that adapts to the instruction and a spatial representation that
exposes scene geometry directly to code.
To this end, we formulate voxel programming, in which a language model expresses its understanding of a
spatial task as a program that constructs and operates on local voxel views (Fig. 1). By discretizing object
surfaces onto regular grids at task-appropriate resolutions, local voxel views expose spatial coordinates
and neighborhood relations directly as array operations. Generated programs can index these grids to
isolate contact regions, extract boundaries, and measure local extent and orientation, turning a uniform grid
representation into task-specific geometric references for pose computation.

and execution feedback. Starting from the instruction and raycast visual anchors, the model first writes an

where object contours govern fit and clearance. Ablation studies further show that constructing explicit
geometric evidence before pose synthesis improves both physical validity and intent alignment over direct
mesh programming, while local voxel views further improve intent alignment across all 71 tasks.

Related Work
Spatial task specification. Language-guided placement has been studied through layout prediction, re-
arrangement search, and geometric optimization. Scene graphs encode inter-object relations for scene
synthesis (Chen et al., 2025; Zhai et al., 2023; Lin & Mu, 2024; Hu et al., 2024b), while scene-layout
and object-placement methods map language to spatial configurations (Feng et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024;
Öcal et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025a; Abdelreheem et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024; Kuang
et al., 2026). Rearrangement methods coordinate multi-object structures using learned spatial priors (Liu
et al., 2022; 2023; Tang et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024) or pattern-guided search (Chang
et al., 2024). At a finer scale, object manipulation methods formulate 6-DoF pose optimization over sparse
2


semantic keypoints, directional axes, and part-level affordances (Manuelli et al., 2022; Hua et al., 2025;
Huang et al., 2025b; Qi et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025).
While layout, surface-constraint, and keypoint formulations parameterize placement over bounding boxes,
planar support patches, or sparse point and axis primitives, open-ended tasks often hinge on extended
surface structures (such as receiving slots, angled ledges, curved rims, or recessed compartments) whose
relevant regions depend on the instruction and paired objects. VoxProg therefore treats geometric reference
construction as an explicit, programmable stage of task specification: generated code composes spatial
selections and neighborhood operations over local voxel views, retaining the selected surface regions and
their source-mesh patches for downstream measurement so that the same object geometry can be segmented
and parameterized differently across placement tasks.
Language models for spatial computation. Executable programs let language models delegate quantita-
tive calculation and visual queries to external interpreters (Gao et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; Gupta & Kem-
bhavi, 2023; Surís et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2024a). In robotics, generated code sequences task plans (Singh
et al., 2023), composes perception calls with control primitives (Liang et al., 2023; Mu et al., 2024; Li
et al., 2024), and synthesizes 3D value maps over workspace grids for motion planning (Huang et al.,
2023a). Recent methods further incorporate geometric processing via part-mask refinement (Tang et al.,
2026), primitive fitting (Lin et al., 2026), and frame-grounded tool calls (Chen et al., 2026).
In most programmatic frameworks, however, 3D perception remains encapsulated behind fixed API calls
that return object centroids, bounding boxes, or pre-segmented masks (Liang et al., 2023; Mu et al., 2024),
or operates on coarse workspace grids to score trajectories (Huang et al., 2023a). We develop voxel pro-
gramming so that generated code can inspect and dissect 3D object surfaces directly. By discretizing can-
didate objects into local voxel views at task-chosen resolutions, VoxProg turns 3D surface inspection into
regular-grid array programming while retaining exact correspondence with the underlying mesh triangles.
Generated code can thus construct custom surface witnesses on demand, verify them through visual and
numerical feedback, and measure both grid bounds and clipped source-mesh patches to solve for precise,
physically valid 6-DoF object placements.

Rather than mapping language directly to 6-DoF coordinates in a single step, VoxProg decouples geometric
reference construction from pose calculation: an evidence program Pe isolates task-relevant surface regions
on the moved and reference objects, and a pose program Pp measures these constructed regions and their
relational constraints to solve for the final rigid transformation.
Fig. 2 illustrates this pipeline on an upright plate-placement task. The model first organizes the required
spatial references into a directed evidence graph and grounds visible cues into 3D surface anchors (§3.1).
Operating on local voxel views of scene meshes (§3.2), the evidence program constructs the rack’s adjacent
dividers, lower support rails, and slot region while refining its selections through selection checks and
program revision (§3.3). The verified evidence record and relational claims are then passed to the pose
program, which queries both discrete voxel bounds and clipped source-mesh patches to align, center, and
seat the plate upright within the target slot (§3.4).
3.1
Task Interpretation and Visual Grounding
An LLM formalizes its understanding of the instruction as a directed evidence graph G = (V, E). Each
node v ∈V specifies either a visible semantic anchor or a derived geometric region to be constructed, along
with its functional role in the task; each directed edge (u, v) ∈E indicates that constructing v depends
on the location or geometry of u. In Fig. 2, visual cues on the plate and dish rack initiate a dependency
chain that extracts the plate’s outer rim together with the rack’s adjacent dividers, lower support rails, and
receiving slot region.
Each node in G is routed according to how its geometry can be acquired. For a directly visible semantic
cue, Locate queries a VLM for a 2D pixel coordinate in the rendered scene image and casts a camera ray
against the scene mesh to recover a 3D surface point as a spatial anchor in A. References that cannot be
reduced to a single clickable pixel, such as an interior slot region, a recessed support ledge, or an occluded
underside, are passed to the evidence program together with their descriptions and upstream 3D anchors,
which localize where on the object mesh the program should inspect and slice the surface.

3.2
Voxel Programming
Generated programs operate on scene geometry through local voxel views. Given a source mesh s and voxel
size h > 0, U = VoxelView(s, h) discretizes the surface of s onto a regular 3D grid at resolution h. On this
regular grid, the view exposes occupied surface cells directly as integer coordinate arrays (N, 3) alongside
directional exposure masks and connected-component queries, while a known grid-to-world rigid transform
maps selected voxel indices and local bounds back to continuous 3D scene coordinates for downstream
geometric computation in the scene.
Each constructed geometric reference forms a witness in the evidence record R. To construct a witness v,
the evidence program instantiates a local view Uv on source geometry sv at resolution hv and evaluates a
programmatic binary mask mv over its surface cells:
Uv = VoxelView(sv, hv),
Wv = {c ∈Uv : mv(c) = 1}.
(1)
Because Uv is a regular grid, the program can compose mv through standard array operations (axis-aligned
slicing, proximity filters around visual anchors or upstream witnesses, directional exposure queries, and
connected-component labeling) to isolate witnesses such as the rack’s adjacent dividers D, E, lower support
rails S, and combined slot region B in Fig. 2. For each witness v, the entry R.v in R stores the selected
cells Wv, their parent view Uv, the source object sv, and the provenance edges from G.
Parameterizing voxel size hv per view lets the program match discretization scale to local geometric struc-
ture without voxelizing the entire scene at uniform fine resolution. A program can use coarse grids to
localize broad object surfaces and switch to a finer hv when resolving thin wire dividers, narrow slots, or
shallow rims that would merge on a coarse grid, while retaining exact geometric correspondence with the
underlying triangles of the source object mesh.

an executable evidence program Pe following the topological order of G:

where R stores the constructed witnesses and C specifies the target spatial relations among them (e.g.,
placing the plate’s rim in slot B between dividers D, E while resting on support rails S in Fig. 2). Following
the dependencies in G allows downstream witnesses to be defined relative to earlier selections.
To tie each witness to its functional role before writing code, the model first states explicit inclusion and
exclusion criteria for each witness (such as excluding adjacent rack loops or vertical walls). When a ge-
ometric question remains unresolved, the model can execute lightweight probes via the same voxel API
to print numerical statistics (such as voxel counts, component counts, and coordinate spans) and inspect
rendered highlights of intermediate selections.
Once a complete candidate Pe is executed, geometric checks and a VLM critic check each witness for part
identity, region completeness, and the absence of unwanted geometry (Fig. 2, center). When a candidate
selection leaks into adjacent structure or remains incomplete, this feedback guides the model to revise its
selection rules or adjust voxel resolution and re-run Pe.

Pose Program Generation and Refinement
Given the verified evidence record R and relational specification C, the LLM generates an executable pose
program Pp to compute the moved object’s target rigid transformation:

where Tcur is the moved object’s current world-frame pose (initialized to its starting pose T0) and S provides
access to scene meshes and initial poses. Every witness enters Pp as concrete geometry rather than pre-

from R under C.
Beyond discrete grid coordinates, each witness entry R.v in R also exposes the continuous source-mesh
patch clipped to its selected voxels Wv, preserving exact face correspondence and outward surface normals.
As shown in Fig. 2 (step 3), Pp combines grid bounds with continuous mesh surfaces to solve accurately
for T on the original object geometry, computing the divider plane normal n from R.D, slot mid-span x
from R.B, and support depth and height y, z from R.S.

4
Experiments
We evaluate VoxProg on VoxProg-Bench (§4.1–§4.2), ablate staged reference construction and local voxel
views alongside external benchmarks from RoboCasa and Open6DOR v2 (§4.3), and examine physical and
perceptual failure modes (§4.4).
4.1
Experimental setup
Benchmark. We evaluate on VoxProg-Bench, a benchmark of 71 individually curated language-guided
3D placement tasks. Each case presents an initial tabletop scene and a free-form instruction specifying the
desired placement of an object. We emphasize diverse instructions that call for fine-grained reasoning about
functional object parts, geometric fit, and contextual arrangements. Although an instruction can involve
multiple spatial cues, each case is assigned to one group according to its primary spatial requirement for
the category-level comparisons reported below:
• Functional (28 cases): The placement must support a specific use, such as placing a cup beneath a
grinder chute to catch grounds, returning headphones to a stand, or orienting a funnel for pouring.
• Geometric (14 cases): The placement depends directly on object contours, such as nesting an oval bowl
inside a matching container or fitting a baking tray into a narrow box at an angle.
• Contextual (29 cases): The placement takes its cues from the surrounding arrangement, such as com-
pleting a missing place setting or restoring a symmetric layout.

Scenes are assembled from generated 3D assets through randomized placement and manual refinement.
Detailed instructions, object geometry, and scene context make the intended final arrangement well defined;
we provide one manually annotated ground-truth 6-DoF target pose per task. All methods receive the scene
meshes, initial poses, instruction, and labeled target-object identity; ground-truth poses and images are used

Evaluation suite. We design an evaluation suite around two complementary requirements of object place-
ment: realizing the intended spatial arrangement and maintaining physical plausibility. We report these as
Intent Alignment and Physical Validity, using visual comparison with ground truth and geometric checks,
respectively.
Intent Alignment measures perceptual similarity using DreamSim (Fu et al., 2023) between full-scene ren-
derings of the predicted and ground-truth placements under matched cameras and lighting. Within each
case, we rank the M compared methods by increasing DreamSim distance and assign rank r the score
100(M −r)/(M −1), using average ranks for ties. Averaging over cases gives the IA Rank Score (0–100;
higher is better), which measures how consistently a method outranks its competitors across tasks. Each
task contributes equally; scores are compared within the same method pool.
Physical Validity assesses whether the final placement establishes plausible static support and avoids inter-
penetration with surrounding objects. We report the percentage of evaluated task placements that satisfy
both 3D contact and clearance checks.
Baselines. We compare VoxProg against three language-guided placement approaches. GenManip (Gao
et al., 2025) constructs placements using predefined spatial relations between target and anchor objects.
GenSim2-kPAM (Hua et al., 2025; Manuelli et al., 2022) specifies placement targets through predicted
semantic keypoints together with geometric point and axis constraints. Direct Mesh generates Blender
Python code for placement and executes it through Blender-MCP1.
Model configuration. All compared methods use GPT-5.6-Sol under a shared inference configuration for
both language code generation and visual grounding.
4.2
Results on the task suite
Quantitative results. VoxProg achieves the highest IA Rank Score overall (83.10) and across all three
task groups, alongside the highest Physical Validity at 95.8% (Table 1), outperforming Direct Mesh (48.59
/ 87.3%), GenSim2-kPAM (42.72 / 77.5%), and GenManip (25.59 / 73.2%). Although Direct Mesh also
writes task-specific Python code over explicit scene meshes, VoxProg improves its IA Rank Score by 34.51
points and Physical Validity by 8.5 points: code generation alone remains brittle without an intermediate
representation that isolates the surfaces relevant to the instruction.
Breaking down performance by category reveals how different representations behave under distinct spa-
tial demands. On Functional tasks, VoxProg reaches 86.90 versus 53.57 for Direct Mesh and 34.52 for
GenSim2-kPAM (with Physical Validity rising from 75.0% and 78.6% to 92.9%), as functional use re-
quires isolating mating parts, such as a chute outlet or headphone arch, rather than whole-object bounding
boxes or sparse keypoints. On Contextual tasks, the strongest baseline shifts from Direct Mesh (45.98) to
GenSim2-kPAM (54.02), while VoxProg retains a clear lead at 77.01. The largest margin occurs on Ge-
ometric tasks: VoxProg reaches an IA Rank Score of 88.10 versus 44.05 for Direct Mesh, although both
attain 100.0% Physical Validity. The difference lies in resolving the local contours and clearances that
distinguish the intended fit from other physically valid configurations.

4.3
Ablation and external evaluation
Effect of voxel programming. To separate the effect of staged reference construction from that of local
voxel views, we compare Direct Mesh, Mesh Evidence, and VoxProg across all 71 tasks in Table 2. Direct
Mesh writes a single placement program directly over the scene meshes; Mesh Evidence shares VoxProg’s
two-stage pipeline and execution feedback but constructs witness regions through direct mesh operations on
vertices and faces; VoxProg constructs witnesses on local voxel views while retaining access to the clipped
source-mesh surfaces during pose computation.
Within this three-method comparison, moving from single-stage Direct Mesh to the staged Mesh Evidence
pipeline raises Physical Validity from 87.3% to 95.8% and more than doubles the IA Rank Score from
26.76 to 56.34. When a single program must simultaneously locate task-relevant geometry and compute
6-DoF coordinates over raw scene meshes, imprecise region selection directly affects the resulting pose;
introducing an explicit evidence stage allows candidate regions to be constructed, checked, and revised
before downstream pose synthesis.

Failure Analysis
Physical failure profiles. Fig. 7a decomposes physical rejections on VoxProg-Bench into support and in-
terpenetration failures. GenManip incurs 19 rejections (11 support, 8 intersection), versus 16 for GenSim2-
kPAM (13 intersection, 3 support). These patterns are consistent with coarse relation offsets leaving support
contact under-specified, and sparse point and axis constraints leaving clearance between extended surfaces
under-constrained. Direct Mesh has 9 rejections (6 intersection, 3 support), while both staged variants cut
rejections to 3 (3 intersection for Mesh Evidence; 2 intersection, 1 support for VoxProg).

Visually ambiguous contact. The pot-lid case in Fig. 7b
illustrates why 2D visual inspection alone cannot establish
physical validity in 3D object placement. From the primary
camera view, the lid appears properly seated: it stands up-
right with its handle facing outward, its circular contour cen-
tered between the side supports, and its lower rim aligned
over the receiving rack. A side view of the same final pose
reveals, however, that the lid remains suspended above the
rack, separated by a clear vertical gap from both the base
tray and the inclined backrest. Here, the rack’s opaque front
frame provides a strong visual cue to the destination while
occluding the recessed region where support contact should
occur. Because 2D projection overlap along the viewing ray
can conceal 3D separation under self-occlusion, a single-
view visual comparison can accept a physically unsupported
placement even when the target object and its apparent rela-
tion to the reference structure look aligned in image space.
Pairing perceptual evaluation with 3D geometric verification
addresses this ambiguity by checking local support contact
and interpenetration conditions between the placed object
and surrounding scene meshes, making the distinction be-
tween apparent 2D seating in the rendered view and actual
physical support in the 3D scene explicit.

Conclusion
We presented VoxProg, a framework for 3D spatial reasoning that formulates language-guided object place-
ment through voxel programming. Rather than parameterizing tasks over fixed spatial relations or sparse
keypoint primitives, VoxProg treats the geometric references required by an instruction as intermediate sur-
face structures constructed by executable code. By coupling regular-grid selection over local voxel views
with exact surface normals and patches from the underlying meshes, our two-stage pipeline enables a lan-
guage model to isolate functional contact regions, verify them through visual and geometric feedback, and
solve for physically valid SE(3) poses. Across the 71-task VoxProg-Bench benchmark and external tasks
from RoboCasa and Open6DOR v2, VoxProg consistently outperforms relation-based, keypoint-based, and
mesh-based baselines in both intent alignment and physical validity. Coupling such programmable sur-
face witnesses with downstream motion generation provides a natural direction for extending fine-grained
geometric reasoning from target placement specification to full manipulation execution.
