Abstract 

Video vision–language models (VLMs) have strong seman-tic understanding, yet remain unreliable at precise tempo-ral grounding. Existing gains from explicitly aligning video frames with absolute time suggest that VLMs may already encode accurate temporal locations internally but lose pre-cision when expressing them as timestamps. Our analysis across four VLMs and two grounding datasets confirms this hypothesis: attention intervals outperform predicted intervals on approximately 66% of valid examples. We term this dis-crepancy the temporal generation gap . Because precise tem-poral annotations are expensive, this finding motivates closing the gap to improve temporal grounding using only unlabeled video–query pairs. We propose Self-Supervised Attention Policy Optimization (SAPO) , which uses internal temporal attention as self-supervision for Group Relative Policy Op-timization and applies a ground-truth-free criterion to select high-value samples. SAPO consistently improves temporal grounding across multiple datasets and models spanning weak to strong initial grounding ability. Relative mIoU gains reach 

33 .7% on Qwen2.5-VL-3B and 5.4% on the strong Time-R1-7B. The improvements transfer across datasets while preserv-ing general video understanding. 

Introduction 

Vision–language models (VLMs) have become a general in-terface for video understanding (Bai et al. 2025). Beyond recognizing what happens, a capable video VLM must also determine precisely when an event occurs. Temporal video grounding (TVG) directly evaluates this ability by requir-ing a model to localize the start and end times of an event described by a natural-language query. Despite their strong general video understanding, current VLMs remain substan-tially less reliable when generating precise timestamps. Recent studies attribute much of this weakness to insuf-ficient alignment between visual features and absolute time. Existing methods therefore inject frame-level temporal in-formation through interleaved textual timestamps, timestamp overlays, or time-aligned position embeddings (Zhang et al. 2026; Huang et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2025; Bai et al. 2025). Their improvements establish that VLMs predict time more accurately when the correspondence between visual evidence and temporal coordinates is made explicit. Across four VLMs, attention achieves a higher IoU than prediction on an average of 66 .0% of exam-ples on Charades-STA and 65 .8% on ActivityNet-Captions. We term this systematic discrepancy the temporal genera-tion gap : the model’s attention can localize the queried event more accurately than its explicit timestamp prediction, as illustrated in Figure 1. Although attention does not provide perfectly accurate boundaries, its relative advantage reveals a useful source arXiv:2610.25923v1 [cs.CV] 19 Oct 2026 of internal temporal supervision. Since annotating precise start and end timestamps for video–query pairs is highly costly, this finding motivates us to exploit unlabeled video– query data and improve temporal grounding directly through attention-based self-supervision. Based on this insight, we propose Self-Supervised At-tention Policy Optimization (SAPO) , which uses internal attention to construct RL rewards for timestamp generation. Each selected video–query pair is processed by a trainable policy and a frozen reference model initialized from the same VLM. The policy samples a group of timestamp intervals, while the reference extracts a stationary temporal-attention distribution. To avoid the sparse supervision of directly using interval IoU as the reward, SAPO projects each rollout inter-val onto the video timeline with a Gaussian kernel and mea-sures its Wasserstein-1 distance to the reference attention dis-tribution, yielding a dense temporal reward. Group-relative advantage estimation then updates the policy, with the ref-erence model also providing KL regularization. SAPO thus transfers internal temporal evidence into native timestamp prediction without ground-truth boundaries for training. Not all video–query pairs, however, contain equally useful attention supervision. We therefore explore how to identify a higher proportion of valuable training examples without ground truth. Among the examined criteria, top-k selection by Jensen–Shannon divergence between the prediction and attention distributions provides a lightweight solution and re-trieves more high-value examples for RL than random sam-pling. We evaluate SAPO on three benchmarks using both Qwen2.5-VL models and Time-R1-7B, a strong tempo-ral grounding model, without temporal annotations during training. SAPO yields relative mIoU improvements of up to 33 .7% on Qwen2.5-VL-3B and 5.4% on Time-R1-7B. Gains under cross-dataset training further demonstrate trans-fer across data domains, while the Video-MME accuracy of Qwen2.5-VL-3B remains close to the base model ( 58 .0 vs. 

57 .4), indicating that SAPO largely preserves general video understanding. Further ablations verify the effects of high-value sample selection and the individual training objectives. Our contributions aresummarized as follows: 

• Across multiple VLMs and grounding datasets, we iden-tify a temporal generation gap : internal temporal atten-tion often localizes queried events more accurately than explicitly generated timestamps. 

• We propose Self-Supervised Attention Policy Opti-mization (SAPO) , which uses internal attention to im-prove temporal grounding through reinforcement learn-ing without temporal-boundary annotations. 

• Experiments across models, datasets, and training sources demonstrate consistent grounding improvements for both general-purpose VLMs and a strong temporal grounding model, together with cross-dataset transfer and largely preserved general video understanding. 

Related Work 

Vision–Language Models. Recent VLMs combine visual encoders with large language models to support general im-age and video understanding (Bai et al. 2025; Li et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2024). Although these models exhibit strong semantic reasoning ability, precise temporal grounding re-mains challenging. Our work directly improves the temporal grounding ability of a VLM without changing its architecture or requiring temporal annotations. 

Temporal Video Grounding. Temporal video grounding lo-calizes the video segment described by a natural-language query. Conventional methods learn this task from anno-tated temporal intervals (Zhang et al. 2020; Lei, Berg, and Bansal 2021). Recent generative VLMs instead formulate grounding as native timestamp generation. They improve temporal grounding through architectural designs, tempo-rally grounded instruction data and supervised fine-tuning (Huang et al. 2024; Ren et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2024; Zeng et al. 2025; Guo et al. 2025b; Wang et al. 2025a; Guo et al. 2025a; Hannan et al. 2025), or more effective post-training algorithms such as reinforcement learning and on-policy dis-tillation (Wang et al. 2025b; Li et al. 2026). Despite their dif-ferent approaches to exploiting supervision, these methods rely on annotated temporal targets. Training-free methods avoid temporal annotations by con-structing external workflows that match frame-level infor-mation from frozen models with the query (Lee et al. 2025; Zheng et al. 2024; Qu et al. 2024). Although they can produce temporal intervals without training, they do not improve the native grounding ability of the underlying VLM. SAPO dif-fers from both supervised and training-free methods: it uses self-supervision from the model’s own attention to improve its native timestamp generation through policy optimization, without temporal annotations. 

Model Mechanism Analysis. Prior studies inspect internal model states to understand how visual evidence is encoded before the final response (Zhao et al. 2024; Palit et al. 2023). They show that model outputs may not fully reflect the infor-mation available in intermediate representations. Following this perspective, we analyze temporal attention in generative VLMs and identify a gap between internal localization and explicit timestamp generation.

Method 

Building on the preceding finding, we propose Self-Supervised Attention Policy Optimization (SAPO) , which uses internal temporal attention as self-supervision to im-prove the native grounding ability of a VLM. As illustrated in Figure 3, for each unlabeled video–query pair, a trainable policy samples timestamp intervals, while a frozen reference model provides a stationary temporal attention distribution. SAPO measures their alignment as the rollout reward, es-timates group-relative advantages, and updates the policy. We next describe the complete optimization procedure and the selection of high-value samples without ground truth. Implementation details are provided in the Appendix.

High-Value Sample Selection 

The preceding objective can train on any unlabeled video– query pair, but not every sample provides equally useful at-tention supervision. If the base prediction already agrees with attention, there is little discrepancy for SAPO to correct; if prediction is more accurate than attention, the internal signal may not provide useful supervision. Determining which sig-nal is more accurate normally requires ground-truth bound-aries. We therefore seek a proxy that does not require ground truth and selects a higher proportion of samples on which attention provides the stronger localization signal. Given an unlabeled pool D = {(Vi, q i)}Mi=1 , we run the base model once to obtain the predicted interval Ip,i and reference at-tention distribution ai for each sample. Experiments 

Experimental Setup 

Base Models. We apply the same SAPO procedure to three base models: Qwen2.5-VL-3B and Qwen2.5-VL-7B (Bai et al. 2025), which represent general-purpose VLMs at two scales, and Time-R1-7B (Wang et al. 2025b), a state-of-the-art model with strong temporal grounding performance. 

Training Data. SAPO uses video–query pairs from the train-ing splits of Charades-STA (Gao et al. 2017) and ActivityNet-Captions (Krishna et al. 2017) after removing all temporal boundaries. Using the ground-truth-free criterion described in the Method, we select 2,000 high-value samples from one dataset for single-source training or 1,000 from each dataset for joint training, keeping the total budget fixed. 

Benchmarks and Metrics. We evaluate temporal grounding on the held-out test splits of Charades-STA and ActivityNet-Captions under the standard train/test separation. To verify that temporal adaptation does not substantially impair general video understanding, we additionally report overall accuracy on Video-MME (Fu et al. 2024). Full training configurations, implementation details, and the Video-MME breakdown are provided in the Appendix. 

Baselines. Our primary baselines are the zero-shot versions of the three base models, which enable controlled, paired comparisons under the same evaluation protocol. We also report ChatVTG (Qu et al. 2024), TFVTG (Zheng et al. 2024), and TAG (Lee et al. 2025) as training-free numerical references because they share the no-annotation condition. They are not strictly comparable, however, because they im-prove inference through external workflows rather than up-date the underlying model. To our knowledge, no existing TVG method provides a directly matched setting that updates a model using video–query pairs without temporal annota-tions. Unless otherwise specified, post-training analyses and ablations use the joint-data setting.

Main Results

The gains are therefore not limited to correcting a weak base model, but extend across model scales and initial grounding abilities. Single-source training also transfers to the other dataset, while joint training remains effective across evalua-tion domains. These results indicate that SAPO learns a trans-ferable improvement to native timestamp generation rather than fitting a dataset-specific temporal prior, supporting its use when adapting a VLM to previously unseen unlabeled data. In-Depth Analysis 

We examine whether SAPO transfers internal localization ev-idence into native timestamps, closes the temporal generation gap, and changes attention accuracy, while also comparing it with direct attention decoding. 

Does SAPO close the temporal generation gap? As shown in Table 5, SAPO substantially improves native times-tamp prediction on both datasets and reduces the prediction– attention gap to within one mIoU point. The trained predic-tion slightly exceeds attention on Charades-STA and nearly matches it on ActivityNet-Captions, indicating that the orig-inal generation gap is effectively closed. Figure 5 provides complementary visual evidence: compared with the base model, the SAPO prediction aligns more closely with both the attention interval and the ground-truth event region. Attention accuracy remains largely stable after SAPO, with only minor increases on both datasets that may arise because prediction and attention share model parameters; we there-fore treat this change as a secondary effect. 

How does SAPO compare with direct attention de-coding? As shown in Table 5, the native SAPO prediction achieves higher aggregate accuracy than directly decoding its attention. To explain this seemingly counter-intuitive re-sult, we partition queries using the initial model: frozen at-tention has higher ground-truth IoU in the attention-better 

group, while the remaining queries form the prediction-better 

group. Each point in Figure 6 compares one query’s initial and post-SAPO prediction IoUs, with the diagonal denot-ing no change. SAPO improves 81% /86% of attention-better queries and, despite optimizing toward attention, also im-proves 38% /34% of prediction-better queries on Charades-STA/ActivityNet-Captions. Thus, gains on most attention-better queries outweigh regressions while many prediction-better cases are retained or further improved. Reward Components. Table 5 compares Full SAPO with five controlled variants that modify one aspect of the re-ward while keeping the training data and optimization set-tings fixed. We report mIoU together with recall at multiple IoU thresholds to assess boundary precision more compre-hensively. The final two columns additionally track whether training changes the attention interval itself. Variant 1 re-moves both attention-derived rewards and performs below the base model, confirming that the gains of SAPO arise from attention-based self-supervision rather than generic format or width constraints. SAPO uses attention supervision at two complementary levels. The temporal distribution reward aligns each roll-out distribution with the full reference attention distribution through W1, providing dense feedback that reflects temporal distance. The attention-interval IoU reward instead aligns the generated interval with the decoded attention interval, pro-viding sparse boundary-level supervision. Variants 2 and 3 show that both components are effective. The larger degrada-tion in Variant 2 further establishes the importance of dense supervision: interval IoU cannot distinguish non-overlapping rollouts, whereas distribution alignment still reflects their distances from the attended region. Variant 4 replaces W1

with JS. Although the two variants achieve similar mIoU, JS reduces recall at stricter IoU thresholds. Unlike JS, W1

preserves temporal geometry and provides distance-sensitive feedback for separated distributions, leading to more precise boundaries. Variant 5 exposes the role of width regulariza-tion. Without it, the model expands its predicted intervals to obtain coarse overlap, keeping mIoU similar while sub-stantially degrading high-precision localization. The pseudo-label SFT baseline is instead constrained by the accuracy of its hard attention targets: its predicted intervals approach but remain below Full SAPO, and its attention accuracy remains essentially unchanged. In contrast, SAPO improves both pre-dicted and attention intervals, indicating a joint adaptation mechanism beyond direct imitation of hard attention targets. 

Conclusion 

We identify a temporal generation gap in video VLMs: at-tention intervals localize queried events more accurately than predicted intervals on approximately 66% of valid examples. This finding reveals internal temporal evidence that can supervise timestamp generation without human-annotated boundaries. Based on this insight, we propose SAPO, which uses temporal attention as self-supervision for group-relative policy optimization to improve native times-tamp prediction. Across multiple models, datasets, and train-ing sources, SAPO improves temporal grounding for both general-purpose VLMs and a strong temporal grounding model, transfers across datasets, and largely preserves gen-eral video understanding. Attention Extraction Protocol 

Evaluation data. The temporal generation gap in Table 1 is measured on all 3,720 Charades-STA examples and a fixed random sample of 4,000 examples from the 17,505 Activi-tyNet examples. The identical subsets are used for all four VLMs. Ground-truth intervals are accessed only to compute the diagnostic IoU values; they are not used to extract atten-tion, choose examples, or train standard SAPO. The explic-itly marked GT-oracle ablation is reported only as an upper bound. 

Diagnostic attention extraction. We take the last prompt token’s cross-modal attention to video tokens from a fixed mid-to-late layer, average it over attention heads, and pool the spatial visual tokens belonging to each frame. After remov-ing the first-bin attention sink, we renormalize the resulting temporal profile. Its attention interval is the shortest contigu-ous window covering 50% of the cumulative attention mass, as defined in Eq. 1, and is then mapped from frame positions to seconds. We use ground-truth boundaries only in this of-fline mechanism analysis to measure diagnostic IoU. Table 7 lists the layer used for each model; the same layer is used on both datasets and throughout all experiments. Figure 8 reports sensitivity across middle-to-late layers and attention-mass thresholds. 

Reference attention distribution. The reference attention distribution used during training follows the same last-token extraction, head averaging, spatial pooling, and interval-decoding procedure. We additionally divide the temporal profile by a position prior estimated from the training pool without temporal boundaries to remove dataset-level tem-poral bias, and renormalize the result into a distribution 

a ∈ ∆T . The attention interval Ia is the shortest contigu-ous window covering 50% of the corrected attention mass. The layer and threshold remain fixed throughout sample se-lection and SAPO adaptation. For each model architecture, we directly specify one representative middle-to-late layer without ranking candidate layers by ground-truth grounding accuracy. The same layer is then used across datasets; the sensitivity analysis below verifies that the diagnostic conclu-sion is stable across nearby middle-to-late layers.
