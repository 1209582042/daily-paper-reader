ABSTRACT
Recent 3D Large Language Models (3D-LLMs) have shown strong performance
on scene question answering with extensive post-training. Yet, existing bench-
marks rarely evaluate how 3D-LLMs respond to referential ambiguity, insufficient
scene evidence, or false presuppositions, obscuring whether their responses are
grounded in 3D evidence. In this work, we introduce UNICORNS, a bench-
mark for evaluating the reliability of 3D-LLM responses across four evidence
conditions. UNICORNS contains 11,387 questions across 557 ScanNet scans,
covering eight challenge categories alongside answerable controls. A response
passes only if it satisfies the required response behavior and contains no scene
claim unsupported by the available evidence.
Evaluation of six open-sourced
3D-LLMs reveals a pronounced performance gap: even the strongest zero-shot
model achieves 61.22% accuracy on answerable controls, but only 40.94% across
the eight challenge categories. To address this gap, we further propose HORN,
an obligation-aware post-training method combining group-relative task rewards,
leaf-conditioned obligation rewards, and supervised control replay. Extensive ex-
periments demonstrate that HORN reaches 75.1±1.0% across the challenge cat-
egories and 77.2±0.8% overall, outperforming all baselines without degrading
answerable-control accuracy. Our findings enable a systematic evaluation and im-
provement of evidence-grounded 3D reasoning for 3D-LLMs (code).

INTRODUCTION
3D large language models (3D-LLMs) extend pretrained language models to 3D scene understand-
ing and reasoning by integrating representations such as point clouds, object-level features, and
multiview observations (Hong et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2024b;a). These models support applica-
tions including 3D dialogue, task planning, and embodied action (Huang et al., 2024b). Reliable
3D reasoning, however, requires models not only to answer questions but also to recognize what the
available observations do and do not support (Wu et al., 2026). In realistic settings, questions may
contain ambiguous references, ask about unobserved information, or rest on premises contradicted
by scene evidence (Wu et al., 2026). Models may nevertheless produce fluent, plausible responses
whose claims are unsupported by the scene. Existing 3D QA benchmarks predominantly focus on
questions with determinate answers and rarely evaluate models under these conditions; moreover,
when scene evidence and language priors favor the same response, correctness alone cannot distin-
guish evidence-grounded reasoning from reliance on language priors (Ma et al., 2026).
Figure 1 illustrates this limitation. Given the same scene, all three models recognize the table, and
two correctly deny that a unicorn is present when asked directly. Yet, when the absent object is em-
bedded in a presupposing attribute question, all three assign it a color. This sensitivity to question
framing despite unchanged scene evidence motivates our central question: do 3D-LLMs really use
3D evidence? Prior work addresses complementary aspects of this problem. SceneCOT grounds
intermediate reasoning in scene objects (Linghu et al., 2026). Real-3DQA filters text-answerable
questions and tests viewpoint-rotation consistency (Ma et al., 2026). AbstainEQA benchmarks
image-based abstention under referential underspecification, unavailable information, and false pre-
suppositions (Wu et al., 2026). However, none jointly evaluates whether response strategies fit
the evidence and all response claims are supported, even an appropriate strategy may introduce
unsupported details. We therefore assess both dimensions, with answerable controls for sufficient-
evidence questions.

To fill this gap, we introduce UNICORNS, a benchmark that jointly evaluates evidence-appropriate
response behavior and factual support for complete responses. The benchmark contains 11,387
questions spanning 557 ScanNet scans (Dai et al., 2017) and 353 object classes. Eight challenge
categories and their paired answerable controls cover four evidence states. Scene-disjoint splits
and executable inventory and geometry checks establish the evidence available for each question.
Three LLM judges (OpenAI, 2025; Google DeepMind, 2026; DeepSeek-AI, 2024) apply a shared
evidence-veto policy by majority vote, passing a response only if it follows the required behavior
and makes no unsupported scene claims.
We further propose HORN (Heterogeneous Obligation Rewards with Non-refusal replay), an
obligation-aware post-training method combining group-relative task rewards, leaf-conditioned obli-
gation rewards, and supervised control replay. Across six released 3D-LLMs, the strongest zero-shot
model achieves 61.22% accuracy on answerable controls but only 40.94% across the challenge cat-
egories. Starting from the same SceneCOT SFT checkpoint, HORN reaches 75.1±1.0% Challenge,
79.3±0.7% Control, and 77.2±0.8% Overall, outperforming matched GRPO by 5.1 and 3.2 per-
centage points on Challenge and Overall, respectively. Controlled scene edits, Ego-Visible evalu-
ation, and text-only tests further assess evidence sensitivity, robustness to partial observations, and
dependence on 3D input. Our main contributions are summarized as:
1. The UNICORNS benchmark. A point-cloud-native 3D benchmark that jointly evaluates
evidence-appropriate response behavior and complete-response factual support across four
evidence states, with executable scene checks and answerable controls.
2. Systematic evaluation and analysis.
An evaluation of six released 3D-LLMs with
response-strategy and factual-support diagnostics, controlled scene edits, Ego-Visible test-
ing, and text-only ablations.
3. The HORN post-training method. An obligation-aware method that outperforms matched
SFT and GRPO on Challenge and Overall accuracy while preserving performance on an-
swerable controls.

RELATED WORK
Grounded 3D-LLMs reasoning. 3D-LLM connects 3D features with language models, while
LEO and Chat-Scene incorporate object features and multiview observations to support scene un-
derstanding and embodied tasks (Hong et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2024b;a). SceneCOT further
grounds intermediate reasoning in specific objects and checks consistency between grounding and
answers (Linghu et al., 2026). Real-3DQA’s reweighted fine-tuning targets language shortcuts by
emphasizing predictions that depend on 3D input (Ma et al., 2026). These methods improve scene
representations and reasoning, motivating evaluation of whether their responses remain reliable un-
der different evidence conditions.

Ambiguity, insufficient evidence, and response strategies. Visual questions may require clarifi-
cation or abstention when the available evidence does not determine an answer. ClearVQA trains
interactive clarification (Jian et al., 2025), AQuA distinguishes direct answering, inference, enumer-
ation, and clarification (Jang & Kim, 2026), and CoA learns when to answer or ask (Cao et al.,
2026). AbstainEQA studies abstention under missing information, referential underspecification,
and false presuppositions, with paired answerable questions, on egocentric RGB frames rather than
3D point clouds (Wu et al., 2026). ProactiveBench and Ask-to-Act extend this perspective to re-
questing evidence and clarifying instructions before action (De Min et al., 2026; Ramrakhya et al.,
2025). Together, these studies motivate adapting response strategies to evidence conditions while
preserving ordinary answering.
Diagnostic benchmarks for response reliability. Existing 3D benchmarks assess different aspects
of scene understanding. ScanQA, SQA3D, and MSR3D focus on scene question answering and
situated reasoning (Azuma et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2023; Linghu et al., 2024). Real-3DQA probes
language shortcuts and viewpoint consistency (Ma et al., 2026), and Ambi3D evaluates instruction
ambiguity detection (Ding et al., 2026). These benchmarks do not jointly assess response strategies
across evidence conditions and factual support for complete responses. UNICORNS unifies both
requirements into a single pass criterion that rejects any unsupported scene claim, even when the
strategy is appropriate. Table 1 summarizes these differences in evaluation coverage.

THE UNICORNS BENCHMARK
This study proposes UNICORNS to jointly assess response strategies and the factual reliability of
complete responses in 3D scenes. Models are required to respond according to the available evidence
and ground every scene claim in it. Full Scene is the primary evaluation setting, using the complete
scene point cloud.
3.1
EVIDENCE STATES AND CHALLENGE TAXONOMY
We define four states by the relationship between a question and scene evidence: determinate, am-
biguous, unobserved, and contradicted. They respectively require direct answering, clarification or
candidate enumeration, an explicit account of what cannot be determined, and rejection or correc-
tion of a false premise. Eight challenge leaves cover ambiguity (U/N), unobserved information (I/C),
and contradiction (O/R/N′/S). Table 2 gives their definitions and required responses. Their initials
spell UNICORNS, with the prime distinguishing the second N. Answerable Control samples serve as
the basis for constructing paired Challenges, testing whether models retain correct direct answering
alongside challenge handling.
3.2

and geometry (Dai et al., 2017). Each leaf draws on prior construction principles without copying re-
leased QA samples. Appendix A.1 maps these principles to their sources. Independent verification
programs recompute candidate cardinality, existence, attributes, relations, visibility, and fit ratios
(Appendix A.2). Samples with incomplete evidence, conflicting sources, or same-scene semantic or
normalized-question duplicates are excluded. Each sample retains scene/source identifiers, its con-

3.3
EVALUATION PROTOCOL
Figure 2 summarizes our evaluation pipeline. A 3D-LLM receives scene observations and a ques-
tion, while an ensemble of three LLM judges evaluates its response using the question, structured
scene evidence, and a response rubric.
Each instance is represented as x = (S, q, e, o, r, z, ℓ): scene S, question q, machine-checkable
evidence e, response obligation o, response rubric r, evidence state z, and challenge leaf or Control
ℓ. Models generate free-form text y from the scene and question. We define two Boolean predicates:
Strategy(y, o, r) checks whether the response follows the required behavior, and Factual(y, e, r)
checks both the required factual content and the support for every scene claim. A response passes
only if both hold:
Adm(y | x) = Strategy(y, o, r) ∧Factual(y, e, r).
(1)
Three LLM judges independently apply the same leaf-aware prompt (Appendix B) using the ques-
tion, response, leaf, obligation, rubric, and structured evidence. Each judge returns strategy and
factual verdicts; a response passes if a majority of judges pass both on that response. Different valid
formulations can pass, but any unsupported scene claim vetoes the complete response, even with an
appropriate strategy. For example, uncertainty followed by an unsupported count, color, or relation
fails. Controls require a correct direct answer, so refusal fails.
Evaluation metrics. For any evaluation subset D, Strategic, Factual, and Veto Accuracy are defined
as the mean values of Strategy, Factual, and Adm, respectively. Unless otherwise stated, accuracy
refers to Veto Accuracy. Let Aℓand AC0 denote Veto Accuracy on challenge leaf ℓand Controls,
respectively. We report per-leaf accuracy together with Challenge = 1

Reward construction. For each scene–question pair (S, q) in instance x, the policy πθ(y | S, q)
generates G = 6 responses. The task reward checks response strategy and factual content against
reference answers and structured evidence, and computes grounding and format scores gi and fi.

scores are assigned automatically from within-group comparisons. Responses are ordered by three
verification levels: strategy failure, strategy-only success, and joint strategy and factual success.
Strategy failures are tied at the bottom; within the other levels, grounding is compared first and
format second, with ties assigned average ranks. Let ui ∈{0, 1} indicate strategy success and let
ranks increase from worst to best, from 1 to G. The task reward is

Policy optimization and control replay. We center each reward coordinate within its response
group and combine them using a shared, fixed global obligation coefficient α to obtain advantages
for group-relative policy updates (Shao et al., 2024). Groups with no variation in either coordinate
are resampled and skipped if they remain uninformative after the resampling limit. On effective
challenge updates, we also draw an answerable-control batch BC0 from the training pool and add
supervised cross-entropy to provide continued direct-answer supervision. The objective is
LHORN = Lclipped + βKLLref + λ 1[ℓ̸ = C0]LSFT(θ; BC0).
(3)
Here Lclipped is the clipped policy loss, Lref is the reference-policy regularizer, βKL controls reg-
ularization strength, and λ controls supervised replay; replay is omitted when no control batch is
available. The model retains its native scene representation. Leaf labels, references, and structured
evidence provide training supervision only; inference requires the scene and question.

5.2
MAIN RESULTS
Limitations of existing 3D-LLMs. Table 3 reports zero-shot(ZS) Challenge accuracies of 12.18–
40.94%. The strongest model, GPT4Scene, reaches 61.22% on Control but only 40.94% on Chal-
lenge, a gap of 20.28 percentage points (pp). Thus, strong performance on ordinary answerable
questions can give the appearance that models use 3D evidence, yet their challenge responses are
often unfaithful to that evidence. Zero-shot SceneCOT reaches 39.09% Challenge but only 30.80%
Control, underscoring the need to evaluate challenge handling and ordinary answering separately.
These results reveal substantial limitations in evidence-grounded response reliability across evidence
conditions. We therefore select the two strongest zero-shot models, GPT4Scene and SceneCOT, for
in-depth analysis. Appendix F.1 visualizes Challenge–Control performance gaps across models.
Effectiveness of supervised fine-tuning. Table 3 shows clear SFT gains on UNICORNS. SceneCOT
gains +29.5/+48.4 pp on Challenge/Control, and GPT4Scene +3.1/+13.1 pp. These gains show
that UNICORNS can improve the model’s robustness to ambiguous and false-premise questions.

ANALYSIS AND DISCUSSION
6.1
STRATEGY SELECTION VERSUS SCENE FACTUALITY
Following 2D benchmark AQuA (Jang & Kim, 2026), we separately assess response strategy and
scene factuality for each model. Table 5(a) shows that, relative to zero-shot SceneCOT, HORN im-
proves Overall Strategic, Factual, and Veto Accuracy by 41.90, 42.38, and 42.25 pp, respectively,
indicating better strategy selection and fidelity to scene facts. Table 5(b) shows that HORN reduces
direct answers on Challenge from SceneCOT’s 51.15% to 16.71%, favoring clarification and cor-
rection, while increasing direct answers on Control from 53.06% to 99.87%. These changes suggest
that, compared with zero-shot SceneCOT, HORN trained on UNICORNS favors evidence-consistent
response strategies when 3D evidence cannot support a direct answer. Yet even strategically ap-
propriate HORN responses can contain unsupported counts, colors, or relations. This highlights the
diagnostic value of UNICORNS beyond aggregate accuracy and the need to verify every scene claim
in the complete response.

CONCLUSION
We introduced UNICORNS, a point-cloud-native benchmark that jointly evaluates response strate-
gies and the factual support of complete responses across four evidence conditions. Our evalua-
tion clearly exposes weaknesses in existing models: success on ordinary answerable questions does
not ensure reliable handling of ambiguity, unobserved information, or false premises; even an ap-
propriate response strategy can coexist with unsupported scene claims. UNICORNS also supports
post-training: we proposed HORN, which achieves state-of-the-art performance on this benchmark.
Removing 3D input substantially reduces performance and narrows HORN’s advantage over GRPO,
suggesting that these gains involve scene information. These findings highlight two requirements
for reliable 3D-LLM responses: choosing a strategy suited to the available evidence and grounding
every scene claim in that evidence.
Limitations. Despite outperforming matched GRPO baselines on both backbones, HORN still pro-
duces factual errors and inappropriate response strategies. Further work is needed to improve its
factual accuracy and better align its response strategies with the available evidence.

the geometric conflict if useful. Answerable controls require a direct scene-grounded
answer. Judge the strategy independently of factual errors: a clarification containing a
wrong count can pass STRATEGY while failing FACTUAL.

the complete response. Check object identity, class, count, attributes, existence, spatial
relations, size compatibility, container contents, and observation scope when relevant. Fail
FACTUAL if required factual content is missing or if any scene claim contradicts,
exceeds, or lacks support in the supplied evidence. A correct opening does not cancel a
later unsupported claim. Accept semantically equivalent wording; do not require verbatim
reproduction of a reference answer.
3. UNCERTAINTY AND SHORT ANSWERS. Concision does not relax the required
behavior or factual checks. A short answer passes only if it fulfills the category obligation
and the required factual content. A bare refusal cannot replace clarification or
enumeration on U/N. For R, a correct “No” to a yes/no attribute question suffices; stating

HORN: REWARD DESIGN AND OPTIMIZATION
This appendix supplements Section 4 with the reward definitions and training details of HORN.
The policy receives a scene and question x = (S, q); training annotations provide category ℓand
evidence c to the reward function only. All reward annotations and replay examples come from the
training split. The rule-based training verifier is separate from the evidence-veto judging protocol
used for evaluation.
Group-ranked task reward.
For each of G = 6 sampled responses, the verifier returns strategy
validity ui ∈{0, 1}, factual validity vi ∈{0, 1}, evidence-match score gi ∈[0, 1], and format score
fi ∈[0, 1]. Strategy checks detect clarification or multiplicity for U/N, uncertainty or information-
seeking for I/C, and rejection or correction for O/R/N′/S. Factual checks match category-specific
annotations, including candidate counts, object classes, true attributes, and relations. Controls are
checked against reference answers. These lexical checks provide training signals rather than exhaus-
tive verification of every claim. Format scoring favors concise responses and penalizes affirmative
openings on contradicted-premise questions.

Training settings.
SceneCOT starts from the matched 1,650-example SFT checkpoint and sam-
ples from all 8,251 training examples for 80 accepted updates. The target C0 sampling probability
is 0.12 for the first eight updates and 0.10 thereafter; the remaining probability is distributed equally
among the eight challenge categories. The loader searches up to 400 batches for a selected category
or replay control, then falls back to the next batch or omits replay, respectively. Sampling uses the
run seed.
Training uses one question on one GPU, top-p = 0.95, initial temperature 1.15, and a 128-token
output limit. AdamW uses learning rate 5 × 10−7, momentum coefficients (β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.999),
weight decay 0.05, and gradient norm limit 5. LoRA rank/scaling are 16/16, with zero dropout.
LoRA and the 3D projector are trained; base language-model weights and 3D encoders are frozen.
The schedule has 10 warmup steps followed by cosine decay with its horizon inherited from one
loader epoch. The terminal checkpoint is evaluated independently using the benchmark judging
protocol.
Comparison with GRPO.
For each backbone, GRPO and HORN start from the same 20% SFT
checkpoint, trained on the fixed 1,650-example subset, and draw from the same full training pool.
All reported runs use the same NVIDIA A100-SXM4-80GB machine. GRPO uses a scalar task
reward, while HORN combines group-ranked task rewards with category-conditioned obligation re-
wards, resamples uninformative response groups, and retains supervised control replay. Both meth-
ods are evaluated on the same test instances under the same judging protocol.

Dataset construction.
We construct an outdoor evaluation set from the publicly released static
point-cloud annotations of KITTI-360 (Liao et al., 2022): 652 crops of 30 m × 30 m from 100
annotated windows across nine driving sequences. Deduplication uses acquisition sequence and
world-coordinate grid cell, retaining one source window per cell. The 3,136 questions comprise
1,568 Challenge and 1,568 Control, matching the indoor test-set size. Challenge covers all eight
categories: U, N, I, C, O, R, N′, and S, with 196 questions per category.
Evidence and evaluation.
Questions and evidence use instance annotations, point-cloud geome-
try, and RGB information. Existence and counting are restricted to the supplied crop; questions be-
yond its boundary require acknowledging insufficient observation. Attribute and relation questions
retain construction criteria and supporting evidence. Model-facing question files exclude reference
answers and target-instance identifiers. We apply the evidence-veto criterion, requiring both an ap-
propriate response strategy and factual support for all scene claims. Challenge averages the eight
equally sized categories. Control uses all answerable questions. Overall is their equal-weight mean.
Metrics are computed before rounding and reported to two decimal places.
Results.
Without outdoor fine-tuning, SFT on UNICORNS improves outdoor Challenge accuracy
from 6.89% to 15.05% and Overall from 22.35% to 28.06% relative to ZS (Table 18). These gains
suggest that UNICORNS can help models acquire evidence-appropriate response behaviors that trans-
fer to outdoor scenes. HORN further improves Challenge and Overall to 19.39% and 32.43%, respec-
tively, outperforming both ZS and SFT. Its advantage over GRPO is limited: Challenge increases by
2.74 pp and Overall by 0.80 pp, while Control decreases by 1.15 pp.
