## Abstract 

Reasoning Video Object Segmentation (RVOS) aims to lo-calize and segment objects referred to by natural-language expressions throughout a video. Although large vision-language models (LVLMs) implicitly encode localization cues in their attention maps, these attentions are often noisy, diffuse, and scattered across layers, heads, and modali-ties, limiting their direct use for segmentation. Existing methods typically rely on handcrafted attention-selection heuristics or computationally expensive attention refine-ment. We propose ATLAS, an attention adaptation frame-work that learns to transform raw LVLM attentions into object-centric localization cues. ATLAS treats attentions from all layers, heads, and modalities as structured fea-tures, combining high-resolution frame-level attentions that capture spatial information with low-resolution video-level attentions that encode temporal context. A lightweight U-Net equipped with temporal attention and FiLM-based modulation fuses these complementary representations to generate coarse segmentation heatmaps, which are propa-gated across the video using SAM2. Extensive experiments on challenging referring and reasoning video segmentation benchmarks show that ATLAS outperforms existing frozen-backbone methods. Code and models are available at 

https://hamidreza-136.github.io/atlas/ .

Nevertheless, recent studies have shown that the visual-token attentions of frozen LVLMs implicitly encode object localization information, enabling visual grounding [8, 13, 14, 19, 22]. However, effectively extracting these localiza-tion cues remains challenging. As illustrated in the top-right panel of Figure 1, averaging attentions across layers and heads produces diffuse, noisy activation maps poorly aligned with object boundaries. Existing training-free ap-proaches either manually select a small subset of attention heads believed to encode localization cues [14] or refine at-tention maps through repeated LVLM inference with con-trastive prompting [13], leaving much of the localization information distributed across layers and heads underuti-lized. Recent work has shown that a learned decoder over the full attention stack can recover this information for im-age grounding [36]. Reasoning video segmentation, how-ever, introduces a fundamentally different challenge: the model must jointly infer where the referred object is and when it becomes relevant over time. We therefore investi-gate how the full multimodal attention stack can be adapted to capture both spatial grounding and temporal relevance. To address these limitations, we propose ATLAS 

(Attention Transformation for Localization through 

Adaptive Segmentation), which leverages the complete visual attention representation from the middle-to-last layers across all attention heads as a stack of spatial feature maps, rather than treating attention maps as localization outputs to be manually selected or refined. As illustrated at the bottom of Figure 1, a lightweight attention adaptation network transforms these rich attention features into object-centric localization cues. The resulting adapted attention maps are then used to guide a frozen SAM2 [29] model, producing accurate and temporally consistent video object segmentation. Given a video-expression pair, ATLAS first samples a set of representative keyframes and extracts visual attention maps from a frozen LVLM. Specifically, it obtains high-resolution frame-level attentions that capture where the re-ferred object is located and low-resolution video-level atten-tions that encode when the object becomes relevant. The stacked attention features are processed by a lightweight encoder-decoder U-Net, while a temporal attention module aggregates complementary information across the sampled keyframes. The resulting temporal representation mod-ulates the U-Net bottleneck through Feature-wise Lin-ear Modulation (FiLM) , enabling the decoder to produce temporally-aware, object-centric localization maps. These adapted attention maps are subsequently used to prompt a frozen SAM2 model, yielding accurate and temporally con-sistent segmentation masks throughout the video. Overall, our contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose ATLAS , an attention adaptation framework for reasoning video segmentation that transforms com-plete LVLM attention representations into object-centric localization cues, eliminating the need for handcrafted at-tention selection or costly refinement. • We introduce a lightweight attention adaptation network that jointly exploits frame-level attentions and video-level attentions, integrating temporal cues into spatial localiza-tion to model both where the target object is located and 

when it becomes relevant. • We achieve state-of-the-art performance on reasoning video segmentation among methods with frozen back-bones, using a lightweight supervised adapter while keep-ing the LVLM and segmentation model fully frozen. 

## 2. Related Work 

Reasoning Video Object Segmentation. Reasoning video object segmentation (RVOS) extends referring video object segmentation [5, 20, 26, 35] by requiring semantic reason-ing over complex language expressions and visual-temporal context [4, 12, 30, 37, 38]. Early approaches primarily re-lied on multimodal fusion architectures that jointly encoded visual and linguistic representations to predict segmentation masks [5, 26, 31, 35]. More recent methods leverage large-scale pretrained LVLMs together with segmentation foun-dation models to improve semantic grounding and gener-alization [4, 17, 37, 38]. Despite this progress, RVOS re-mains challenging because accurate segmentation requires not only semantic understanding but also precise spatial lo-calization and reasoning over temporal dynamics. 

Large Vision-Language Models for Visual Ground-ing. Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs), such as LLaVA [23, 24], Qwen2-VL [34], and Qwen2.5-VL [3], have demonstrated strong capabilities in multimodal rea-soning and visual grounding. Recent studies have shown that LVLMs implicitly encode object localization cues in their internal representations, particularly in the attention maps of visual tokens. This observation has motivated a growing body of work that repurposes frozen LVLMs for visual grounding and segmentation by extracting atten-tion maps or other internal representations as localization cues [13, 14]. However, these representations are often coarse and poorly aligned with object boundaries, motivat-ing better approaches for exploiting LVLM attention. 

Attention-Based Localization from LVLMs. Recent stud-ies exploit LVLM attention maps as localization cues for visual grounding and segmentation [13, 14, 19, 22, 36]. TAM [19] utilizes attention rollout [1] to obtain object localization cues from LVLM attentions, whereas VL-SAM [22] enhances these cues using SAM [16]. 3.1. Method Overview 

Given a video V = {It}Tt=1 and a reasoning expression E,the goal is to predict a sequence of binary masks M =

{Mt}Tt=1 , where It ∈ RR×W ×3 is the RGB frame at time step t, with spatial resolution R × W (height × width), and 

Mt ∈ { 0, 1}R×W denotes the segmentation mask of the referred object. Our framework consists of three stages (de-picted in Figure 2). 

(1) Attention Extraction: Given a video-expression pair, a frozen LVLM extracts frame-level and video-level visual attention maps from a set of sparsely sampled keyframes. 

(2) Attention Adaptation: ATLAS transforms the com-plete stack of attention maps from the middle-to-last lay-ers across all attention heads into object-centric localization maps using a lightweight attention adaptation network. 

(3) Segmentation and Propagation: The adapted attention maps are converted into point prompts that guide a frozen SAM2 model to segment the sampled keyframes and prop-agate the masks to the remaining frames.

The third stage converts the predicted localization maps into temporally consistent object masks using a frozen SAM2 model. For each keyframe, a positive point prompt is se-lected as the maximum-response location of the predicted localization map,

Several keyframes may be seeded on the same object, so redun-dant seeds are removed before propagation. Spatial non-maximum suppression across keyframes, as used in De-cAF [13], is insufficient here: it compares masks at dif-ferent time steps, so if the object moves, its masks at two keyframes do not overlap and both seeds survive even though they refer to the same object. Instead, we test whether two seeds correspond to the same object by prop-agating one to the other’s keyframe. Starting from the seed at the earliest keyframe, we propagate its mask through the video; at every later keyframe k, we compare the propa-gated mask with the seed mask at k and discard the latter if their IoU exceeds a threshold τ (we use τ = 0 .8), since the object it identifies is already tracked. We then repeat the procedure from the earliest remaining seed until none are left. This yields M tracklets corresponding to distinct object hypotheses, and each duplicate removed also saves a full propagation pass. Each tracklet T m is downsampled to the attention reso-lution and sampled at the Ts keyframes, yielding a tracklet volume Vm ∈ [0 , 1] Ts×Rf ×Wf for m = 1 , . . . , M . ## 4. Experiments 

Datasets. We evaluate ATLAS on five referring video ob-ject segmentation benchmarks, including three conventional datasets: Ref-DAVIS [15], Ref-YouTube-VOS [31], and MeViS [10], together with two reasoning-oriented bench-marks: ReasonVOS [4] and ReVOS [37]. While the con-ventional benchmarks primarily evaluate referring object segmentation, ReasonVOS and ReVOS contain more chal-lenging expressions that require multi-step reasoning, com-positional understanding, and temporal reasoning. 

Evaluation Metrics. Following [13], we report the stan-dard video object segmentation metrics: region similar-ity ( J ), contour accuracy ( F), and their average ( J &F). Specifically, J measures the intersection-over-union (IoU) between the predicted and ground-truth masks, while F

evaluates boundary accuracy by comparing the predicted and ground-truth object contours. The combined J &F

score provides an overall measure of segmentation quality by jointly assessing region overlap and boundary precision. 

Models. We use publicly available pretrained LVLM checkpoints, including LLaVA-OV-7B [18], InternVL3-8B [39], Qwen2-VL-7B [34], and Qwen2.5-VL-7B [3]. For mask propagation, we adopt the pretrained SAM2-Hiera-Large [29] segmentation model. 

Implementation Details. The attention adaptation network is a lightweight U-Net with two encoder and two decoder stages, where each stage consists of two convolutional lay-ers. The bottleneck feature has dimension d = 512 and is conditioned via FiLM [28] using a two-layer temporal Transformer with four attention heads. For attention ex-traction, we use a maximum visual token budget of 1024 for the frame branch to obtain high-resolution spatial atten-tions and 64 for the video branch to capture complementary temporal cues. We train the network exclusively on the Ref-Youtube-VOS [31] training split, demonstrating strong gen-eralization to unseen reasoning video segmentation bench-marks. The network is optimized using AdamW with a batch size of 8, an initial learning rate of 1 × 10 −4 decayed linearly to 1 × 10 −5, and is trained for 20 epochs. Training requires approximately 6 GPU hours on a single NVIDIA RTX Pro 6000 GPU. Additional architectural details and training hyperparameters are provided in Appendix B.

4.2. Evaluation on Additional Datasets 

To further evaluate the generalization of ATLAS, we re-port results on the MeViS [10] and Ref-YouTube-VOS [31] benchmarks in Table 2. ATLAS consistently achieves com-petitive performance across both datasets. On MeViS, ATLAS with Qwen2.5VL-7B attains a J &F

score of 54.5, outperforming both DecAF (48.1) and the best fully fine-tuned method, Veason-R1 (52.2). Similar im-provements are observed with the other LVLM backbones, demonstrating that the learned attention adaptation general-izes well across different visual-language models. On Ref-YouTube-VOS, ATLAS achieves competitive performance. In particular, using Qwen2VL-7B, it obtains a J &F score of 71.0, substantially outperforming previ-ous frozen-backbone methods and approaching the perfor-mance of the best fully fine-tuned approaches. These re-sults further demonstrate that adapting intermediate LVLM attention representations provides an effective and compu-tationally efficient alternative to end-to-end fine-tuning.

## 6. Conclusion and Limitations 

We presented ATLAS, an attention adaptation framework for reasoning video object segmentation that transforms diffuse LVLM attentions across layers, heads, modalities, and time into object-centric localization maps that guide a frozen SAM2 model. Without fine-tuning either backbone, ATLAS outperforms frozen-backbone methods and remains competitive with fully fine-tuned approaches, showing that LVLM attentions contain rich localization cues that can be unlocked through learned adaptation. ATLAS, however, adapts attention rather than correcting LVLM reasoning, so severe reasoning failures may still limit localization. It also assumes access to intermediate visual attentions and uses uniform keyframe sampling. Future work could improve reasoning, support models with restricted attention access, and explore adaptive keyframe selection for long or dy-namic videos. 8References

## B. More Implementation Details 

The training hyperparameters of the proposed attention adapter are summarized in Table 9. We optimize the net-work using AdamW with an initial learning rate of 10 −4

that linearly decays to 10 −5 over 20 epochs. A weight de-cay of 10 −4 is applied for regularization, and training is per-formed with a batch size of eight video clips. The network is supervised using a combination of binary cross-entropy with logits and Soft Dice loss, which jointly encourage ac-curate pixel-wise classification and robust mask overlap. Table 10 summarizes the architecture of the proposed at-tention adaptation network. The network follows a U-Net-style encoder–decoder design operating on concatenated frame- and video-level LVLM attention features. Given 

C=392 channels from each modality, the input contains 

2C=784 channels over a 24 ×42 spatial grid. The first en-coder stage applies two 3×3 convolutions to produce 128 channels, followed by 2×2 max pooling. The second en-coder stage increases the feature dimension to 256 channels at half the spatial resolution. At the bottleneck, two additional 3×3 convolutions pro-duce a 512-channel representation. To capture temporal dependencies across keyframes, the bottleneck feature of

Its output is mapped to FiLM parameters γ and β, which modulate the 512-channel bottleneck features channel-wise. This con-ditions the spatial decoding of each frame on information from the full video sequence rather than processing frames independently.
